\section{Three notions that must not be confused}\label{sec:cn-terms}

``Good morning and welcome back'': today we finally add further structure to a manifold, first on a principal bundle, later inherited by the associated bundles (section 5.4 of the lecture): \emph{connections on a principal bundle} \ts{21}{1}{0:08} \ts{21}{1}{0:28}. On the principal bundle there is the right action of a Lie group $G$ (\cref{ch:l19}).

A warning \ts{21}{1}{0:49}. You have probably met ``connection'' in differential geometry or general relativity, where the word is used synonymously with \emph{covariant derivative} and with \emph{parallel transport}. Forget this. In decreasing order of generality \ts{21}{1}{1:10}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \textbf{connection} is a structure on a principal bundle;
  \item once there is a connection on the principal bundle, one gets a \textbf{parallel transport} on the principal bundle and hence on every associated bundle (\cref{ch:l23});
  \item in the very special case that the associated bundle is a vector bundle (the fibre carries a vector space structure), the connection defines a \textbf{covariant derivative} (\cref{ch:l25}).
\end{enumerate}
\ts{21}{1}{1:31} \ts{21}{1}{1:52} \ts{21}{1}{2:12} \ts{21}{1}{2:33} So the three are not synonymous at all, but all of them are still defined in terms of the connection on the principal bundle; it is important to say this because the terms are used interchangeably and this leads to confusion \ts{21}{1}{2:54}.
\begin{coursenote}[Gamma symbols]
For those who know covariant derivatives (otherwise forget this): in coordinates the covariant derivative of a vector bundle is given by $\Gamma$ symbols, which do not transform as a tensor but as ``connection components'' (\cref{ch:l22}). The beauty is that a connection on the principal bundle turns out to be something very simple, a \emph{1-form on the principal bundle}, nothing special. One cannot write a local form of it without choosing a section, and a global section of a principal bundle exists only if the bundle is trivial (\cref{thm:pb-trivial-section}); this leads to the funny transformation behaviour of the local components. \ts{21}{1}{3:15} \ts{21}{1}{3:35} \ts{21}{1}{3:56}
\end{coursenote}
Abstractly speaking the connection is a 1-form on the principal bundle. However we do not define it as such: we first define it differently (as a choice of subspaces) and only then see that this is equivalent to choosing a 1-form with particular properties \ts{21}{1}{4:16} \ts{21}{1}{4:38}.

\section{Vertical vector fields and the vertical subspace}\label{sec:cn-vertical}

Let $(P,\pi,M)$ be a principal $G$-bundle with right action $\ract$ \ts{21}{1}{5:00}. The right action induces vector fields on $P$ \ts{21}{1}{5:23}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the vector field $X^A$ generated by $A\in T_eG$]\label{def:cn-XA}
For every $A$ in the Lie algebra $T_eG$ of $G$ (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}) the vector field $X^A$ on $P$ is defined at each $p\in P$ by its action on functions $f\in C^\infty(P)$,
\[
  X^A_p\,f:=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,f\bigl(p\ract\exp(tA)\bigr)\Big|_{t=0},
\]
that is, $X^A_p$ is the tangent vector at $t=0$ of the curve $t\mapsto p\ract\exp(tA)$ through $p$. \ts{21}{1}{5:43} \ts{21}{1}{6:04} \ts{21}{1}{6:25} \ts{21}{1}{6:45} \ts{21}{1}{7:06}
\end{definition}
We know that a Lie algebra element induces a vector field on the \emph{group} by the exponential map (\cref{def:rc-exp}); here we want one on $P$. The right action needs a group element, and from the Lie algebra element one gets one by exponentiating, but one looks at the entire one-parameter subgroup, hence at a curve in $P$ which starts at $p$ and is pushed along by the action; then one takes the derivative with respect to the parameter at $t=0$, that is at $p$ \ts{21}{1}{6:04} \ts{21}{1}{6:25} \ts{21}{1}{6:45}. It is useful to give the assignment a name:
\[
  \mathcal I\colon T_eG\to\Gamma(TP),\qquad A\longmapsto X^A ,
\]
which assigns to $A$ the entire vector field $X^A$, a section of the tangent bundle of $P$ \ts{21}{1}{7:27} \ts{21}{1}{7:48}. This map $\mathcal I$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism: for $A,B\in T_eG$, with the bracket on $T_eG$ constructed in \cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}, one can bracket first and then map, $\mathcal I([\![A,B]\!])$, or map first and take the commutator of the resulting vector fields, $[X^A,X^B]$, and the two agree: $\mathcal I([\![A,B]\!])=[X^A,X^B]$ (the lecture says ``clearly''; no proof is given, and it is not used below) \ts{21}{1}{8:20} \ts{21}{1}{8:40} \ts{21}{1}{9:01}.

Now another definition, essential \ts{21}{1}{9:23}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the vertical subspace $V_pP$]\label{def:cn-vertical}
For $p\in P$ the \textbf{vertical subspace} of $T_pP$ is
\[
  V_pP:=\ker\bigl(\pi_{*,p}\bigr)=\{X\in T_pP\mid\pi_{*,p}(X)=0\},
\]
the kernel of the push-forward (\cref{def:tb-push}) of the bundle projection $\pi$ at $p$. It is a vector \emph{subspace} (not only a subset) of $T_pP$, since the kernel of a linear map is one. The name: these are the vectors pointing along the fibre, ``vertically'' in the usual picture. \ts{21}{1}{9:44} \ts{21}{1}{10:05} \ts{21}{1}{10:26}
\end{definition}
Pictorially (these pictures only work locally) \ts{21}{1}{10:49} \ts{21}{1}{11:13}: draw a piece of the base manifold with, at each point, the fibre $G$ attached. The projection $\pi$ takes a point down to its base point. What is $V_pP$ at a point $p$? It is the tangent space to the whole fibre through $p$. A vector tangent to a curve which is not contained in the fibre is pushed forward to the tangent vector of the projected curve (the push-forward of a tangent vector is the tangent vector of the image curve, \cref{prop:tb-push-curve}), and this is not zero in the base: such a vector is not in the kernel. A vector along the fibre is tangent to a curve that is projected to a constant curve, whose tangent vector is zero: so it lies in the vertical subspace \ts{21}{1}{11:34} \ts{21}{1}{11:55} \ts{21}{1}{12:18} \ts{21}{1}{12:38} \ts{21}{1}{12:59} \ts{21}{1}{13:20} \ts{21}{1}{13:42}. Note that $V_pP$ is defined canonically by the bundle structure alone: no choice has been made.
\begin{lemma}[$X^A$ is vertical]\label{lem:cn-vertical}
For every $A\in T_eG$ and every $p\in P$, $X^A_p\in V_pP$. \ts{21}{1}{14:03} \ts{21}{1}{14:24}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$X^A_p$ is the tangent vector of the curve $\gamma(t)=p\ract\exp(tA)$. The group only moves a point within its fibre, so $\gamma$ lies entirely in the fibre $\pi^{-1}(\pi(p))$. Then $\pi\circ\gamma$ is constant, and
\[
  \pi_{*,p}(X^A_p)=\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\pi\circ\gamma)\big|_0=0 .
\]
Hence $X^A_p\in V_pP$. \ts{21}{1}{14:45} \ts{21}{1}{15:07} \ts{21}{1}{15:28}
\end{proof}
What have we seen so far? We used nothing but the principal bundle structure, in particular the action of the Lie group, and we constructed, for every element of the Lie algebra, a vector field; these are \emph{vertical} vector fields, given by the very structure of the principal bundle \ts{21}{1}{15:49} \ts{21}{1}{16:09}. This is something we are going to use, but it is not what we actually want.
\begin{aside}[$\mathcal I$ is an isomorphism onto $V_pP$]
Said in class later, at the definition of the connection 1-form (\cref{sec:cn-form}): the image of $A\mapsto X^A_p$ is only $V_pP$, and it is invertible, ``because the Lie algebra maps diffeomorphically into a small region of the Lie group and the vertical fibre is the Lie group''; so $A\mapsto X^A_p$ is a vector space isomorphism $T_eG\to V_pP$ \ts{21}{1}{38:39} \ts{21}{1}{39:00}. Not said in class, a direct reason: by \cref{prop:pb-orbit-G} the map $G\to\pi^{-1}(\pi(p))$, $g\mapsto p\ract g$, is a diffeomorphism (the action is free and the fibre is an orbit), and its push-forward at $e$ is exactly $A\mapsto X^A_p$, an isomorphism of $T_eG$ onto $T_p(\text{fibre})=V_pP$.
\end{aside}

\section{A connection: a choice of horizontal subspaces}\label{sec:cn-def}

\textbf{The idea.} The idea of a connection is to make a \emph{choice}, and whenever one makes a choice one introduces structure; the structure will be called the connection. The choice is how to \emph{connect} the points of neighbouring fibres of a principal bundle: that is where the name comes from \ts{21}{1}{16:30} \ts{21}{1}{16:51} \ts{21}{1}{17:15}. Picture again the local principal bundle with two very nearby fibres (think of ``nearby'' topologically). We would like to say that this point of the first fibre is to be identified with that point of the second: in walking from here to there, which point in the other fibre do I call ``the same'' as this one? On the tangent space of a manifold there is no such notion either: one has a direction, a tangent vector, and the idea that after walking over there it is ``still the same direction'', but it is a tangent vector in a very different place \ts{21}{1}{17:36} \ts{21}{1}{17:56} \ts{21}{1}{18:18} \ts{21}{1}{18:38} \ts{21}{1}{19:00}. Obviously one could have chosen differently (identify with another point), so there is a choice.

What does ``nearby'' mean? A \emph{linearisation}: at the point $p$ we look at the vertical subspace $V_pP$ and \emph{choose another subspace} $H_pP\subseteq T_pP$ such that $V_pP\oplus H_pP$ is the entire tangent space. Choosing a complementary subspace: of course a different one could be chosen; the only ones excluded are those which intersect $V_pP$ non-trivially, which are not complementary \ts{21}{1}{19:21} \ts{21}{1}{19:42} \ts{21}{1}{20:02}. It is an abuse to say that the arrow ``points to'' a point of the neighbouring fibre, as little as a tangent vector on the surface of a sphere points to another point of the sphere: it lies in the tangent space. But it is the linearisation of the idea: the closer one goes, the smaller the mistake of identifying (as one does with the exponential map). Later, integrating along a curve which is tangent everywhere to the chosen subspaces, one can really identify points of different fibres: this is the idea of parallel transport (\cref{ch:l23}) \ts{21}{1}{20:23} \ts{21}{1}{20:44} \ts{21}{1}{21:04} \ts{21}{1}{21:25}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: connection on a principal bundle]\label{def:cn-connection}
A \textbf{connection} on a principal $G$-bundle $(P,\pi,M)$ is an assignment which to every $p\in P$ assigns a vector subspace $H_pP$ of $T_pP$ (the \textbf{horizontal subspace}) such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(A)] $T_pP=H_pP\oplus V_pP$ for every $p$;
  \item[(B)] the choice is compatible with the right action: for every $g\in G$ the push-forward of the right translation $r_g\colon P\to P$, $r_g(p):=p\ract g$, maps the horizontal subspace at $p$ onto the one at $p\ract g$,
  \[
    (r_g)_{*,p}\bigl(H_pP\bigr)=H_{p\ract g}P ;
  \]
  \item[(C)] the assignment is smooth, in the following sense: by (A) every $X\in T_pP$ decomposes uniquely as $X=\mathrm{hor}\,X+\mathrm{ver}\,X$, with $\mathrm{hor}\,X\in H_pP$ and $\mathrm{ver}\,X\in V_pP$; if $X$ is a smooth vector field on $P$, then $\mathrm{hor}\,X$ and $\mathrm{ver}\,X$ (defined pointwise) are smooth vector fields.
\end{enumerate}
\ts{21}{1}{21:45} \ts{21}{1}{22:07} \ts{21}{1}{22:38} \ts{21}{1}{23:01} \ts{21}{1}{23:22} \ts{21}{1}{23:43}
\end{definition}
\noindent(The technical word for such an assignment of subspaces is a \emph{distribution}, but it is the distribution of differential geometry, not of functional analysis; the lecture absorbs this into the definition \ts{21}{1}{22:07}.)

Comments on the three conditions. (A) is the formalisation of the complementary subspace. Condition (B): this has to be done at every point, also at $q=p\ract g$ in the same fibre, and we want the choices to be compatible with the group action: a vector of $H_pP$ is related to a vector at $q$ by the push-forward of the right action, and the requirement is that this push-forward maps the \emph{entire} horizontal space at $p$ onto the entire horizontal space at $p\ract g$. ``A very reasonable compatibility condition, without which nothing would work'' \ts{21}{1}{23:43} \ts{21}{1}{24:03} \ts{21}{1}{24:24} \ts{21}{1}{24:45} \ts{21}{1}{25:06} \ts{21}{1}{25:27} \ts{21}{1}{25:47} \ts{21}{1}{26:08} \ts{21}{1}{26:30}. Condition (C): the choice must not jump, neither within a fibre nor from fibre to fibre. Normally one would say ``smooth distribution''; here it is expressed by the smoothness of the two parts of a smooth vector field, which takes care that the assignment is not merely continuous \ts{21}{1}{26:30} \ts{21}{1}{26:54} \ts{21}{1}{27:15} \ts{21}{1}{27:38} \ts{21}{1}{27:59} \ts{21}{1}{28:21} \ts{21}{1}{28:43} \ts{21}{1}{29:06}. This just formalises the intuition: ``nothing more, nothing less''.

\textbf{A caveat} \ts{21}{1}{29:34}. The decomposition into vertical and horizontal part is \emph{not} canonical: both the horizontal part of a vector and its vertical part depend on the choice of $H_pP$. By terminology one might be misled to think that the vertical part, since $V_pP$ is canonical, is independent of the choice and only the horizontal part depends on it, or the other way around; but both depend on it \ts{21}{1}{29:54} \ts{21}{1}{30:15}.

\section{The connection 1-form}\label{sec:cn-form}

The definition of a connection as a complementary subspace is geometrically very intuitive, but technically tricky: how does one write down such an assignment of subspaces at every point? We can talk about it, it is well defined, but it is difficult to write down. Technically the choice of horizontal subspaces is conveniently encoded in a \emph{Lie-algebra valued 1-form} \ts{21}{1}{32:42} \ts{21}{1}{33:04} \ts{21}{1}{35:06} \ts{21}{1}{35:29} \ts{21}{1}{35:51}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the connection 1-form $\omega$]\label{def:cn-omega}
Given a connection (a choice $H_pP$ as in \cref{def:cn-connection}), the \textbf{connection 1-form} with respect to it is the Lie-algebra valued 1-form $\omega$ on $P$, defined at each $p\in P$ as the linear map
\[
  \omega_p\colon T_pP\to T_eG,\qquad\omega_p(X):=\mathcal I_p^{-1}\bigl(\mathrm{ver}\,X\bigr),
\]
where $\mathcal I_p\colon T_eG\to V_pP$, $A\mapsto X^A_p$, is the isomorphism of \cref{def:cn-XA} and the aside above. \ts{21}{1}{36:12} \ts{21}{1}{36:33} \ts{21}{1}{36:54} \ts{21}{1}{37:15} \ts{21}{1}{37:36} \ts{21}{1}{37:57} \ts{21}{1}{38:17}
\end{definition}
So one takes a tangent vector, keeps its vertical part (and this does depend on the choice of horizontal subspace, as in the caveat: ``the vertical part is already information about the horizontal subspace''), and takes the inverse of $\mathcal I$, which sends it to a Lie algebra element $A\in T_eG$; this is exactly what is wanted. The choice of horizontal subspaces, hidden in $\mathrm{ver}$, has been turned by the canonical map $\mathcal I$ into a 1-form \ts{21}{1}{38:39} \ts{21}{1}{39:00} \ts{21}{1}{39:21} \ts{21}{1}{39:42}. It is a 1-form on the \emph{entire principal bundle}, not a 1-form on the base manifold, and it is called the connection 1-form with respect to the given connection \ts{21}{1}{40:24} \ts{21}{1}{40:45} \ts{21}{1}{41:05}.
\begin{answer}{$\mathcal I$ maps to a whole vector field, not to a vector?}[$\mathcal I$ at a point]
A student observes that $\mathcal I$ was defined as a map to an entire vector field. Yes: it takes an element of the Lie algebra to a vector field, and one looks at the field at the point $p$, which gives the map $\mathcal I_p$ used in the definition \ts{21}{1}{40:04}
\end{answer}
\textbf{The horizontal subspaces are recovered.} Given the horizontal subspaces we can construct the form; conversely \ts{21}{1}{41:05} \ts{21}{1}{41:31}
\[
  H_pP=\ker\omega_p=\{X\in T_pP\mid\omega_p(X)=0\}
\]
(indeed $\omega_p(X)=0$ iff $\mathrm{ver}X=0$ iff $X\in H_pP$). From a choice of subspaces we define the form, and from the form we recover the subspaces as its kernel \ts{21}{1}{41:52} \ts{21}{1}{42:12} \ts{21}{1}{42:33}. Of course this holds only because the form is defined in precisely this way: not every $\mathfrak g$-valued 1-form on $P$ gives, by taking the kernel, a valid $H_pP$. What we now want is a complete replacement: to find the properties of a 1-form defined like this, and then to \emph{elevate these properties to a definition} of a connection 1-form, without giving a connection first; then $H_pP$ is recovered as the kernel and automatically has the properties (A)--(C). At the end one can think alternatively of a choice of $H_pP$ with properties, or of a 1-form on the principal bundle with properties \ts{21}{1}{42:54} \ts{21}{1}{43:14} \ts{21}{1}{43:35}.

\begin{theorem}[Properties of the connection 1-form]\label{thm:cn-omega}
The connection 1-form $\omega$ with respect to a connection has the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] $\omega_p\bigl(X^A_p\bigr)=A$ for all $p\in P$ and $A\in T_eG$, that is $\omega\circ\mathcal I=\mathrm{id}_{T_eG}$;
  \item[(b)] for all $g\in G$, $p\in P$ and $X\in T_pP$,
  \[
    \bigl(r_g^{*}\omega\bigr)_p(X)=(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*\bigl(\omega_p(X)\bigr);
  \]
  \item[(c)] $\omega$ is a smooth 1-form.
\end{enumerate}
\ts{21}{1}{43:55} \ts{21}{1}{44:21} \ts{21}{1}{44:41} \ts{21}{1}{45:02} \ts{21}{1}{45:22} \ts{21}{1}{45:43} \ts{21}{1}{46:03} \ts{21}{1}{46:24} \ts{21}{1}{46:44} \ts{21}{1}{47:05} \ts{21}{1}{47:26} \ts{21}{1}{47:47} \ts{21}{1}{48:08} \ts{21}{1}{48:28} \ts{21}{1}{48:49} \ts{21}{1}{49:10}
\end{theorem}
Explanations. (a): before, $\mathcal I$ took a Lie algebra element into the vertical subspace; $\omega$ acts on the entire tangent space, but its restriction to the vertical subspace is the inverse of $\mathcal I$, because $\omega$ was defined as the inverse of the vertical part. (b): this reflects (B) on the form. The group acts on $P$ by $r_g$, and a form can only be acted upon by pull-back (\cref{def:tb-pull}), not by push-forward. At first the formula looks complicated: $\omega_p(X)$ lies in $T_eG$, and $(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*$ is the push-forward at $e$ of the adjoint map $\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(h)=g^{-1}\bullet h\bullet g$ (\cref{def:rp-Ad}), which maps $T_eG$ to $T_{\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}(e)}G=T_eG$: so the right-hand side is again a Lie algebra element, as it must be, since a 1-form applied to a vector gives a Lie algebra element \ts{21}{1}{47:05} \ts{21}{1}{47:26} \ts{21}{1}{47:47} \ts{21}{1}{48:08} \ts{21}{1}{48:28} \ts{21}{1}{48:49}. Why it has this form we see in the proof. (c): $\omega$ is smooth; ABC correspond ``more or less'' to the (A)(B)(C) of the definition of a connection: roughly speaking (a) is the analogue of (A), (b) of (B) and (c) of (C) \ts{21}{1}{49:10} \ts{21}{1}{49:32}.
\begin{coursenote}[On the formula in (b)]
Not said in class: the formula compares the pull-back at $p$ with $\omega_p$ at the \emph{same} point $p$ (not at $p\ract g$); the pull-back is what carries the information from $p\ract g$ back to $p$. The lecture first says ``it looks a little funny'' and only explains it afterwards, in the proof. \ts{21}{1}{47:05}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{proof}
(a) By the definition of $\omega$, since $X^A_p$ is already vertical (\cref{lem:cn-vertical}): $\omega_p(X^A_p)=\mathcal I_p^{-1}(X^A_p)=A$. \ts{21}{1}{49:54} \ts{21}{1}{50:14} \ts{21}{1}{50:35} \ts{21}{1}{50:55}

(b) The left-hand side is linear in $X$ (pull-back of a 1-form is a 1-form). So decompose $X=\mathrm{ver}X+\mathrm{hor}X$ and check the two cases \ts{21}{1}{51:16} \ts{21}{1}{51:37} \ts{21}{1}{51:57}.

(b1) $X\in V_pP$: then $X=X^A_p$ for some $A$. By the definition of the pull-back
\[
  (r_g^*\omega)_p(X^A_p)=\omega_{p\ract g}\bigl((r_g)_*X^A_p\bigr).
\]
By the \emph{lemma} below, $(r_g)_*X^A_p=X^{A'}_{p\ract g}$ with $A'=(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*A$; so by (a)
\[
  (r_g^*\omega)_p(X^A_p)=A'=(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*\,\omega_p(X^A_p).
\]
\ts{21}{1}{52:19} \ts{21}{1}{52:41} \ts{21}{1}{53:02} \ts{21}{1}{53:23} \ts{21}{1}{53:43} \ts{21}{1}{54:05} \ts{21}{1}{54:25} \ts{21}{1}{54:52} \ts{21}{1}{55:13} \ts{21}{1}{55:34} \ts{21}{1}{55:54} \ts{21}{1}{56:15} \ts{21}{1}{56:35}

(b2) $X\in H_pP$: by (B), $(r_g)_*X\in H_{p\ract g}P=\ker\omega_{p\ract g}$, hence

The step marked \emph{lemma} was moved by the lecturer to the problem sheet: it is a ``technical, but not uninteresting'' step, namely that if one pushes forward the vector field generated by $A$, one gets the field generated by another Lie algebra element, namely $(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*A$; the lecturer hesitates for a moment about it (``on a push forward\ldots yes, I think that is right, so that is on the problem sheet'') \ts{21}{1}{53:43} \ts{21}{1}{54:05} \ts{21}{1}{54:25} \ts{21}{1}{54:52} \ts{21}{1}{55:13}. Here it is, for completeness.
\begin{lemma}[Right translations and the generated fields]\label{lem:cn-ad}
$(r_g)_*X^A_p=X^{(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*A}_{p\ract g}$ for all $p\in P$, $g\in G$, $A\in T_eG$. \ts{21}{1}{54:05}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(Not said in class; the lecture leaves it to the problem sheet.)} Apply $r_g$ to the defining curve:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  r_g\bigl(p\ract\exp(tA)\bigr)&=p\ract\bigl(\exp(tA)\bullet g\bigr)\\
  &=(p\ract g)\ract\bigl(g^{-1}\exp(tA)\,g\bigr)\\
  &=(p\ract g)\ract\exp\bigl(t(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*A\bigr),
  \end{aligned}
\]
the last step by \cref{thm:rc-hom} for the homomorphism $\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\colon G\to G$ ($h\mapsto g^{-1}hg$). Differentiate at $t=0$: the left side gives $(r_g)_*X^A_p$, the right side $X^{(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*A}_{p\ract g}$.
\end{proof}

\end{coursenote}

\textbf{The converse} \ts{21}{1}{1:01:37}. This may scare one at first sight: we had the intuitive definition by horizontal subspaces, and of course it is much more convenient to say ``you hand me a principal bundle and now a 1-form with these properties''. It would be nice to show the converse, namely that any 1-form with these properties induces horizontal subspaces $H_pP$ satisfying the conditions of a connection, and that is the case. The proof is very similar to the one above, and it goes to the next problem sheet ``without much help'' \ts{21}{1}{1:01:57} \ts{21}{1}{1:02:18} \ts{21}{1}{1:02:42}.
\begin{theorem}[Connection 1-forms define connections]\label{thm:cn-converse}
Let $\omega$ be a $T_eG$-valued 1-form on a principal $G$-bundle $P$ satisfying (a), (b), (c) of \cref{thm:cn-omega}. Then $H_pP:=\ker\omega_p$ defines a connection, and $\omega$ is the connection 1-form with respect to it. This is the unique connection with connection 1-form $\omega$. \ts{21}{1}{1:02:42} \ts{21}{1}{1:03:03}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(Left to the problem sheet in the lecture; the argument is added, not said in class.)} (A): by (a), $\omega_p|_{V_pP}=\mathcal I_p^{-1}$ is injective, so $\ker\omega_p\cap V_pP=0$; and every $X\in T_pP$ equals $\bigl(X-X^{\omega_p(X)}_p\bigr)+X^{\omega_p(X)}_p$, where the first summand is in the kernel by (a). (B): for $X\in\ker\omega_p$, (b) gives $\omega_{p\ract g}\bigl((r_g)_*X\bigr)=(\mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}})_*\omega_p(X)=0$, so $(r_g)_*H_pP\subseteq H_{p\ract g}P$, with equality because $r_g$ is a diffeomorphism and the dimensions agree. (C): $\mathrm{ver}X=X^{\omega(X)}$ is smooth by (c). Finally $\mathrm{ver}X=X^{\omega(X)}$ shows that the connection 1-form of $H$ is $\omega$; and the 1-form determines the subspaces as its kernel, so the connection is unique.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Smoothness of $X^A$]
Not said in class: $p\mapsto X^{A}_p$ is smooth, and so is $p\mapsto X^{\omega_p(X)}_p$ for smooth $\omega,X$, because the action $(p,g)\mapsto p\ract g$ and $\exp$ are smooth; this is used in (C) above and in (c) of \cref{thm:cn-omega} (the lecture only says ``smooth after smooth is smooth'').
\end{coursenote}
The philosophy \ts{21}{1}{1:02:42}: I hand you a principal bundle, and \emph{every 1-form on the principal bundle with these properties defines a unique connection}. It is much more convenient to give such a 1-form, and nothing else: the connection can then \emph{be defined} to be a 1-form with these properties.

\textbf{Outlook} \ts{21}{1}{1:03:03} \ts{21}{1}{1:03:23}. Next time we will see how a connection gives rise to parallel transport on the principal bundle, and then to parallel transport on any associated fibre bundle. If one goes to a vector bundle, the fibre has a linear structure: one can transport a point, take the difference with another point and write down a differential quotient. For a differential quotient one needs a minus, the additive inverse of a plus: the fibre must be a vector space, and so the associated bundle must in particular be a vector bundle. But on associated bundles which are not vector bundles one can still have parallel transport: it is absolutely \emph{not} true that one needs a covariant derivative to define parallel transport, let alone a connection \ts{21}{1}{1:03:43} \ts{21}{1}{1:04:04}. For applications in physics, in differential geometry one looks at the frame bundle as the principal bundle and establishes the connection on it, which then passes by parallel transport to all associated bundles, in particular the vector bundles, the tensor bundles, the spinor bundles; and the same holds for non-abelian gauge theory \ts{21}{1}{1:04:25} \ts{21}{1}{1:04:45}.
