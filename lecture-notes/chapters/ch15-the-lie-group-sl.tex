\section{The set and the group $SL(2,\C)$}\label{sec:sl-group}

The lecture is about half-way through the course, and the plan is to present \emph{one central physical example} that uses about everything done so far: ``a very simple example, but if you carry it through properly you will meet almost everything'' \ts{15}{1}{0:00} \ts{15}{1}{0:22}. Formally it is still part of the section on Lie groups and Lie algebras (numbered 4.4 in the course); the example is the \emph{relativistic spin group} $SL(2,\C)$. To use it in physics one also needs its representations, which have not been studied yet (\cref{ch:l17}); but to understand it as a Lie group and derive its Lie algebra one only needs what is available \ts{15}{1}{0:42} \ts{15}{1}{1:04} \ts{15}{1}{1:25} \ts{15}{1}{1:46}.
\begin{aside}[Why ``relativistic spin group''?]
Not said in class: $SL(2,\C)$ is the (double) cover of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group, and contains $SU(2)$, the spin group of the rotations, as a subgroup; this is the reason for the name. The lecture only announces the name and defers the physics to the representations \ts{15}{1}{1:04}.
\end{aside}

Before it is a Lie group, it is a \emph{set} \ts{15}{1}{1:46}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the set $SL(2,\C)$]\label{def:sl-set}
$SL(2,\C)$ is the set of all quadruples $(a,b,c,d)\in\C^4=\C\times\C\times\C\times\C$ subject to the condition
\[
  ad-bc=1 .
\]
Instead of $(a,b,c,d)$ one writes, following tradition, the square scheme $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$; it is a 4-tuple, since one always knows which is the first, second, third and fourth entry. So $SL(2,\C)$ is defined by a restricted comprehension, as a \emph{subset} of $\C^4$ (``$S$'' for special: determinant one; ``$L$'' for linear: the operation below is linear-algebraic; the lecture does not spell out the name) \ts{15}{1}{2:07} \ts{15}{1}{2:27} \ts{15}{1}{2:47} \ts{15}{1}{3:09} \ts{15}{1}{3:29}.
\end{definition}
There is nothing more to say about the set. Now it is made into a group.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the group operation of $SL(2,\C)$]\label{def:sl-op}
The \textbf{group operation} $\bullet\colon SL(2,\C)\times SL(2,\C)\to SL(2,\C)$ (``blob'' in the lecture) is defined by
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\bullet\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\g&h\end{pmatrix}
  :=\begin{pmatrix}ae+bg&af+bh\\ce+dg&cf+dh\end{pmatrix}.
\]
It is easy to remember because everybody has been trained to do \emph{matrix multiplication}; but one should not think of matrices: it is just a rule of how to combine two 4-tuples into a new 4-tuple \ts{15}{1}{3:49} \ts{15}{1}{4:11} \ts{15}{1}{4:32} \ts{15}{1}{4:53} \ts{15}{1}{5:14}.
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}[$SL(2,\C)$ is a group]\label{prop:sl-group}
$(SL(2,\C),\bullet)$ is a non-abelian group, with neutral element $\mathbb 1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and inverse
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Closure}: one has to check that the result of $\bullet$ again satisfies $ad-bc=1$. One can multiply out by hand; equivalently, the determinant of a matrix product is the product of the determinants, so it stays $1\cdot1=1$ \ts{15}{1}{5:34} \ts{15}{1}{5:55}.\par
\emph{Associativity} is a boring check (it is associativity of matrix multiplication) \ts{15}{1}{6:17}.\par
\emph{Neutral element}: $\mathbb 1\bullet g=g\bullet\mathbb 1=g$ for all $g$, and $\mathbb 1$ is in the set since $1\cdot1-0\cdot0=1$ \ts{15}{1}{6:37}.\par
\emph{Inverse}: for $g=(a,b,c,d)$ the lecture claims the displayed inverse (``look it up if you do not believe it''): transpose, take the minors with the chessboard pattern of signs and divide by the determinant, here $1$. It lies again in $SL(2,\C)$ because $da-(-b)(-c)=ad-bc=1$ \ts{15}{1}{6:58} \ts{15}{1}{7:19} \ts{15}{1}{7:40} \ts{15}{1}{8:01} \ts{15}{1}{8:21}.\par
\emph{Non-abelian}: the group is non-commutative (e.g.\ with $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ one gets different products in the two orders; the lecture only says ``non-commutative'') \ts{15}{1}{8:42}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[The commutation example]
The two matrices are not in the lecture: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\bullet\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, while the other order gives $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Not said in class. \ts{15}{1}{8:42}
\end{coursenote}

\section{The topology}\label{sec:sl-top}

Next the set underlying the group is considered as a \emph{topological space}: first a topology is needed, and, as always, one avoids constructing it explicitly and \emph{inherits} one from a space one knows: $\C^4$ \ts{15}{1}{9:02} \ts{15}{1}{9:23}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[1.] \textbf{On $\C$}: the techniques for $\R$ extend to $\C$. The \textbf{open ball} of radius $r\in\R^+$ around $z\in\C$ is
  \[
    B_r(z):=\{\,y\in\C\mid |y-z|<r\,\}
  \]
  with $|\cdot|$ the absolute value of complex numbers; in the complex plane it is the disc around $z$ without its boundary. By the standard construction a subset $U\subseteq\C$ is called \emph{open} if for every point $z\in U$ there is an $r>0$ with $B_r(z)\subseteq U$, exactly as for $\R$ and $\R^2$. This is a topology $\mathcal O_{\C}$ on $\C$ \ts{15}{1}{9:44} \ts{15}{1}{10:06} \ts{15}{1}{10:26} \ts{15}{1}{10:47} \ts{15}{1}{11:08} \ts{15}{1}{11:28};
  \item[2.] \textbf{On $\C^4$}: the product topology of the topology of $\C$ \ts{15}{1}{11:49};
  \item[3.] \textbf{On $SL(2,\C)\subsetneq\C^4$}: the induced (subset) topology, whose open sets are the $U\cap SL(2,\C)$ with $U$ open in $\C^4$. It is a proper subset because the condition removes one complex degree of freedom \ts{15}{1}{12:10} \ts{15}{1}{12:31} \ts{15}{1}{12:54}.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{recall}[Product and induced topology]
Product topology: \cref{def:to-product}; induced (subset) topology: \cref{def:to-induced}. Here they are applied to $\C^4$ and to $SL(2,\C)\subset\C^4$.
\end{recall}
So $SL(2,\C)$ is a topological space: ``not a single technique we introduced was in vain'' \ts{15}{1}{13:17}.

\section{A complex three-dimensional topological manifold}\label{sec:sl-manifold}

Is it one of the special topological spaces called manifolds \ts{15}{1}{13:39}? By \cref{def:mf-manifold}: around every point there must be an open set that is mapped homeomorphically to some $\C^{n}$ (the lecture goes to the complex numbers, so $\C^n$ instead of $\R^n$). One might think $n=4$, but the condition $ad-bc=1$ kills one degree of freedom, and on the right-hand side the $1+0\cdot i$ kills a complex one: the feeling is that the manifold is complex three-dimensional; but one does not need feelings, one constructs charts that cover it \ts{15}{1}{13:59} \ts{15}{1}{14:20} \ts{15}{1}{14:40} \ts{15}{1}{15:02}. The idea: use the condition to express one entry through the other three, for instance (if $a\ne0$) $d=\frac{1+bc}{a}$ \ts{15}{1}{15:25} \ts{15}{1}{15:46}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the charts $(U,x)$, $(V,y)$, $(W,z)$ of $SL(2,\C)$]\label{def:sl-charts}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(U)] $U:=\{(a,b,c,d)\in SL(2,\C)\mid a\ne0\}$ and $x\colon U\to\C^*\times\C\times\C$, $\ x(a,b,c,d):=(a,b,c)$, where $\C^*=\C\setminus\{0\}$. The inverse is
  \[
    x^{-1}(a,b,c)=\Bigl(a,\,b,\,c,\,\tfrac{1+bc}{a}\Bigr).
  \]
  \item[(V)] $V:=\{d\ne0\}$ and $y\colon V\to\C\times\C\times\C^*$, $\ y(a,b,c,d):=(b,c,d)$, with $y^{-1}(b,c,d)=\bigl(\tfrac{1+bc}{d},b,c,d\bigr)$.
  \item[(W)] $W:=\{b\ne0\}$ and $z\colon W\to\C\times\C^*\times\C$, $\ z(a,b,c,d):=(a,b,d)$, with $z^{-1}(a,b,d)=\bigl(a,b,\tfrac{ad-1}{b},d\bigr)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
Why this works \ts{15}{1}{16:07} \ts{15}{1}{16:27} \ts{15}{1}{16:48} \ts{15}{1}{17:10} \ts{15}{1}{17:31} \ts{15}{1}{17:52} \ts{15}{1}{18:13}: a chart is a chart domain together with a chart map. The chart domain $U$ is a subset of the topological space; the restriction $a\ne0$ removes some points ($SL(2,\C)$-elements with a zero in that place), so $U$ does not cover the whole group, but it is necessary to be able to \emph{divide by $a$} when reconstructing $d$. The chart map $x$ looks only at the three coordinates $(a,b,c)$; the fourth, $d$, is not needed since it is reconstructed from the other three by the condition.
\begin{proposition}[$(U,x)$ is a chart]\label{prop:sl-chart}
$U$ is open in $SL(2,\C)$ and $x$ is a homeomorphism from $U$ onto the open set $x(U)=\C^*\times\C\times\C\subset\C^3$. The same holds for $(V,y)$ and $(W,z)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{$U$ is open}: it is the preimage of the open set $\C^*\subset\C$ under the continuous map $(a,b,c,d)\mapsto a$, equivalently its complement $\{a=0\}$ is closed \ts{15}{1}{20:56} \ts{15}{1}{21:21}.\par
\emph{$x$ is continuous}: the preimages of open sets are open; $x$ is a restriction of a coordinate projection of $\C^4$, which is continuous for the product topology and stays continuous on the subset \ts{15}{1}{18:33} \ts{15}{1}{18:56} \ts{15}{1}{19:16}.\par
\emph{$x$ is invertible with continuous inverse}: one restricts the target to $\C^*\times\C\times\C$, because only there can one reconstruct the fourth entry; the inverse is $(a,b,c)\mapsto(a,b,c,\frac{1+bc}{a})$, continuous because $a\ne0$ on this domain (a quotient of continuous functions with non-vanishing denominator). The inverse lands in $SL(2,\C)$ since $a\cdot\frac{1+bc}{a}-bc=1$ \ts{15}{1}{19:37} \ts{15}{1}{19:58} \ts{15}{1}{20:19} \ts{15}{1}{20:40} \ts{15}{1}{22:22} \ts{15}{1}{22:45}.\par
So $x$ is a homeomorphism and $(U,x)$ is a chart. The same arguments hold for $V$ (dividing by $d$) and for $W$ (dividing by $b$ and using $c=\frac{ad-1}{b}$) \ts{15}{1}{23:05} \ts{15}{1}{23:26} \ts{15}{1}{23:49} \ts{15}{1}{24:10} \ts{15}{1}{24:30} \ts{15}{1}{24:52} \ts{15}{1}{25:14} \ts{15}{1}{25:36}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Slips: closedness, ``divide by $d$'', and ``lower-dimensional sets are closed'']
The lecture asks ``open in what topology?'' and answers that $\{a=0\}$ is a ``one-dimensional subset'' and that ``every lower-dimensional subset is closed in the standard topology''. Both statements are loose: $\{a=0\}$ has complex dimension two in the three-dimensional manifold, and lower-dimensional subsets are not closed in general (a punctured line is not). The correct argument is the one above: $\{a=0\}=\mathrm{pr}_a^{-1}(\{0\})$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{0\}$ under a continuous map. For the chart $W$ the lecture says at one point ``divide by $d$'' (``the $c$ can be reconstructed as ...'') while the entry that has to be non-zero is $b$: $c=\frac{ad-1}{b}$. Not said in class. \ts{15}{1}{21:00} \ts{15}{1}{21:41} \ts{15}{1}{27:02} \ts{15}{1}{27:24}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{answer}{Open in what topology? Is $\{a\neq 0\}$ really open?}[Openness of the chart domain]
The set $U=\{a\ne0\}$ is open in the topology of $SL(2,\C)$, i.e.\ in the induced topology: take $\{a\ne0\}\subset\C^4$, open in the product topology, and intersect with $SL(2,\C)$. The lecturer guarantees it is open and remarks that one ``has to go through the motions'' of proving it, which would take three days if done for every set; here it is a standard technique that is applied; the whole point of working through the example is to see \emph{what has to be done} and where one can say ``this can be done now'' \ts{15}{1}{21:00} \ts{15}{1}{21:21} \ts{15}{1}{21:41} \ts{15}{1}{22:02}.
\end{answer}
\textbf{Covering.} The three charts have chart domains $\{a\ne0\}$, $\{d\ne0\}$, $\{b\ne0\}$. The only point of $\C^4$ they could miss is where all of them vanish, which does not satisfy $ad-bc=1$ \ts{15}{1}{26:41} \ts{15}{1}{27:45} \ts{15}{1}{28:05} \ts{15}{1}{28:26}. A student then remarks that the second chart is not needed: \emph{two} charts suffice, since if $a=0$ the condition gives $-bc=1$, so $b\ne0$; one says: $U\cup W=SL(2,\C)$ \ts{15}{1}{28:48} \ts{15}{1}{29:09}. There is no harm in having more charts, as the atlas can always be extended to a maximal atlas; but the two charts $U$ and $W$ are enough \ts{15}{1}{29:31}.
\begin{coursenote}[Which two charts cover? $U$ and $V$ do not]
The student's remark is about $U$ and $W$ (the lecturer repeats ``these two''). It would be false for $U$ and $V$: the element $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $a=d=0$ and lies in neither. Also the formulation ``the only point not covered is where all four entries are zero'' is for the three charts, and is in any case not a point of $SL(2,\C)$. Not said in class: the clean statement is that the first row $(a,b)$ of an element is never $(0,0)$ (the determinant would vanish), so $\{a\ne0\}\cup\{b\ne0\}$ is everything. \ts{15}{1}{28:48}
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Questions from the room}\label{sec:sl-questions}
A student asks whether one could not simply use the \emph{four} coordinates, or use $(a,b,c,d)$ with one of them unused. The answer: if one maps with $y$ to $(b,c,d)$ and tries to go back to a quadruple with $a$ chosen independently, one lands in $\C^4$ and not in $SL(2,\C)$: the inverse map does not know the condition, which is encoded precisely by prescribing $a=\frac{1+bc}{d}$. One would lose bijectivity otherwise \ts{15}{1}{30:36} \ts{15}{1}{30:57} \ts{15}{1}{31:18} \ts{15}{1}{31:39} \ts{15}{1}{32:00} \ts{15}{1}{32:20}.

\begin{theorem}[$SL(2,\C)$ is a complex three-dimensional topological manifold]\label{thm:sl-topmanifold}
$SL(2,\C)$ with the induced topology is a topological manifold of complex dimension $\dim_{\C}SL(2,\C)=3$ (real dimension $6$) \ts{15}{1}{29:53} \ts{15}{1}{30:14}.
\end{theorem}
The lecture leaves the sentence at ``topological manifold'' (``some would call it \dots\ no, not yet'').

\section{The smooth structure}\label{sec:sl-smooth}

To get a Lie group one needs a \emph{smooth} manifold: an atlas whose charts are pairwise compatible in the sense of $C^\infty$ (\cref{def:df-ck}), here for the complex-valued case \ts{15}{1}{32:41} \ts{15}{1}{33:07} \ts{15}{1}{33:29} \ts{15}{1}{33:49}. So far the charts $(U,x)$ and $(W,z)$ form a topological atlas $\mathcal A_{\rm top}$ (automatically $C^0$-compatible). Now the transition maps are checked \ts{15}{1}{34:11} \ts{15}{1}{34:33} \ts{15}{1}{34:55}.
\begin{proposition}[The transition maps are holomorphic]\label{prop:sl-transition}
On $x(U\cap W)=\C^*\times\C^*\times\C$ the transition map is
\[
  z\circ x^{-1}\colon(a,b,c)\longmapsto\Bigl(a,\,b,\,\tfrac{1+bc}{a}\Bigr),
\]
and on $z(U\cap W)=\C^*\times\C^*\times\C$ the opposite one is
\[
  x\circ z^{-1}\colon(a,b,d)\longmapsto\Bigl(a,\,b,\,\tfrac{ad-1}{b}\Bigr).
\]
Both are rational maps without poles on their domain, hence differentiable arbitrarily often (indeed holomorphic).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The chart $x^{-1}$ takes $(a,b,c)$ to $(a,b,c,\frac{1+bc}{a})$; then $z$ forgets the third entry: $z\circ x^{-1}(a,b,c)=(a,b,\frac{1+bc}{a})$. On the intersection $a\ne0$ (from $U$) and $b\ne0$ (from $W$), so the target is in $\C^*\times\C^*\times\C$, and the map has the only denominator $a$, non-zero there: it is differentiable any number of times. Similarly $x\circ z^{-1}(a,b,d)=x(a,b,\frac{ad-1}{b},d)=(a,b,\frac{ad-1}{b})$ with the only denominator $b\ne0$. The lecture only does the first one and says that ``the opposite one, which one also needs, you will find the same'' \ts{15}{1}{35:17} \ts{15}{1}{35:38} \ts{15}{1}{35:58} \ts{15}{1}{36:18} \ts{15}{1}{36:41} \ts{15}{1}{37:02} \ts{15}{1}{37:23} \ts{15}{1}{37:44} \ts{15}{1}{38:04} \ts{15}{1}{38:24} \ts{15}{1}{38:45} \ts{15}{1}{39:06} \ts{15}{1}{39:27} \ts{15}{1}{40:10} \ts{15}{1}{40:32}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Complex differentiability is stronger]
Differentiability on $\C$ is much stronger than on $\R$: one could write the transition map as a map between six real numbers (three real and three imaginary parts), differentiable in the real sense, but one would additionally have to check the Cauchy--Riemann equations. For rational maps without poles this is automatic (they are holomorphic), and ``on $\C$ if it is differentiable once it is differentiable arbitrarily often: Taylor expansions exist''; the lecture speaks of ``$C^\infty$'' for convenience. The manifold is then a \emph{complex} manifold in the sense of \cref{ch:l14} (transition functions satisfying Cauchy--Riemann) \ts{15}{1}{39:49} \ts{15}{1}{42:37} \ts{15}{1}{42:59}.
\end{coursenote}
So the topological atlas is in fact a \emph{differentiable} atlas. One can then define uniquely the \textbf{maximal atlas} $\mathcal A$ containing it, by adding all further charts compatible with it \ts{15}{1}{40:53} \ts{15}{1}{41:15}. One never writes it down: it is rather the \emph{excuse} that later, when a computation needs another chart (like the redundant $V$), one may use it, after quickly checking compatibility with the charts already in there. ``That is the philosophy'' \ts{15}{1}{41:35} \ts{15}{1}{41:56} \ts{15}{1}{42:16}. Conclusion: $SL(2,\C)$ with $\mathcal A$ is a smooth, in fact complex-differentiable, manifold \ts{15}{1}{42:37}.

\section{It is a Lie group}\label{sec:sl-liegroup}

At this point $SL(2,\C)$ carries a topology, a differentiable atlas and the group law $\bullet$ (that it is a group was the first thing checked) \ts{15}{1}{42:59} \ts{15}{1}{43:20}. By \cref{def:ll-lie-group} one must still check that
\[
  \mu\colon SL(2,\C)\times SL(2,\C)\to SL(2,\C),\ (g,g')\mapsto g\bullet g',\qquad i\colon SL(2,\C)\to SL(2,\C),\ g\mapsto g^{-1},
\]
are smooth. The Cartesian product of two smooth manifolds inherits a smooth atlas from its factors (a problem-sheet fact), so $\mu$ is a map between smooth manifolds, and so is $i$; smoothness of a map between manifolds is checked chart by chart (\cref{def:df-map}). The lecture carries it out in detail for $i$ \ts{15}{1}{43:40} \ts{15}{1}{44:01} \ts{15}{1}{44:21} \ts{15}{1}{44:44} \ts{15}{1}{45:05} \ts{15}{1}{45:25} \ts{15}{1}{45:45} \ts{15}{1}{46:06} \ts{15}{1}{46:26} \ts{15}{1}{46:48} \ts{15}{1}{47:08}.
\begin{proposition}[The inversion is smooth]\label{prop:sl-inversion}
The map $i\colon g\mapsto g^{-1}$ is smooth (holomorphic).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
It is enough to treat the charts $U$ and $W$ in the domain, since they cover.
Take first the chart $(U,x)$. For $g=(a,b,c,d)$ one has $i(g)=(d,-b,-c,a)$, whose lower-right entry is $a\ne0$: so $i$ maps $U$ into the chart domain $V$ of $y$ (``you can choose the charts independently, but you better make sure that you map into that chart''). The map in coordinates, from an open set of $\C^3$ to an open set of $\C^3$, is
\[
  y\circ i\circ x^{-1}(a,b,c)=y\Bigl(\tfrac{1+bc}{a},-b,-c,a\Bigr)=(-b,-c,a).
\]
(Equivalently: $\frac1{ad-bc}$ in the lecture's formula is $1$ because $a\cdot\frac{1+bc}{a}-bc=1$.) This is linear, so certainly differentiable, and holomorphic \ts{15}{1}{47:30} \ts{15}{1}{47:50} \ts{15}{1}{48:11} \ts{15}{1}{48:31} \ts{15}{1}{48:52} \ts{15}{1}{49:12} \ts{15}{1}{49:33} \ts{15}{1}{49:53} \ts{15}{1}{50:14} \ts{15}{1}{50:34} \ts{15}{1}{50:56} \ts{15}{1}{51:18} \ts{15}{1}{51:38} \ts{15}{1}{52:00} \ts{15}{1}{52:21} \ts{15}{1}{52:41} \ts{15}{1}{53:02} \ts{15}{1}{53:23} \ts{15}{1}{53:44} \ts{15}{1}{54:04} \ts{15}{1}{54:25}.\par
Now the chart $(W,z)$: the upper-right entry of $i(g)$ is $-b\ne0$, so $i$ maps $W$ into $W$, and
\[
  z\circ i\circ z^{-1}(a,b,d)=z\Bigl(d,-b,\tfrac{ad-1}{b}\cdot(-1),a\Bigr)=(d,-b,a),
\]
again linear. Since $U\cup W$ is the whole group, $i$ is smooth \ts{15}{1}{56:08} \ts{15}{1}{56:29} \ts{15}{1}{56:49} \ts{15}{1}{57:10} \ts{15}{1}{57:32}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[A slip, and why the charts are indispensable]
The lecture first reads off the result as ``minus $b$, minus $c$, minus $a$'' and then ``$-b,-c,a$'': the last entry is $a$, the (unchanged) upper-left entry, which became the lower-right one. Also, at the point where the lecture says ``this is obviously differentiable, it takes these numbers to those numbers'', it warns that this is only tempting, because differentiability here means differentiability of a map between \emph{manifolds}: $i$ is a function of four variables restricted to a surface $ad-bc=1$, not an unconstrained function of four variables; one cannot just differentiate in the four entries, and the way out is exactly to ``push the problem down with the charts''. \ts{15}{1}{54:46} \ts{15}{1}{55:07} \ts{15}{1}{55:27} \ts{15}{1}{55:48}
\end{coursenote}
The multiplication $\mu$ is shown to be smooth in exactly the same way, with the atlas $\mathcal A\times\mathcal A$ on $SL(2,\C)\times SL(2,\C)$, and the lecture does not carry it out ``in detail'': in the chart $U\times U$, where $a,e\ne0$, the coordinates of the product are the polynomials $(ae+bg,\ af+b\frac{1+fg}{e},\ ce+dg)$, as written below in \cref{sec:sl-pushforward} \ts{15}{1}{57:54} \ts{15}{1}{58:16} \ts{15}{1}{58:37} \ts{15}{1}{58:58}.
\begin{theorem}[$SL(2,\C)$ is a complex three-dimensional Lie group]\label{thm:sl-liegroup}
$(SL(2,\C),\bullet)$ with the topology and the maximal atlas $\mathcal A$ above is a complex three-dimensional Lie group \ts{15}{1}{59:18} \ts{15}{1}{59:46}.
\end{theorem}
It is a lot of work if one is a real skeptic, and ``it does not come entirely for free'' \ts{15}{1}{58:58}. It is the first example of a Lie group presented in the course, other than the commutative ones of \cref{ex:ll-translation,ex:ll-u1,ex:ll-gl}: so far the Lie group and Lie algebra theory was rushed through without examples, since to construct one ``you have to invest some effort''; it took a bit more than an hour \ts{15}{1}{59:18} \ts{15}{1}{59:46}.

\section{The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$: left-invariant fields}\label{sec:sl-algebra}

Next step: the Lie algebra of the Lie group (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}). \textbf{Notation}: for a Lie group with a capital-letter name the Lie algebra carries the same name in small letters, so $L(SL(2,\C))$ is written $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ (``lots to write''; just notation) \ts{15}{1}{1:00:07} \ts{15}{1}{1:00:27} \ts{15}{1}{1:00:51} \ts{15}{1}{1:01:15} \ts{15}{1}{1:01:36}.
\begin{recall}[Left-invariant fields and the isomorphism with $T_eG$]
$L(G)$ is the set of vector fields $X$ with $(L_g)_*X_h=X_{gh}$ (\cref{def:ll-left-inv,def:ll-LG}); $L(G)\cong T_eG$ via $j(A)_g=(L_g)_*A$ (\cref{thm:ll-iso}); the bracket $[\![A,B]\!]=[j(A),j(B)]_e$ is the Lie bracket of $T_eG$ (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}).
\end{recall}
As a \emph{set}, then, $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is the set of all left-invariant vector fields on $SL(2,\C)$. In this case the left translation by $g=(a,b,c,d)$ is the matrix product from the left,
\[
  L_g\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\g'&h\end{pmatrix}=g\bullet\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\g'&h\end{pmatrix}
\]
(the lecture reuses the letter $g$ for the entry; here the third entry of the argument is written $g'$ to avoid a clash). It is a smooth map $SL(2,\C)\to SL(2,\C)$ (a restriction of $\mu$ where one slot is fixed), so it has a push-forward $(L_g)_*$ (\cref{def:tb-push}), and the left-invariant fields are those with $(L_g)_*X_h=X_{gh}$ for all $g,h$ \ts{15}{1}{1:01:56} \ts{15}{1}{1:02:17} \ts{15}{1}{1:02:38} \ts{15}{1}{1:03:00} \ts{15}{1}{1:03:20} \ts{15}{1}{1:03:40} \ts{15}{1}{1:04:01} \ts{15}{1}{1:04:22} \ts{15}{1}{1:04:44}.

By the isomorphism of \cref{thm:ll-iso} between $T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$ and $L(SL(2,\C))$ it is enough to look at the three-dimensional vector space of tangent vectors at the identity $\mathbb 1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, \emph{instead of} the space of fields, which is a priori infinite-dimensional ($\C$-vector subspace of the sections of the tangent bundle) \ts{15}{1}{1:05:05} \ts{15}{1}{1:05:27} \ts{15}{1}{1:05:48} \ts{15}{1}{1:06:08}. How can the two be isomorphic? Left-invariance is a very strong condition, and the picture shows its advantage \ts{15}{1}{1:06:28} \ts{15}{1}{1:06:48}.
\begin{nfigure}

The bracket is the point. On the space of all vector fields one has the differential-geometric commutator $[X,Y]f=X(Yf)-Y(Xf)$: one field acts on the other, which requires entire fields since one has to differentiate one along the other. At a single point one cannot do this: applying the white vector to the green \emph{field} gives a result, but one cannot then apply the green \emph{vector} to the white one, since one has to know how the second field changes. So how does $T_eG$ inherit a bracket? By \cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}: construct from each tangent vector at the identity an entire left-invariant field by pushing forward with all $L_g$, bracket the fields, and read the result at the identity \ts{15}{1}{1:07:09} \ts{15}{1}{1:07:30} \ts{15}{1}{1:07:51} \ts{15}{1}{1:08:11} \ts{15}{1}{1:08:32} \ts{15}{1}{1:08:52} \ts{15}{1}{1:09:13}.
\begin{definition}[Recalled: the bracket on $T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$]\label{def:sl-bracket}
For tangent vectors $A,B\in T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$ define $[\![A,B]\!]\in T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$ by the vector field $[\,(L_g)_*A,\ (L_g)_*B\,]$ (the commutator of the two fields obtained for varying $g$; the lecture's notation ``there should not be a $g$ there, there should be the abstract field'') \ts{15}{1}{1:09:39} \ts{15}{1}{1:10:01} \ts{15}{1}{1:10:22} \ts{15}{1}{1:10:42} \ts{15}{1}{1:11:04} \ts{15}{1}{1:11:25} \ts{15}{1}{1:11:46}.
\end{definition}
It is bilinear, so it is determined by its values on a \emph{basis}; to calculate one chooses coordinates anyway: the basis $\partial/\partial x^i|_{\mathbb 1}$, $i=1,2,3$, induced by the chart $x$ (\cref{def:tv-ddx}); then these are pushed forward to fields. \textbf{Task}: determine the bracket on this basis, i.e.\ the \emph{structure constants} of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ \ts{15}{1}{1:12:06} \ts{15}{1}{1:12:26} \ts{15}{1}{1:12:47} \ts{15}{1}{1:13:10}.

\subsection{The push-forward of the coordinate vectors}\label{sec:sl-pushforward}

The chart $(U,x)$ contains the identity (it has $a=1\ne0$), so the coordinate vectors of $x$ at the identity are available. Let $g=(a,b,c,d)\in U$ and $i\in\{1,2,3\}$. The vector $(L_g)_*\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}\in T_gSL(2,\C)$ is computed by its action on an arbitrary smooth function $f\colon SL(2,\C)\to\C$ \ts{15}{1}{1:13:32} \ts{15}{1}{1:13:53} \ts{15}{1}{1:14:14} \ts{15}{1}{1:14:35} \ts{15}{1}{1:14:55} \ts{15}{1}{1:15:15} \ts{15}{1}{1:15:36} \ts{15}{1}{1:15:57}. By the definition of push-forward,
\[
  \bigl((L_g)_*\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}\bigr)f=\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}\bigl(f\circ L_g\bigr),
\]
and by the definition of the chart-induced basis the right-hand side is the $i$-th partial derivative of $f\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}$ at the point $x(\mathbb 1)=(1,0,0)$. Now insert a chart map and its inverse, $f\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}=(f\circ x^{-1})\circ(x\circ L_g\circ x^{-1})$, an identity between maps from an open subset of $\C^3$ to $\C$ (``that obviously has no effect''), and one is in the realm of ordinary analysis: the \textbf{multidimensional chain rule} (``learned in the first week of your studies, just properly applied here'') \ts{15}{1}{1:16:18} \ts{15}{1}{1:16:38} \ts{15}{1}{1:17:01} \ts{15}{1}{1:17:29} \ts{15}{1}{1:17:50} \ts{15}{1}{1:18:10} \ts{15}{1}{1:18:30} \ts{15}{1}{1:18:51} \ts{15}{1}{1:19:11} \ts{15}{1}{1:19:31} \ts{15}{1}{1:19:52} \ts{15}{1}{1:20:14} \ts{15}{1}{1:20:35} \ts{15}{1}{1:20:57} \ts{15}{1}{1:21:18}:
\[
  \partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}(f\circ L_g)=\sum_{m=1}^{3}\partial_m\bigl(f\circ x^{-1}\bigr)\Big|_{x(g)}\ \cdot\ \partial_i\bigl(x^m\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}\bigr)\Big|_{(1,0,0)}
  =M^m{}_i\ \partial_m|_g f,
\]
with the matrix of the inner derivatives
\[
  M^m{}_i:=\partial_i\bigl(x^m\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}\bigr)(1,0,0).
\]
Here the \emph{outer} derivative is the $m$-th partial of $f\circ x^{-1}$, evaluated at $x(L_g\mathbb 1)=x(g)=(a,b,c)$, which is nothing but $\partial_m|_gf$ (this is why one inserts the chart: the symbol $\partial/\partial x^m$ of differential geometry always has a chart behind it, ``this is what should have been used always'') \ts{15}{1}{1:21:39} \ts{15}{1}{1:22:00} \ts{15}{1}{1:22:20} \ts{15}{1}{1:22:41} \ts{15}{1}{1:23:01} \ts{15}{1}{1:23:22} \ts{15}{1}{1:23:43} \ts{15}{1}{1:24:03} \ts{15}{1}{1:24:26} \ts{15}{1}{1:24:47} \ts{15}{1}{1:25:08} \ts{15}{1}{1:25:30} \ts{15}{1}{1:25:51} \ts{15}{1}{1:26:11} \ts{15}{1}{1:26:33} \ts{15}{1}{1:26:54} \ts{15}{1}{1:27:15}.

\textbf{Where the group law enters.} What is needed is the map $x\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}$. Let $h=(e,f,g',h')\in U$ with chart coordinates $(e,f,g')$ and $h'=\frac{1+fg'}{e}$. By the group law $\bullet$,
\[
  g\bullet h=\begin{pmatrix}ae+bg'&af+bh'\\ce+dg'&cf+dh'\end{pmatrix},
\]
so in the chart $x$ (first row and lower-left entry):
\[
  x\circ L_g\circ x^{-1}(e,f,g')=\Bigl(ae+bg',\ af+b\,\tfrac{1+fg'}{e},\ ce+dg'\Bigr).
\]
This is the point where the \emph{specific} group law (matrix multiplication) enters and passes everything it has to say on to the Lie algebra; ``the rest is all formal dressing: we need it to know what we are doing, but this is the point'' \ts{15}{1}{1:27:35} \ts{15}{1}{1:27:55} \ts{15}{1}{1:28:16} \ts{15}{1}{1:28:37} \ts{15}{1}{1:28:58} \ts{15}{1}{1:29:18} \ts{15}{1}{1:29:39} \ts{15}{1}{1:29:59} \ts{15}{1}{1:30:19} \ts{15}{1}{1:30:41} \ts{15}{1}{1:31:03} \ts{15}{1}{1:31:23} \ts{15}{1}{1:31:46}.

The $3\times3$ matrix of partial derivatives with respect to $(e,f,g')$, row $m$ and column $i$ (``this just means writing down these numbers in a systematic way, nothing else is implied'') \ts{15}{1}{1:32:07} \ts{15}{1}{1:32:27} \ts{15}{1}{1:32:48} \ts{15}{1}{1:33:09} \ts{15}{1}{1:33:31} \ts{15}{1}{1:33:52} \ts{15}{1}{1:34:13}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item first row, $m=1$: derivative of $ae+bg'$: $\partial_e=a$, $\partial_f=0$, $\partial_{g'}=b$ \ts{15}{1}{1:34:33};
  \item second row, $m=2$: derivative of $af+b\frac{1+fg'}{e}$: $\partial_e=-b\frac{1+fg'}{e^2}$, $\partial_f=a+\frac{bg'}{e}$, $\partial_{g'}=\frac{bf}{e}$ \ts{15}{1}{1:34:55} \ts{15}{1}{1:35:15} \ts{15}{1}{1:35:36} \ts{15}{1}{1:35:57};
  \item third row, $m=3$: derivative of $ce+dg'$: $\partial_e=c$, $\partial_f=0$, $\partial_{g'}=d$ \ts{15}{1}{1:36:17}.
\end{itemize}
\begin{coursenote}[Two slips corrected from the room: the missing $a$, and where to evaluate]
In the second row the lecture first leaves out the term $a$ in the $f$-derivative ($af$ is differentiated to $a$, the product $b\frac{1+fg'}{e}$ adds $\frac{bg'}{e}$); a student points it out (``ah yes, thank you, plus $a$''). More importantly, the lecture first writes the result with the general letters $e,f,g'$ and the point $g$ both present and a student notes that the derivative has to be \emph{evaluated at the identity}, $e=1,f=g'=0$: ``why are there still $e,f,g'$? of course''. After evaluation the point $g=(a,b,c,d)$ is the only place where the entries $a,b,c,d$ survive, which is as it must be, since this is the point where the vector is pushed to \ts{15}{1}{1:36:38} \ts{15}{1}{1:36:58} \ts{15}{1}{1:37:31} \ts{15}{1}{1:37:52} \ts{15}{1}{1:38:12} \ts{15}{1}{1:38:34} \ts{15}{1}{1:38:54} \ts{15}{1}{1:39:15}.
\end{coursenote}
Evaluating at $(e,f,g')=(1,0,0)$ one finds
\[
  M=\bigl(M^m{}_i\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&b\\-b&a&0\\c&0&d\end{pmatrix},
\]
the only part that depends on the point $g$ \ts{15}{1}{1:39:36} \ts{15}{1}{1:39:57} \ts{15}{1}{1:40:18}. Reading down the columns one gets the push-forwards of the three coordinate vectors, which are defined at $g=(a,b,c,d)$ (with $d$ here the function $\frac{1+bc}{a}$ in the chart) \ts{15}{1}{1:40:38} \ts{15}{1}{1:40:59} \ts{15}{1}{1:41:21} \ts{15}{1}{1:41:42}:
\begin{theorem}[The left-invariant vector fields of $SL(2,\C)$]\label{thm:sl-fields}
In the chart $(U,x)$ the left-invariant vector fields $\Lambda_i|_g:=(L_g)_*\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}$ are
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \Lambda_1&=a\,\partial_1-b\,\partial_2+c\,\partial_3,\\
  \Lambda_2&=a\,\partial_2,\\
  \Lambda_3&=b\,\partial_1+d\,\partial_3,
  \end{aligned}
  \qquad d=\tfrac{1+bc}{a},
\]
where $\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3$ are the coordinate-induced vectors $\partial/\partial x^i$ at the point $g=(a,b,c,d)$ (the lecture writes: $\partial/\partial a$, $\partial/\partial b$, $\partial/\partial c$).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\Lambda_i|_g=M^m{}_i\,\partial_m|_g$, i.e.\ the $i$-th column of $M$ gives the components. That the fields are left-invariant (not checked in the lecture beyond ``we check that if I do this construction the result is a left-invariant field'') follows from the chain rule for push-forward and from $L_h\circ L_g=L_{hg}$: $(L_h)_*\Lambda_i|_g=(L_h)_*(L_g)_*\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}=(L_{hg})_*\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}=\Lambda_i|_{hg}$ \ts{15}{1}{1:42:03} \ts{15}{1}{1:42:24} \ts{15}{1}{1:42:44} \ts{15}{1}{1:43:05} \ts{15}{1}{1:43:26} \ts{15}{1}{1:43:48}.
\end{proof}
So from three tangent vectors at the identity, one obtains three vector fields: they are linear combinations of the coordinate fields at each point $(a,b,c,d)$ with coefficients $a,-b,c$; $0,a,0$; $b,0,d$ \ts{15}{1}{1:44:08}. At the identity $a=d=1$, $b=c=0$ they reduce to $\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3$, as they must.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: what the three vectors are as matrices]
The curve $t\mapsto(1+t,0,0,\frac1{1+t})$ has velocity $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ at $t=0$, so $\partial_1|_{\mathbb 1}$ is this matrix; similarly $\partial_2|_{\mathbb 1}$ is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ ($b$-direction) and $\partial_3|_{\mathbb 1}$ is $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ ($c$-direction): the three traceless $2\times2$ matrices $H,E,F$. The lecture stresses in the next lecture that the Lie algebra \emph{is} the abstract tangent space and ``has nothing to do with $2\times2$ traceless matrices'': matrices will be a \emph{representation} of it (\cref{ch:l16}). Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{The bracket: set-up}\label{sec:sl-bracketsetup}
Now the double bracket of \cref{def:sl-bracket} can be computed on the basis: for instance for $\partial_1|_{\mathbb 1}$ and $\partial_2|_{\mathbb 1}$ it is the differential-geometric commutator
\[
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_1|_{\mathbb 1},\partial_2|_{\mathbb 1}\bigr]\!\bigr]\ \leftrightarrow\ \bigl[\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2\bigr]=\bigl[a\partial_1-b\partial_2+c\partial_3,\ a\partial_2\bigr],
\]
acting on an arbitrary function: one uses $[X,Y]f=X(Yf)-Y(Xf)$ and meets, everywhere, symbols $\partial_if$ of the form $\partial_i(f\circ x^{-1})$ at $x(g)$ \ts{15}{1}{1:44:31} \ts{15}{1}{1:44:53} \ts{15}{1}{1:45:13} \ts{15}{1}{1:45:34} \ts{15}{1}{1:45:56} \ts{15}{1}{1:46:17} \ts{15}{1}{1:46:37} \ts{15}{1}{1:46:58} \ts{15}{1}{1:47:18}.

At this point the lecturer notices a slip in the set-up (``bad definition, of course I forgot something'') \ts{15}{1}{1:47:46}: the commutator of the two fields is a vector at the point $g=(a,b,c,d)$, while the bracket on $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ must be a vector \emph{at the identity}. The remedy proposed: push back with $(L_{g^{-1}})_*$, the inverse of the left translation ($L_g$ has the inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$), and obtain a vector at the identity: a linear combination
\[
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_1,\partial_2\bigr]\!\bigr]=C^1{}_{12}\,\partial_1|_{\mathbb 1}+C^2{}_{12}\,\partial_2|_{\mathbb 1}+C^3{}_{12}\,\partial_3|_{\mathbb 1}
\]
(the lecture calls the three coefficients ``flower, pine tree, star''), whose coefficients are read off as the structure constants $C^m{}_{12}$ of \cref{def:cl-structure}; the same is done for the other independent pairs and one obtains the structure constants of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ \ts{15}{1}{1:48:07} \ts{15}{1}{1:48:27} \ts{15}{1}{1:48:48} \ts{15}{1}{1:49:10} \ts{15}{1}{1:49:32} \ts{15}{1}{1:49:53} \ts{15}{1}{1:50:14} \ts{15}{1}{1:50:34}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: back-translation is the same as evaluating at $\mathbb 1$]
The commutator of two left-invariant fields is again left-invariant (\cref{thm:ll-subalgebra}), so $(L_{g^{-1}})_*[\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2]_g=[\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2]_{\mathbb 1}$: the three coefficients do not depend on the point $g$. This is why in the next lecture (\cref{sec:sa-bracket}) it is enough to evaluate the commutator at the identity, which is also exactly $j^{-1}$ of \cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}. \ts{15}{1}{1:48:07}
\end{coursenote}
The lecture runs out of time here (``two lines, time is up''): the computation is picked up next time, together with the Dynkin diagram of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ \ts{15}{1}{1:50:55}.
