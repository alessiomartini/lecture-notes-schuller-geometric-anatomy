\section{Plan: complex Lie algebras and the Levi decomposition}\label{sec:cl-plan}

In the previous lecture one went from a Lie group to its Lie algebra (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}). Next lecture one goes the opposite way: start from a Lie algebra and ask which Lie group, and which part of one, can be reconstructed (\cref{ch:l18}). Today one stays on the level of the Lie algebra, with a question quite independent of where it comes from: a \textbf{classification of all Lie algebras}. This is possible, and particularly simple, for \emph{complex} Lie algebras, i.e.\ a complex vector space $L$ with an (abstract) Lie bracket (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra}) \ts{14}{1}{0:00} \ts{14}{1}{0:21} \ts{14}{1}{0:42} \ts{14}{1}{1:03}.

Such algebras arise, for instance, from a \textbf{complex Lie group}: a Lie group whose underlying smooth manifold is a \emph{complex manifold}, as defined when various transition conditions between charts were studied: the transition functions do not only have to be smooth, they must satisfy the Cauchy--Riemann equations \ts{14}{1}{1:24} \ts{14}{1}{1:45} \ts{14}{1}{2:06}.

The key theorem for the promised classification is due to Levi. The lecture does not prove much today: several results are given \emph{without proof}, but the construction and the theorems linking its elements are presented in a conceptually clean way, ``and that is important for applications'' \ts{14}{1}{2:27} \ts{14}{1}{2:48}. The first target:
\begin{theorem}[Levi decomposition, to be explained]\label{thm:cl-levi}
Every finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra $L$ can be decomposed as
\[
  L=R\ \oplus_s\ \bigl(L_1\oplus\dots\oplus L_n\bigr),
\]
a \emph{semidirect} sum ($\oplus_s$) of a solvable Lie algebra $R$ and a \emph{direct} sum of a finite number of simple Lie algebras $L_1,\dots,L_n$  \ts{14}{1}{3:10} \ts{14}{1}{3:31} \ts{14}{1}{3:52}.
\end{theorem}
Since in the formula the sums are a little more complicated than for vector spaces, one has to explain: $R$ and the $L_i$, direct and semidirect sums \ts{14}{1}{4:14}.

\subsection{Solvable and simple Lie algebras}\label{sec:cl-solvable}
\begin{definition}[New concepts: solvable Lie algebra]\label{def:cl-solvable}
Let $R$ be a Lie algebra. Write $[R,R]$ for the set (more precisely: the span) of all brackets $[r_1,r_2]$ with $r_1,r_2\in R$. One can iterate, feeding the previous set into both slots of the bracket:
\[
  R\ \supseteq\ [R,R]\ \supseteq\ \bigl[[R,R],[R,R]\bigr]\ \supseteq\ \dots
\]
Each set is contained in the previous one. If this sequence \emph{terminates}, i.e.\ if at some stage one arrives at the vector space containing only the zero element $\{0\}$, then $R$ is called \textbf{solvable}. This is the first bit of the decomposition: $R$ is a Lie algebra in its own right (a subalgebra, though for the theorem it is enough that it is a Lie algebra) which is solvable \ts{14}{1}{5:16} \ts{14}{1}{5:43} \ts{14}{1}{6:03} \ts{14}{1}{6:24} \ts{14}{1}{6:44} \ts{14}{1}{7:06} \ts{14}{1}{7:29}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Why ``solvable''?]
Not said in class: the name comes from the analogy with solvable groups (Galois theory), whose derived series also reaches the trivial group. The lecture does not discuss the name. 
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: ideal, simple Lie algebra]\label{def:cl-simple}
An \textbf{ideal} of a Lie algebra $L$ is a sub-vector space $I\subseteq L$ such that the bracket of any element of $I$ with \emph{any} element of $L$ stays in $I$: $[I,L]\subseteq I$. Its elements are so ``strong'' that even bracketing them with an element from outside one remains inside. The sets $\{0\}$ and $L$ itself are always ideals, since $[\{0\},L]=\{0\}$ and $[L,L]\subseteq L$: these are the \textbf{trivial ideals}. A Lie algebra $L$ is \textbf{simple} if it is \emph{non-abelian} and contains \emph{no non-trivial ideals} (it would make no sense to demand no ideals at all, the two trivial ones are always there) \ts{14}{1}{7:49} \ts{14}{1}{8:14} \ts{14}{1}{8:35} \ts{14}{1}{8:58} \ts{14}{1}{9:19} \ts{14}{1}{9:40} \ts{14}{1}{10:00} \ts{14}{1}{10:21} \ts{14}{1}{10:43} \ts{14}{1}{11:03} \ts{14}{1}{11:24}.
\end{definition}
So a finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra decomposes into a solvable algebra semidirect-summed with a direct sum of finitely many simple algebras \ts{14}{1}{11:45}.

\subsection{Direct and semidirect sums of Lie algebras}\label{sec:cl-sums}
\begin{definition}[New concepts: direct and semidirect sum of Lie algebras]\label{def:cl-sums}
For vector spaces the direct sum is the Cartesian product with componentwise operations (\cref{sec:rm-projective}). For \emph{Lie algebras} the sums carry a compatibility condition with the bracket.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] The \textbf{direct sum} $L_1\oplus L_2$: as a vector space the standard direct sum, with the extra condition that the splitting into the two parts is respected by the bracket: $[l_1,l_2]=0$ for all $l_1\in L_1$ and $l_2\in L_2$ (the bracket in the big algebra). The two parts do not see each other with respect to the Lie bracket.
  \item[(b)] The \textbf{semidirect sum} $R\oplus_s L_1$ (``$\oplus_s$'' in the lecture), which exists only between \emph{Lie algebras} (a vector space alone has not enough structure): again the direct sum as vector spaces, but with the weaker condition that the bracket $[r,l_1]$ of an element of $R$ and an element of $L_1$ \emph{is not zero} (that would be the direct sum) but lies in $R$ again; that is, $R$ is an \textbf{ideal} of the sum.
\end{enumerate}
\ts{14}{1}{12:05} \ts{14}{1}{12:26} \ts{14}{1}{12:47} \ts{14}{1}{13:10} \ts{14}{1}{13:30} \ts{14}{1}{13:51} \ts{14}{1}{14:11} \ts{14}{1}{14:31}.
\end{definition}
With this, \cref{thm:cl-levi}: every finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra decomposes into a semidirect sum of a solvable Lie algebra with a direct sum of simple Lie algebras \ts{14}{1}{14:51} \ts{14}{1}{15:12}. This is the basic Levi decomposition theorem. Remarks:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] It is \emph{very hard} to classify the solvable Lie algebras: there is no classification known, one has to rely on a case-by-case analysis  \ts{14}{1}{15:33}.
  \item[(b)] It is indeed possible to classify the \emph{simple} Lie algebras \ts{14}{1}{15:53}.
  \item[(c)] A Lie algebra that has \emph{no solvable part}, i.e.\ can be written as a direct sum of simple Lie algebras, is called \textbf{semisimple}. This is very easy to check, as will be seen in a moment \ts{14}{1}{16:16} \ts{14}{1}{16:38} \ts{14}{1}{17:00}.
  \item[(d)] The solvable algebras cannot be classified, so one focuses on the semisimple ones; and one does not even need all of them, since a semisimple algebra decomposes into finitely many simple ones that do not see each other (a direct sum in the Lie algebra sense). It suffices to study the building blocks to understand every Lie algebra that decomposes like this: the \textbf{simple Lie algebras} are the independent building blocks to be classified in these lectures  \ts{14}{1}{17:20} \ts{14}{1}{17:41} \ts{14}{1}{18:05}.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{coursenote}[Fine print on the definitions]
The lecture defines simple as ``non-abelian with no non-trivial ideals'' and ``every simple algebra is semisimple'' (used later); semisimple is defined here as a direct sum of simples without a solvable part. In the literature semisimple is usually defined as having no non-zero solvable ideal, and Levi's theorem holds over any field of characteristic zero; the lecture states it over $\C$. Also, the lecture's ``semidirect sum with a solvable part'' means $R$ is a solvable ideal, as in (b) of the definition; the semisimple part is a subalgebra, not an ideal in general. Not said in class. \ts{14}{1}{15:12}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{history}[Levi, Killing, Cartan, Dynkin]
\emph{Not said in class; the lecture only says ``a classic result, by Killing and independently by Cartan''.} Wilhelm Killing classified the simple Lie algebras over $\C$ in 1888--1890 (with gaps), \'Elie Cartan completed and put the theory on a firm footing in his 1894 thesis; E.~E.~Levi proved the decomposition theorem in 1905; the diagrams are due to E.~B.~Dynkin (1940s).\par
\smallskip
\emph{References.} W.~Killing, Math.\ Ann.\ 31 (1888), 33 (1889), 34 (1889), 36 (1890); \'E.~Cartan, \emph{Sur la structure des groupes de transformations finis et continus}, thesis, Paris 1894; E.~E.~Levi, Atti Accad.\ Sci.\ Torino 40 (1905) 551--565; E.~B.~Dynkin, Uspekhi Mat.\ Nauk 2 (1947).
\end{history}

\section{The adjoint map and the Killing form}\label{sec:cl-killing}

Preparation for the classification: two technical constructions \ts{14}{1}{18:05} \ts{14}{1}{18:27}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: adjoint map]\label{def:cl-ad}
Let $(L,[\cdot,\cdot])$ be a complex Lie algebra and $h\in L$. The \textbf{adjoint map} with respect to $h$ is
\[
  \mathrm{ad}_h\colon L\to L,\qquad \mathrm{ad}_h(l):=[h,l],
\]
symbolically $\mathrm{ad}_h=[h,\,\cdot\,]$ with an open slot. It is linear since the bracket is linear in the second entry. It is one of those formal tricks: the commutator could be written out each time, but it is convenient to think of the map $\mathrm{ad}_h$ (an endomorphism of $L$) rather than of the bracket acting on something always the same. It will be used heavily \ts{14}{1}{18:49} \ts{14}{1}{19:10} \ts{14}{1}{19:32} \ts{14}{1}{19:53} \ts{14}{1}{20:15} \ts{14}{1}{20:36}.
\end{definition}
Before looking at components, a second object of the utmost importance, defined without a basis \ts{14}{1}{20:56}. Throughout the lecture $L$ is a complex Lie algebra.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: Killing form]\label{def:cl-killing}
The \textbf{Killing form} is the bilinear map $K\colon L\times L\to\C$ (the lecture says $\to L$ at times, a slip) defined by
\[
  K(a,b):=\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\mathrm{ad}_a\circ\mathrm{ad}_b\bigr),
\]
the trace of the endomorphism $\mathrm{ad}_a\circ\mathrm{ad}_b$ of $L$ \ts{14}{1}{21:18} \ts{14}{1}{21:39} \ts{14}{1}{21:59} \ts{14}{1}{22:20}.
\end{definition}
``Form'' is a little unfortunate: it refers to a bilinear \emph{form}, which need not be antisymmetric, unlike the differential forms of earlier lectures. The Killing form will be \emph{symmetric}: because $L$ is finite-dimensional the trace is cyclic (this fails in infinite-dimensional spaces, which causes trouble in quantum mechanics), so $K(a,b)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_a\mathrm{ad}_b)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_b\mathrm{ad}_a)=K(b,a)$ \ts{14}{1}{22:41} \ts{14}{1}{23:01} \ts{14}{1}{23:21} \ts{14}{1}{23:42}.
\begin{theorem}[Cartan's criterion, quoted]\label{thm:cl-cartan-criterion}
A finite-dimensional complex Lie algebra $L$ is semisimple if and only if its Killing form $K$ is non-degenerate (\emph{non-degenerate}: if $K(a,b)=0$ for all $a\in L$ then $b=0$)  \ts{14}{1}{24:05} \ts{14}{1}{24:25}.
\end{theorem}
Without proof (``I think I mentioned this before''). Semisimplicity, that the algebra is a direct sum of simple algebras, i.e.\ that there is no solvable part, sounds at first not so easy to determine; but all one does is calculate the Killing form and check whether it is non-degenerate, in which case there is no solvable part \ts{14}{1}{24:47} \ts{14}{1}{25:07}. Equivalently: $L$ is semisimple iff $K$ is a \emph{pseudo-inner product} (\cref{def:ll-pseudo}); over $\C$ there is no signature to speak of \ts{14}{1}{34:58} \ts{14}{1}{35:19} \ts{14}{1}{35:40}.

\subsection{Components: structure constants}\label{sec:cl-components}
For actual calculations one goes to a basis \ts{14}{1}{25:07} \ts{14}{1}{25:31}. $\mathrm{ad}_h$ is a linear map on the underlying vector space and $K$ a bilinear form (non-degenerate if $L$ is semisimple), so both have components with respect to a basis $E_1,\dots,E_{\dim L}$ of $L$ and its dual basis $\varepsilon^1,\dots,\varepsilon^{\dim L}$ of $L^*$ \ts{14}{1}{25:52} \ts{14}{1}{26:12} \ts{14}{1}{26:34} \ts{14}{1}{26:55}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: structure constants]\label{def:cl-structure}
Since the bracket of two basis elements is again in $L$, it can be expanded in the basis:
\[
  [E_i,E_k]=C^{m}{}_{ik}\,E_m
\]
(summation convention). The complex numbers $C^m{}_{ik}$ are the \textbf{structure constants} (or structure coefficients) of the Lie algebra \emph{with respect to the chosen basis} \ts{14}{1}{28:20} \ts{14}{1}{28:41} \ts{14}{1}{29:03} \ts{14}{1}{29:23} \ts{14}{1}{29:44}.
\end{definition}
\begin{proposition}[Components of $\mathrm{ad}$ and of $K$]\label{prop:cl-components}
Let $(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i})^j{}_k:=\varepsilon^j\bigl(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i}E_k\bigr)$ be the $(j,k)$ component of $\mathrm{ad}_{E_i}$. Then
\[
  (\mathrm{ad}_{E_i})^j{}_k=C^j{}_{ik},\qquad K_{ij}=C^m{}_{in}\,C^n{}_{jm}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
One extracts the component of an endomorphism by plugging in the basis vector and applying the dual one (the definition of components of a tensor, \cref{def:te-components}):
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \varepsilon^j(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i}E_k)&=\varepsilon^j([E_i,E_k])\\
  &=C^m{}_{ik}\,\varepsilon^j(E_m)\\
  &=C^m{}_{ik}\,\delta^j_m=C^j{}_{ik}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
(The upper index of the structure constant is the row index, the middle one labels which map one looks at.) \ts{14}{1}{26:55} \ts{14}{1}{27:16} \ts{14}{1}{27:38} \ts{14}{1}{27:59} \ts{14}{1}{30:05} \ts{14}{1}{30:25} \ts{14}{1}{30:45} \ts{14}{1}{31:07} \ts{14}{1}{31:28}\par
For the Killing form plug in the basis, $K_{ij}=K(E_i,E_j)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i}\circ\mathrm{ad}_{E_j})$. The composition of the maps is mimicked at the level of the coefficients by matrix multiplication, $(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i}\mathrm{ad}_{E_j})^l{}_k=C^l{}_{im}C^m{}_{jk}$, and the trace sets $l=k$ and sums:
\[
  K_{ij}=C^l{}_{im}\,C^m{}_{jl}.
\]
There are four open-looking indices ($i$, $j$ and the two summed pairs) and one contracts in the only meaningful way. \ts{14}{1}{31:49} \ts{14}{1}{32:10} \ts{14}{1}{32:31} \ts{14}{1}{32:52} \ts{14}{1}{33:12} \ts{14}{1}{33:33} \ts{14}{1}{33:55} \ts{14}{1}{34:16}
\end{proof}
So $\mathrm{ad}$ ``represents'' the structure constants without choosing a basis, which is why this abstract map plays such a big role. The Killing form in components is easy to calculate: compute all brackets of the basis elements, form $K_{ij}$, and check the matrix for non-degeneracy; if it is non-degenerate the algebra is semisimple \ts{14}{1}{31:28} \ts{14}{1}{34:37} \ts{14}{1}{34:58}.

\subsection{$\mathrm{ad}$ is antisymmetric with respect to $K$}\label{sec:cl-antisym}
In general, for a linear map $f\colon V\to V$ and a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form $B$ on $V$, $f$ is called \textbf{symmetric} (resp.\ \textbf{antisymmetric}) with respect to $B$ if
\[
  B\bigl(f(v),w\bigr)=\pm\,B\bigl(v,f(w)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V,
\]
with $+$ for symmetric and $-$ for antisymmetric \ts{14}{1}{36:00} \ts{14}{1}{36:21} \ts{14}{1}{36:43} \ts{14}{1}{37:08} \ts{14}{1}{37:29}. A fact, which one can check:
\begin{proposition}[$\mathrm{ad}_h$ is antisymmetric with respect to $K$]\label{prop:cl-ad-antisym}
For every $h\in L$ the map $\mathrm{ad}_h$ is antisymmetric with respect to the Killing form: $K([h,a],b)=-K(a,[h,b])$ \ts{14}{1}{37:50} \ts{14}{1}{38:12}.
\end{proposition}
The Killing form is non-degenerate if $L$ is semisimple, and a simple algebra is in any case semisimple. From now on one focuses on the simple pieces $L_1,\dots,L_n$  \ts{14}{1}{38:34}. This closes the preparation: the adjoint map with respect to an element, and the Killing form \ts{14}{1}{38:54}.
\begin{exercise}[Antisymmetry of $\mathrm{ad}$]\label{ex:cl-ad-antisym}
\emph{Not said in class.} Prove \cref{prop:cl-ad-antisym}: from the Jacobi identity $\mathrm{ad}_{[h,a]}=[\mathrm{ad}_h,\mathrm{ad}_a]$, and invariance $K([a,b],c)=K(a,[b,c])$ obtained from the cyclicity of the trace. (\cref{exam:ch14}.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Cartan subalgebras and roots}\label{sec:cl-cartan}

Now the key definition of the classification; everything follows from this definition and the following theorem  \ts{14}{1}{39:16}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: Cartan subalgebra]\label{def:cl-cartan}
Let $(L,[\cdot,\cdot])$ be a complex Lie algebra, $d=\dim L$. A \textbf{Cartan subalgebra} $H$ (the letter ``$H$'' is traditional, probably because $C$ was already taken by the structure constants) is a \emph{maximal} subalgebra of $L$ (in particular a vector subspace) such that there exists a basis $H_1,\dots,H_m$ of $H$ which can be extended to a basis
\[
  H_1,\dots,H_m,E_1,\dots,E_{d-m}\quad\text{of }L
\]
with the extension vectors being \textbf{eigenvectors of $\mathrm{ad}_h$ for every $h\in H$}:
\[
  [h,E_\alpha]=\lambda_\alpha(h)\,E_\alpha\qquad\text{for all }h\in H,\ \alpha=1,\dots,d-m.
\]
The eigenvalue $\lambda_\alpha(h)\in\C$ depends on which $E_\alpha$ is considered (the $E_1$ has another eigenvalue than $E_2$) and also on $h$ (for another $h$ it is a different map)  \ts{14}{1}{39:36} \ts{14}{1}{39:57} \ts{14}{1}{40:17} \ts{14}{1}{40:38} \ts{14}{1}{41:00} \ts{14}{1}{41:20} \ts{14}{1}{41:42} \ts{14}{1}{42:05} \ts{14}{1}{42:26} \ts{14}{1}{42:46} \ts{14}{1}{43:07}.
\end{definition}
Of course the big question is whether such a thing always exists:
\begin{theorem}[Existence and abelianness, quoted]\label{thm:cl-cartan}
(i) Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra possesses a Cartan subalgebra. (ii) If $L$ is simple, then $H$ is \textbf{abelian}: any two elements of $H$ commute \ts{14}{1}{43:28} \ts{14}{1}{43:50} \ts{14}{1}{44:12} \ts{14}{1}{44:32} \ts{14}{1}{44:53}.
\end{theorem}
The lecture does not prove it (``a little involved''); this is the central statement for the classification, to be looked up under \emph{Cartan subalgebra} if one does not believe it. The abelianness is consistent: the $\mathrm{ad}_h$ for all $h\in H$ are \emph{simultaneously diagonalised} by the $E_\alpha$, and simultaneously diagonalisable maps better commute \ts{14}{1}{44:12} \ts{14}{1}{45:14} \ts{14}{1}{45:35}.
\begin{coursenote}[Existence is for finite-dimensional algebras, diagonalisation for semisimple ones]
As stated, the definition is the one for complex semisimple (here simple) algebras; for a general finite-dimensional Lie algebra the standard definition of a Cartan subalgebra is a nilpotent self-normalising subalgebra, which exists, but its ``eigenvector'' property (simultaneous diagonalisation of the adjoint action) is a theorem for semisimple $L$. The lecture's use is for the simple $L_i$. Not said in class. \ts{14}{1}{43:50}
\end{coursenote}

An important observation. Seen as pure notation, $\lambda_\alpha(h)$ is ``the $\alpha$-th eigenvalue for a given $h$''; but the left side $[h,E_\alpha]=\mathrm{ad}_h E_\alpha$ is linear in $h$ (the bracket is linear in its first entry), hence so is the eigenvalue: $\lambda_\alpha(3h)=3\lambda_\alpha(h)$ and $\lambda_\alpha(h+h')=\lambda_\alpha(h)+\lambda_\alpha(h')$. So $\lambda_\alpha$, without the $h$, is a \emph{linear map} $H\to\C$, an element of the dual space $H^*$. It matters whether one writes the $h$ (then it is a complex number) or leaves it out (then it is a linear map) \ts{14}{1}{45:58} \ts{14}{1}{46:20} \ts{14}{1}{46:42} \ts{14}{1}{47:03} \ts{14}{1}{47:24}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: roots and root set]\label{def:cl-roots}
The linear maps $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{d-m}\in H^*$ are called the \textbf{roots} of the Lie algebra (traditional terminology). The collection of all roots is the \textbf{root set} $\Phi\subset H^*$ (``root set'' rather than ``root space'': it is just a set) \ts{14}{1}{47:46} \ts{14}{1}{48:07} \ts{14}{1}{48:31} \ts{14}{1}{48:51} \ts{14}{1}{49:12}.
\end{definition}
Remarks, the roots being the eigenvalues, now as linear functions, of the $\mathrm{ad}_h$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] If $\lambda$ is a root, then $-\lambda$ is a root: \emph{the roots come in pairs}. The reason given: $\mathrm{ad}_h$ is antisymmetric with respect to the Killing form (\cref{prop:cl-ad-antisym}), and a symmetric or antisymmetric endomorphism with respect to a non-degenerate but not positive definite form is not in general diagonalisable; but over the complex numbers the signature does not matter (it is only defined for symmetric forms on real vector spaces) and one can bring it to a block form with $\pm i$, so eigenvalues come in pairs \ts{14}{1}{49:33} \ts{14}{1}{49:54} \ts{14}{1}{50:15} \ts{14}{1}{50:38} \ts{14}{1}{50:58} \ts{14}{1}{51:19} \ts{14}{1}{51:39} \ts{14}{1}{51:59};
  \item[(b)] the roots $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{d-m}$ are in general \emph{not linearly independent} (one can show this) \ts{14}{1}{52:20}.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{coursenote}[The pairing of roots]
The lecture's justification (block-diagonal form with $\pm i$) is an indication, not a proof. The standard argument, not said in class: if $E_\lambda$ is a root vector then $K(E_\lambda,E_\mu)=0$ unless $\mu=-\lambda$ (from the antisymmetry of $\mathrm{ad}_h$: $(\lambda+\mu)(h)K(E_\lambda,E_\mu)=0$ for all $h$), so non-degeneracy of $K$ forces $-\lambda$ to be a root. \ts{14}{1}{49:33}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Fundamental roots}\label{sec:cl-fundamental}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: set of fundamental roots]\label{def:cl-fundamental}
A \textbf{set of fundamental roots} is a subset $\Pi\subseteq\Phi$ of the root set such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] $\Pi$ is \textbf{linearly independent} (as a subset of the vector space $H^*$);
  \item[(b)] every root $\lambda\in\Phi$ can be written, for suitable $\varepsilon=\pm1$ (depending on $\lambda$) and non-negative integers $n_i\in\mathbb N_0$, as
  \[
    \lambda=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{f}n_i\,\pi_i,\qquad\Pi=\{\pi_1,\dots,\pi_f\}.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\ts{14}{1}{52:41} \ts{14}{1}{53:02} \ts{14}{1}{53:25} \ts{14}{1}{53:46} \ts{14}{1}{54:08} \ts{14}{1}{54:29} \ts{14}{1}{54:52} \ts{14}{1}{55:12} \ts{14}{1}{55:33} \ts{14}{1}{55:55}.
\end{definition}
The overall sign $\varepsilon$ must be allowed because if a root is generated with non-negative integers, the negative root (which is always also a root) cannot be; and one cannot simply allow arbitrary integers, since then the first coefficient could be positive and the second negative: ``either all positive or all negative''. Of any pair $\{\lambda,-\lambda\}$ one can only choose one into $\Pi$, since they are linearly dependent  \ts{14}{1}{56:16} \ts{14}{1}{56:36} \ts{14}{1}{56:57}. It is a fact that such a $\Pi\subseteq\Phi$ \emph{can always be found}  \ts{14}{1}{57:18}.
\begin{theorem}[$\Pi$ spans $H^*$]\label{thm:cl-span}
The \emph{complex} span of the fundamental roots is the dual of the Cartan subalgebra: $\mathrm{span}_\C\Pi=H^*$  \ts{14}{1}{57:40} \ts{14}{1}{58:01}.
\end{theorem}
So the roots, a subset of $H^*$ which is not a basis, contain redundant information; one chooses a basis from them and already spans $H^*$. As always with a basis, $\Pi$ is \emph{not unique}: two mathematicians may choose different sets of fundamental roots, always taken from the whole root set $\Phi$  \ts{14}{1}{58:21} \ts{14}{1}{58:42}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the real span $H^*_{\R}$]\label{def:cl-hr}
$H^*_{\R}:=\mathrm{span}_{\R}\Pi$: only \emph{real} linear combinations of the fundamental roots (the complex ones would give all of $H^*$) \ts{14}{1}{59:04} \ts{14}{1}{59:27} \ts{14}{1}{59:48}.
\end{definition}
So one has the chain of sets (the lecture: ``all three ways of combining the fundamental roots play a role''):
\[
  \Phi\ \subseteq\ \mathrm{``}\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb Z}^{\pm}\Pi\mathrm{''}\ \subseteq\ \mathrm{span}_{\R}\Pi=H^*_{\R}\ \subseteq\ \mathrm{span}_{\C}\Pi=H^*,
\]
where the quotation marks denote the funny span with non-negative integer coefficients and one overall sign, which gives back exactly the roots (note the lecture's own correction: the span with integer coefficients is \emph{bigger} than $\Phi$, since not every such combination is a root; (b) only says that every root is of that form) \ts{14}{1}{1:00:09} \ts{14}{1}{1:00:31} \ts{14}{1}{1:00:52} \ts{14}{1}{1:01:13} \ts{14}{1}{1:01:35} \ts{14}{1}{1:01:56} \ts{14}{1}{1:10:31} \ts{14}{1}{1:10:52}.

\subsection{The dual Killing form}\label{sec:cl-dual}
Second definition: one wants the Killing form not on $L$ but on the dual of $H$ \ts{14}{1}{1:02:17} \ts{14}{1}{1:02:38}. One can restrict $K$ to $H\times H\to\C$ by plugging in only elements of $H$ (the lecture corrects itself: $K$ goes into the complex numbers, not into $H$) \ts{14}{1}{1:02:58} \ts{14}{1}{1:03:19}. What is wanted is the \emph{dual} of the restricted Killing form, a map $K^*\colon H^*\times H^*\to\C$: an inner product on vectors induces one on covectors if it is non-degenerate, via the inverse matrix \ts{14}{1}{1:03:39} \ts{14}{1}{1:04:00}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: dual Killing form]\label{def:cl-dual-killing}
Let $I\colon H\to H^*$, $I(h):=K(h,\cdot\,)$, the canonical isomorphism (an element of $H^*$, since another $h'$ can be put in the open slot and gives a complex number). $I$ has an inverse because $K$ is non-degenerate (on $H$) when $L$ is semisimple; ``simple is enough''. The \textbf{dual Killing form} is
\[
  K^*(\mu,\nu):=K\bigl(I^{-1}(\mu),I^{-1}(\nu)\bigr),\qquad\mu,\nu\in H^*.
\]
In components, if $K$ has indices $K_{ij}$ then $K^*$ has the inverse matrix $K^{ij}$, with $K^{ij}K_{jk}=\delta^i{}_k$ (``it looks terrible''; this is the coordinate-free version) \ts{14}{1}{1:04:20} \ts{14}{1}{1:04:41} \ts{14}{1}{1:05:02} \ts{14}{1}{1:05:23} \ts{14}{1}{1:05:43} \ts{14}{1}{1:06:04}.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[$K^*$ is a real inner product on $H^*_{\R}$]\label{thm:cl-positive}
Restricted to $H^*_{\R}$, the dual Killing form is real-valued, bilinear and \emph{positive definite}: for $\alpha\in H^*_{\R}$, $K^*(\alpha,\alpha)\ge0$, and $K^*(\alpha,\alpha)=0$ iff $\alpha=0$ \ts{14}{1}{1:06:32} \ts{14}{1}{1:06:54} \ts{14}{1}{1:07:14}.
\end{theorem}
(Quoted without proof.) This is remarkable: before one only knew that $K$ is non-degenerate on a complex vector space, now there is a restriction to a real vector space on which it is a real inner product. On $H^*_{\R}$ one can therefore calculate \textbf{lengths and angles}, in particular of the fundamental roots, which span it; and since all the roots lie in $H^*_{\R}$ one can calculate lengths and angles between \emph{any} roots; but one will need it on the fundamental roots \ts{14}{1}{1:07:36} \ts{14}{1}{1:07:59} \ts{14}{1}{1:08:19} \ts{14}{1}{1:08:45} \ts{14}{1}{1:09:07}.

\section{The Weyl group}\label{sec:cl-weyl}

One more thing is needed \ts{14}{1}{1:09:29}. The $\pi_i$ were chosen to span $H^*$ and form a subset of $\Phi$, fine; but one may worry whether, with only this information, \emph{all the other roots} can be recovered. The answer is yes, and the whole classification of Lie algebras comes from understanding the fundamental roots: for a simple algebra, if one understands this little object (not even the whole $L$, only the dual of the Cartan subalgebra) one recovers everything. Note that the span with non-negative integer coefficients and one sign is bigger than $\Phi$: the question is how to recover \emph{precisely} $\Phi$ from a given $\Pi$  \ts{14}{1}{1:09:50} \ts{14}{1}{1:10:10} \ts{14}{1}{1:10:31} \ts{14}{1}{1:10:52} \ts{14}{1}{1:11:13} \ts{14}{1}{1:11:35} \ts{14}{1}{1:11:55}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: Weyl transformation and Weyl group]\label{def:cl-weyl}
For $\lambda\in\Phi$ define the map $s_\lambda\colon H^*_{\R}\to H^*_{\R}$ by
\[
  s_\lambda(\mu):=\mu-2\,\frac{K^*(\lambda,\mu)}{K^*(\lambda,\lambda)}\,\lambda .
\]
It is linear in $\mu$ (one $\mu$ in a slot of a bilinear map) and highly \emph{non}linear in $\lambda$: under a real rescaling $\lambda\to c\lambda$ the factors cancel ($c$ in the numerator, $c^2$ in the denominator, and another $c$ from the last factor), so only the ray matters (a warning, as with states in quantum mechanics, where one also considers rays but with complex rescalings). Each $s_\lambda$ is called a \textbf{Weyl transformation}. The \textbf{Weyl group} $W$ is the group generated by all $s_\lambda$, $\lambda\in\Phi$, with the group operation the composition of maps  \ts{14}{1}{1:12:16} \ts{14}{1}{1:12:38} \ts{14}{1}{1:12:59} \ts{14}{1}{1:13:19} \ts{14}{1}{1:13:40} \ts{14}{1}{1:14:01} \ts{14}{1}{1:14:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:14:42} \ts{14}{1}{1:15:10}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[What $s_\lambda$ is]
Not said in class: $s_\lambda$ is the \emph{reflection} in the hyperplane orthogonal to $\lambda$ with respect to $K^*$ (it sends $\lambda\mapsto-\lambda$ and fixes the orthogonal complement). This also shows $s_\lambda^2=\mathrm{id}$ and that $s_\lambda$ preserves $K^*$. \ts{14}{1}{1:12:16}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{theorem}[Weyl group, quoted]\label{thm:cl-weyl}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] $W$ is \emph{generated by the fundamental roots}: every $w\in W$ can be written as a finite composition $w=s_{\pi_1}\circ s_{\pi_2}\circ\dots\circ s_{\pi_n}$ with $\pi_k\in\Pi$.
  \item[(ii)] Every root can be produced from a fundamental root by an element of the Weyl group: for every $\lambda\in\Phi$ there exist $w\in W$ and $\pi\in\Pi$ with $\lambda=w(\pi)$.
  \item[(iii)] The Weyl group \emph{permutes} the roots: for all $w\in W$ and $\lambda\in\Phi$, $w(\lambda)\in\Phi$.
\end{enumerate}
\ts{14}{1}{1:15:10} \ts{14}{1}{1:15:31} \ts{14}{1}{1:15:52} \ts{14}{1}{1:16:14} \ts{14}{1}{1:16:35} \ts{14}{1}{1:16:55} \ts{14}{1}{1:17:16} \ts{14}{1}{1:17:38} \ts{14}{1}{1:18:01} \ts{14}{1}{1:18:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:19:04} \ts{14}{1}{1:19:28} \ts{14}{1}{1:19:49}.
\end{theorem}
By (iii) the Weyl transformations, which act on all of $H^*_{\R}$ and might take one out of $\Phi$, do not: they just permute the roots. So if one knows the fundamental roots one knows the whole Weyl group, and then every root, produced by the Weyl group from a fundamental one: the fundamental roots determine, in this two-step process, the whole set of roots, which is why they and the Weyl group are so important \ts{14}{1}{1:18:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:18:44} \ts{14}{1}{1:20:09}.
A student remarks that the form used in $s_\lambda$ is the \emph{dual} Killing form in both places; the lecturer confirms (``from the dual Killing form, because that can eat these guys'')  \ts{14}{1}{1:20:30}.

\section{The Cartan matrix and the bond numbers}\label{sec:cl-cartan-matrix}

``Now showdown''  \ts{14}{1}{1:20:53}. Tie everything together: apply the Weyl transformation of one fundamental root to another. For $\pi_i,\pi_j\in\Pi$, by the definition,
\[
  s_{\pi_i}(\pi_j)=\pi_j-2\,\frac{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)}{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)}\,\pi_i .
\]
By \cref{thm:cl-weyl}(iii) this is a root. On the other hand it is an expansion of a root in terms of two fundamental roots, and by \cref{def:cl-fundamental}(b) all its coefficients (with respect to the linearly independent $\pi$'s) have the same sign: the coefficient of $\pi_j$ is $1>0$, so the coefficient of $\pi_i$ must be a non-negative integer too, i.e.
\[
  -2\,\frac{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)}{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)}\in\mathbb N_0\qquad(i\neq j)
\]
 \ts{14}{1}{1:21:14} \ts{14}{1}{1:21:35} \ts{14}{1}{1:21:55} \ts{14}{1}{1:22:16} \ts{14}{1}{1:22:37} \ts{14}{1}{1:22:57} \ts{14}{1}{1:23:18} \ts{14}{1}{1:23:39} \ts{14}{1}{1:24:00} \ts{14}{1}{1:24:21}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: Cartan matrix]\label{def:cl-cartan-matrix}
The \textbf{Cartan matrix} of $\Pi$ has entries
\[
  C_{ij}:=2\,\frac{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)}{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)}.
\]
(Not the structure constants: a new $C$ with two indices; the lecture first overlooked the sign.) Thus $C_{ii}=2$: the diagonal is $2,2,\dots,2$, and the off-diagonal entries are \emph{non-positive integers}, $C_{ij}\in\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}$ for $i\ne j$  \ts{14}{1}{1:24:41} \ts{14}{1}{1:25:02} \ts{14}{1}{1:25:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:26:29} \ts{14}{1}{1:26:49}.
\end{definition}
It is \emph{not symmetric}: the numerator is symmetric ($K^*$ is symmetric), but the denominator contains $K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)$, and exchanging $i\leftrightarrow j$ puts in $K^*(\pi_j,\pi_j)$, which may be different. (``Early this morning I thought the same'': one must look at the whole expression) \ts{14}{1}{1:25:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:25:46} \ts{14}{1}{1:26:07}.
\begin{coursenote}[A slip, a student question and the resolution]
At this point the lecture runs into a puzzle: the argument above makes $-C_{ij}$ a non-negative integer, ``for $i=j$ it would then give $-2$''. A student helps: the conclusion (non-positive integer) is only valid for $i\ne j$, because for $i=j$ the two terms $\pi_j$ and $-C_{jj}\pi_j$ combine into one multiple of the same fundamental root and there is no sign constraint; for $i=j$ the value is simply $C_{ii}=2$. ``No contradiction anywhere''. Some further inconclusive talk of antisymmetry of $K$ and positive definiteness of $K^*$ in the middle of this episode is not reproduced. \ts{14}{1}{1:27:12} \ts{14}{1}{1:27:33} \ts{14}{1}{1:27:53} \ts{14}{1}{1:28:14} \ts{14}{1}{1:28:34} \ts{14}{1}{1:28:55} \ts{14}{1}{1:29:15} \ts{14}{1}{1:29:38} \ts{14}{1}{1:29:58} \ts{14}{1}{1:30:18} \ts{14}{1}{1:30:39} \ts{14}{1}{1:31:00} \ts{14}{1}{1:31:22} \ts{14}{1}{1:31:42}
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: bond number]\label{def:cl-bond}
The \textbf{bond number} between $\pi_i$ and $\pi_j$ is $n_{ij}:=C_{ij}\,C_{ji}$ (no summation). Then
\[
  n_{ij}=4\,\frac{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)\,K^*(\pi_j,\pi_i)}{K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)\,K^*(\pi_j,\pi_j)}=4\cos^2\theta_{ij},
\]
with $\theta_{ij}$ the angle between $\pi_i$ and $\pi_j$ in the real inner product $K^*$  \ts{14}{1}{1:32:02} \ts{14}{1}{1:32:23} \ts{14}{1}{1:32:43}.
\end{definition}
Since $\cos^2$ lies between $0$ and $1$, one has $0\le n_{ij}\le4$, and for $i\ne j$ strictly $<4$ (the two roots are linearly independent, so not parallel); moreover, for $i\ne j$, $n_{ij}$ is the product of two non-positive integers. Hence $n_{ij}\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ \ts{14}{1}{1:33:03} \ts{14}{1}{1:33:24} \ts{14}{1}{1:33:44} \ts{14}{1}{1:34:05} \ts{14}{1}{1:34:29}. The possible pairs $(C_{ij},C_{ji})$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n_{ij}=0$: $(0,0)$, necessarily both zero at once (the numerator is the same);
  \item $n_{ij}=1$: $(-1,-1)$;
  \item $n_{ij}=2$: $(-1,-2)$ or $(-2,-1)$;
  \item $n_{ij}=3$: $(-1,-3)$ or $(-3,-1)$.
\end{itemize}
\ts{14}{1}{1:34:49} \ts{14}{1}{1:35:11} \ts{14}{1}{1:35:32} \ts{14}{1}{1:35:52}. In the cases $2$ and $3$ one derives (``a two-liner, I leave it to you'') that either $K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)<K^*(\pi_j,\pi_j)$ or the other way round: the two roots have different lengths, and \emph{which one is longer} is the extra information in the Cartan matrix \ts{14}{1}{1:36:13} \ts{14}{1}{1:36:33}.
\begin{exercise}[Lengths from the Cartan matrix]\label{ex:cl-lengths}
\emph{Not said in class: the ``two-liner''.} Since $C_{ij}/C_{ji}=K^*(\pi_j,\pi_j)/K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)$, show that for $n_{ij}=2$ the squared lengths are in ratio $2:1$ and for $n_{ij}=3$ in ratio $3:1$; for $n_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$ the ratio is $1$ if $\pi_i,\pi_j$ are joined, and unconstrained if $n_{ij}=0$. (\cref{exam:ch14}.)
\end{exercise}

\section{Dynkin diagrams and the classification}\label{sec:cl-dynkin}

From the Cartan matrix one draws the diagram .
\begin{definition}[New concepts: Dynkin diagram]\label{def:cl-dynkin}
The \textbf{Dynkin diagram} of a set of fundamental roots is drawn by three rules:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item for every fundamental root $\pi_i$ draw a circle (a node);
  \item between the circles for $\pi_i$ and $\pi_j$ draw $n_{ij}$ lines (none, one, two or three);
  \item if there are two or three lines, there are two subcases (one root longer than the other, or the reverse): put a relation sign on the lines (``like an arrow''), pointing to the shorter root; ``the length of this root is bigger than that one''.
\end{enumerate}
\ts{14}{1}{1:36:58} \ts{14}{1}{1:37:20} \ts{14}{1}{1:37:41} \ts{14}{1}{1:38:02} \ts{14}{1}{1:38:24} \ts{14}{1}{1:38:45}.
\end{definition}
The number of lines between two nodes records the angle between the roots (through $n_{ij}=4\cos^2\theta_{ij}$), and the arrow their relative lengths; in diagrams with only single lines (or none) all the roots have the same length, and only with double or triple bonds do the lengths jump \ts{14}{1}{1:44:31} \ts{14}{1}{1:44:51}.

\end{theorem}

\end{itemize}
These are the \textbf{four classical series}; ``actually sequences, but they are called series'' \ts{14}{1}{1:43:06}. Then five \textbf{exceptional} algebras, which do not go on in a series:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$: chains of single lines with one extra node attached (six, seven, eight nodes in total). Some people try to base physics entirely on the $E_8$ group  \ts{14}{1}{1:43:27} \ts{14}{1}{1:43:50};

\end{itemize}

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the Levi decomposition. What is a solvable Lie algebra, an ideal, a simple Lie algebra? What is the difference between a direct and a semidirect sum of Lie algebras?
  \item Why does one only classify the simple Lie algebras? What is a semisimple one?
  \item Define $\mathrm{ad}_h$, the structure constants and the Killing form; give $(\mathrm{ad}_{E_i})^j{}_k$ and $K_{ij}$ in components. Why is $K$ symmetric? How is semisimplicity decided?
  \item Define a Cartan subalgebra. What is a root and why does it live in $H^*$? Why do roots come in pairs?
  \item Define a set of fundamental roots and say what it spans. Is it unique?
  \item Define the dual Killing form and say why $H^*_{\R}$ matters (positive definiteness).
  \item Define the Weyl transformation and the Weyl group. State the three properties.
  \item Why is $-2K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)/K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)$ a non-negative integer for $i\ne j$? Define the Cartan matrix; is it symmetric?
  \item Define the bond number; why is it $0,1,2$ or $3$? List the possible pairs $(C_{ij},C_{ji})$.
  \item Draw the Dynkin diagrams $A_3$, $B_2$, $D_4$, $G_2$ and name the exceptional ones. What do the lines and the arrow mean?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Complex Lie algebra, Levi decomposition, solvable, ideal, simple, direct and semidirect sums (\cref{thm:cl-levi,def:cl-solvable,def:cl-simple,def:cl-sums}).
  \item Adjoint map, structure constants, Killing form, Cartan's criterion (\cref{def:cl-ad,def:cl-structure,def:cl-killing,thm:cl-cartan-criterion,prop:cl-components,prop:cl-ad-antisym}).
  \item Cartan subalgebra, roots, fundamental roots, $H^*_{\R}$, dual Killing form (\cref{def:cl-cartan,thm:cl-cartan,def:cl-roots,def:cl-fundamental,thm:cl-span,def:cl-hr,def:cl-dual-killing,thm:cl-positive}).
  \item Weyl group (\cref{def:cl-weyl,thm:cl-weyl}); Cartan matrix and bond numbers (\cref{def:cl-cartan-matrix,def:cl-bond}).
  \item Dynkin diagrams and the classification (\cref{def:cl-dynkin,thm:cl-classification}).
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
