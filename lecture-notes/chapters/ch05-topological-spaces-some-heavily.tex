\section{A zoo of topological properties}\label{sec:iv-zoo}

There is a whole zoo of topological properties that are heavily used: \emph{separation properties} (written down below),
\emph{connectedness}, \emph{path connectedness}, \emph{simple connectedness}, the \emph{Hausdorff} property,
\emph{compactness}, \emph{paracompactness}, and so on, a very long list \ts{5}{1}{0:04}\ts{5}{1}{0:24}. There is even a book of
topological properties in which, for many spaces, hundreds of properties are listed with whether the space has them or not;
but no such set is sufficient: one always finds two spaces that are not homeomorphic and yet share all of them
\ts{5}{1}{0:24}\ts{5}{1}{0:46}. Apart from possibly serving a future classification, these properties are important in themselves
\ts{5}{1}{0:46}\ts{5}{1}{1:06}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: topological property, topological invariant]\label{def:iv-invariant}
A property of a topological space defined solely in terms of its topology is a \textbf{topological property}. Two homeomorphic spaces
(\cref{def:to-homeo}) share all of them, which is why one also calls them \textbf{topological invariants}: invariant under
homeomorphism \ts{5}{1}{15:44}\ts{5}{1}{16:04}\ts{5}{1}{54:15}.
\end{definition}
Often one checks a property by finding a homeomorphism to a space of which one already knows it \ts{5}{1}{16:25}. As said at the end of the lecture:
no collection of such invariants is known that would settle homeomorphism (compare \cref{sec:iv-end}). It is not that one should be afraid of
any topological property: one looks up the definition, it is always defined through the open sets \ts{5}{1}{1:56:00}\ts{5}{1}{1:56:20}.

For many of the theorems below the proofs are not presented, as they are rather involved. In a physics course it is important to understand the
notions precisely, know where they are applied, get a feeling for how they connect, and how to construct new spaces with such properties from old
ones: at the end one has a toolbox of spaces known to be connected, compact, Hausdorff, and composes them. The proofs can be looked up in a topology
textbook; there is no time to spend the whole semester on topology, but an overview is fully sufficient for most physical applications
\ts{5}{1}{42:55}\ts{5}{1}{43:16}\ts{5}{1}{43:36}\ts{5}{1}{43:57}\ts{5}{1}{44:17}.

\section{Separation properties}\label{sec:iv-sep}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $T_1$]\label{def:iv-T1}
A topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ is called $\mathbf{T_1}$ if for any two distinct points $p\ne q$ there exists an open neighbourhood
$U\in\mathcal O$ of $p$ that does not contain $q$, i.e.\ $\{q\}\cap U=\emptyset$ \ts{5}{1}{1:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:34}\ts{5}{1}{1:56}\ts{5}{1}{2:17}\ts{5}{1}{2:38}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Board and captions]
The transcript has ``$p$ does not lie in $U$'' and ``$\{p\}$ does not intersect $U$''. With $U$ a neighbourhood of $p$ that cannot be
meant; the condition is on $q$, as written here. (Reconstructed from context.)
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $T_2$, Hausdorff]\label{def:iv-T2}
A topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ is called $\mathbf{T_2}$ or \textbf{Hausdorff} if for any two distinct points $p\ne q$ there exist an open
neighbourhood $U$ of $p$ and an open neighbourhood $V$ of $q$ with $U\cap V=\emptyset$ \ts{5}{1}{2:38}\ts{5}{1}{3:01}\ts{5}{1}{3:43}\ts{5}{1}{4:04}.
\end{definition}
Hausdorff actually formulated the topological axioms in terms of neighbourhoods, fully equivalent to the three conditions written down in
\cref{def:to-topology}; but he had one additional axiom, the \emph{Hausdorff axiom}. Since many interesting topological spaces do not have that
property, it was removed from the general definition of topology; but for many spaces, as Hausdorff was very clever to see, one wants the
additional property: hence $T_2$ \ts{5}{1}{3:01}\ts{5}{1}{3:22}\ts{5}{1}{3:43}.

Intuitively: take $\R^2$ with the standard topology; for two distinct points one always finds an open set around each such that the two do not
intersect, a separation axiom \ts{5}{1}{4:04}\ts{5}{1}{4:24}. $T_1$ asks only that around $p$ there is an open set not containing $q$, one cloud instead of two: this is
\emph{weaker} \ts{5}{1}{4:24}\ts{5}{1}{4:46}\ts{5}{1}{5:06}.

\begin{example}[Examples of $T_1$ and $T_2$]\label{ex:iv-T}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\R^d$ with the standard topology is $T_2$, hence $T_1$; $T_1$ is weaker than $T_2$ \ts{5}{1}{5:06}\ts{5}{1}{5:26}.
  \item The \textbf{Zariski topology}, which appears in algebraic geometry, is $T_1$ but not $T_2$; it is a very important topology there
        and has driven the subject forward. It is on the problem sheet \ts{5}{1}{5:26}\ts{5}{1}{5:47}\ts{5}{1}{6:08}.
  \item The chaotic topology (\cref{ex:to-chaotic}) on a set with at least two points is not even $T_1$: given $p\ne q$, the only open set containing
        $p$ is $M$, which contains $q$. This is on the problem sheet \ts{5}{1}{7:54}\ts{5}{1}{8:15}\ts{5}{1}{8:36}.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}
\begin{exercise}[Zariski]\label{ex:iv-zariski}
Look up the Zariski topology (it is not defined in class) and show that it is $T_1$ but not $T_2$ (problem sheet) \ts{5}{1}{5:47}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{remark}[Further separation axioms]\label{rem:iv-sep}
There are stronger and stronger separation axioms; the original one by Hausdorff is $T_2$, but there are situations in which one wants one and
others in which one wants another \ts{5}{1}{6:08}\ts{5}{1}{6:51}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathbf{T_{2\frac12}}$ (``I like this''): for two distinct points there is a \emph{closed} neighbourhood of one of them that does not
        meet the other point \ts{5}{1}{6:08}\ts{5}{1}{6:31} [?: as said, a closed neighbourhood around one point; the usual $T_{2\frac12}$ asks closed
        neighbourhoods of both points that are disjoint].
  \item $\mathbf{T_3}$: not two points, but a closed set and a point; around the closed set an open set that does not meet the point
        \ts{5}{1}{6:51}\ts{5}{1}{7:12} [?: the usual statement asks for disjoint open neighbourhoods of the point and of the closed set].
  \item then $T_4$ ``and whatnot''. They all have applications in some branch of mathematics \ts{5}{1}{6:31}\ts{5}{1}{7:12}.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of limits]\label{thm:iv-limit}
In a Hausdorff space every convergent sequence has a unique limit point \ts{5}{1}{7:12}\ts{5}{1}{7:33}.
\end{theorem}
Proof: on the problem sheet. It is a very intuitive notion: a sequence converges to a point, so it does not converge to another one
\ts{5}{1}{7:12}\ts{5}{1}{7:33}. For the chaotic topology it fails, since every sequence converges against every point (\cref{ex:to-conv-chaotic}): so the
chaotic topology cannot be Hausdorff, which is a counterexample to $T_2$ \ts{5}{1}{7:33}\ts{5}{1}{7:54}\ts{5}{1}{8:15}.
\begin{exercise}[Limits in Hausdorff spaces]\label{ex:iv-limit}
Prove \cref{thm:iv-limit} (problem sheet) \ts{5}{1}{7:12}. The long version is in \cref{exam:ch05}.
\end{exercise}

As already said, no complete list of properties exists such that two spaces differing in at least one of them are known to be non-homeomorphic;
yet it is convenient to add to the axioms of a general topological space additional special properties, sometimes to prove theorems that cannot
be proved without them, sometimes to understand the situation better \ts{5}{1}{8:36}\ts{5}{1}{8:57}\ts{5}{1}{9:18}.

\section{Compactness}\label{sec:iv-compact}

The next pair of properties: compactness and paracompactness, two very heavily used ones \ts{5}{1}{9:18}\ts{5}{1}{9:38}. If one wants to prove a theorem in a setting
that has a topological space as underlying structure, one very often first tackles the \emph{compact} case, because proofs are usually simpler there; once
the compact case is understood one tries to extend the theorem to the non-compact case, which is sometimes not possible but sometimes is \ts{5}{1}{10:00}\ts{5}{1}{10:21}.
Paracompactness is a much weaker notion, so weak that it is very difficult to find topological spaces appearing naturally that are not paracompact
(there is a theorem to that effect); for manifold theory, our later concern, it is a fundamental assumption that we will always make, like the Hausdorff
assumption \ts{5}{1}{10:21}\ts{5}{1}{10:42}\ts{5}{1}{11:02}. Hausdorff plus paracompactness gives a very convenient property, partitions of unity
(\cref{sec:iv-pou}) \ts{5}{1}{11:02}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: open cover, subcover, compact]\label{def:iv-compact}
Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a topological space.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A subset $C\subseteq\mathcal O$ (a collection of open sets) is an \textbf{open cover} of $M$ if $\bigcup C=M$
        \ts{5}{1}{11:45}\ts{5}{1}{12:05}\ts{5}{1}{12:27}.
  \item A \textbf{subcover} of $C$ is a subset $\tilde C\subseteq C$ that is itself a cover: one takes open sets out of the cover but still has a
        cover. It is a \textbf{finite subcover} if $\tilde C$ is finite \ts{5}{1}{12:51}\ts{5}{1}{13:11}\ts{5}{1}{13:52}\ts{5}{1}{14:17}\ts{5}{1}{14:39}.
  \item $(M,\mathcal O)$ is \textbf{compact} if every open cover has a finite subcover \ts{5}{1}{11:23}\ts{5}{1}{14:39}.
  \item A subset $N\subseteq M$ is \textbf{compact} if $N$ with the induced topology $\mathcal O|_N$ (\cref{def:to-induced}) is compact as a topological
        space in its own right \ts{5}{1}{14:39}\ts{5}{1}{15:01}\ts{5}{1}{15:23}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Board correction]
Schuller started writing the definition of a subcover with a condition of the form ``for every $U\in C$ there is a $\tilde U\in\tilde C$ with\dots'',
stopped (``that's already the refinement, that's too weak'') and replaced it with $\tilde C\subseteq C$; refinements come with paracompactness
(\cref{def:iv-paracompact}) \ts{5}{1}{13:11}\ts{5}{1}{13:32}\ts{5}{1}{13:52}.
\end{coursenote}
The cover and subcover business makes it manifest that compactness is a topological property \ts{5}{1}{15:44}. If $(M,\mathcal O)$ and $(N,\mathcal O')$ are
homeomorphic and one is compact, so is the other \ts{5}{1}{16:04}\ts{5}{1}{16:25}. It sometimes makes compactness hard to prove directly; hence the
following famous theorem \ts{5}{1}{16:45}.

\paragraph{Metric spaces.} In a \emph{metric space}, when talking about topology one always assumes the \emph{metric induced topology}
\ts{5}{1}{17:10}\ts{5}{1}{17:39}. There are different notions of metric in mathematics; here a \textbf{metric} is a map $d\colon M\times M\to[0,\infty)$ with
\ts{5}{1}{18:02}\ts{5}{1}{18:23}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] $d(m,n)=d(n,m)$: the distance from Paris to London is that from London to Paris \ts{5}{1}{18:23}\ts{5}{1}{18:43};
  \item[(b)] $d(m,n)\ge0$, with equality if and only if $m=n$: distinct points have non-zero distance \ts{5}{1}{18:43}\ts{5}{1}{19:04};
  \item[(c)] $d(m,n)\le d(m,l)+d(l,n)$, the triangle inequality: going from A to B and then to C is never shorter than going from A to C directly
        \ts{5}{1}{19:04}\ts{5}{1}{19:25}.
\end{enumerate}
The \textbf{metric induced topology} is defined very much like the standard topology on $\R^d$ (\cref{sec:to-standard}), with the ball of radius $r\in\R^+$
around a point $p$ now $B_r(p)=\{q\in M\mid d(q,p)<r\}$ \ts{5}{1}{19:47}\ts{5}{1}{20:08}\ts{5}{1}{20:29}\ts{5}{1}{20:50}.
(A student asks about the radius being in $\R^+$ rather than in $\R^+_0$: the notation is cryptic, it only means a number $>0$ \ts{5}{1}{23:55}\ts{5}{1}{24:15}.)
\begin{coursenote}[Which metric]
This is not the metric of differential geometry met later in the course, ``quite a different beast'': under certain circumstances one can extract such
a distance function from the latter on a manifold, but not the other way around \ts{5}{1}{19:25}\ts{5}{1}{19:47}.
\end{coursenote}
A norm gives such a metric through $d(p,q)=\|p-q\|$ on $\R^d$, where one can add and subtract, but there are spaces on which there is no addition, so the
difference of two points makes no sense and one needs this more resolved object \ts{5}{1}{20:50}\ts{5}{1}{21:10}.

\begin{example}[The French railroad metric]\label{ex:iv-sncf}
Take Paris and, say, Marseille, Calais and the North Sea coast. By train one goes from one place to another only via Paris, because every train goes via
Paris; the distance between two places not Paris is the sum of the distances to Paris \ts{5}{1}{21:10}\ts{5}{1}{21:31}\ts{5}{1}{21:52}\ts{5}{1}{22:12}. This satisfies the conditions of
a metric, but it is not induced by a norm: one would not find a norm reproducing it if the world, as far as France is concerned, were a plane
\ts{5}{1}{22:12}\ts{5}{1}{22:33}\ts{5}{1}{22:53}. So a metric is more general than what can be constructed from a norm, but it still gives open balls and an
induced topology.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}[New concepts: bounded subset]
A subset of a metric space is \textbf{bounded} if there is a number $R$ such that all its points lie within a ball of radius $R$ (a finite number)
\ts{5}{1}{22:53}\ts{5}{1}{23:13}.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Heine--Borel]\label{thm:iv-HB}
In $\R^d$ with the standard (metric induced) topology every closed and bounded subset is compact. As stated in class: in a metric space $(M,d)$ with its
induced topology, every closed and bounded subset is compact \ts{5}{1}{16:45}\ts{5}{1}{17:39}\ts{5}{1}{23:34}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{coursenote}[Correction: only for $\R^d$]
The statement as made in class (every closed and bounded subset of an arbitrary metric space is compact) is not true in general: an infinite set with
the metric $d(p,q)=1$ for $p\ne q$ is closed and bounded in itself, but the cover by the one-point sets (open) has no finite subcover. The theorem holds for
$\R^d$ with its Euclidean metric, which is what is used later. (Not said in class; the lecture calls it ``the theorem of H.\ Borel'', here Heine--Borel.)
\end{coursenote}
In many circumstances one has a metric space, so for a closed and bounded subset it is easy to decide that it is compact \ts{5}{1}{24:15}\ts{5}{1}{24:36}.

\begin{example}[Examples of compact and non-compact sets]\label{ex:iv-compact-ex}
Compact and non-compact subsets of $\R$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $[0,1]$ is compact as a subset of the real line, which is a metric space with $d(p,q)=|p-q|$, by Heine--Borel; and as a topological space with the
        topology induced from $\R$ \ts{5}{1}{24:36}.
  \item $\R$ is not compact: it is usually easy to see that a space is not compact, since one has just to construct one cover without a finite
        subcover \ts{5}{1}{25:37}\ts{5}{1}{26:02}. Take a cover of the real line by open intervals, one around each integer (for instance
        $C=\{(n-1,n+1)\mid n\in\mathbb Z\}$ [?: the picture on the board is not in the captions]); together they cover the line, and the set $C$ has infinitely many elements
        \ts{5}{1}{26:27}\ts{5}{1}{26:54}\ts{5}{1}{27:16}. A finite subcover would need one to take elements away, but if one takes only one away the cover
        property is violated; so not even an infinite proper subset is still a cover, much less a finite one \ts{5}{1}{27:16}\ts{5}{1}{27:37}.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}
\begin{theorem}[Products of compact spaces]\label{thm:iv-compact-prod}
If $(M,\mathcal O_M)$ and $(N,\mathcal O_N)$ are compact, then $M\times N$ with the product topology (\cref{def:to-product}) is compact; hence compactness
is inherited by finite products \ts{5}{1}{28:01}.
\end{theorem}
(Proof not given.)

\section{Paracompactness}\label{sec:iv-paracompact}

Compactness is a very strong property and sometimes simply does not hold; but there is a much weaker notion \ts{5}{1}{29:03}\ts{5}{1}{29:25}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: refinement, locally finite, paracompact]\label{def:iv-paracompact}
Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a topological space. (Covers are always open covers here; ``cover'' without ``open'' also means open cover.)
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An \textbf{open refinement} of an open cover $C$ is an open cover $\tilde C$ such that for every $U\in C$ [?: as heard, for every $\tilde U\in\tilde C$ there is a $U\in C$]
        with $\tilde U\subseteq U$ \ts{5}{1}{30:29}\ts{5}{1}{30:50}\ts{5}{1}{31:11}\ts{5}{1}{31:31}.
  \item An open refinement $\tilde C$ is \textbf{locally finite} if for every point $p\in M$ there is an open neighbourhood $U\in\mathcal O$ of $p$ such that
        $U\cap\tilde U\ne\emptyset$ for only finitely many $\tilde U\in\tilde C$ \ts{5}{1}{32:17}\ts{5}{1}{32:37}\ts{5}{1}{32:59}\ts{5}{1}{33:23}.
  \item $(M,\mathcal O)$ is \textbf{paracompact} if every open cover $C$ has an open refinement $\tilde C$ that is locally finite
        \ts{5}{1}{29:25}\ts{5}{1}{29:48}\ts{5}{1}{30:09}\ts{5}{1}{30:29}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Order of the quantifiers in a refinement]
As written on the board the sentence reads ``for every $U$ in the original cover there is a $\tilde U$ in the refinement with $\tilde U\subseteq U$''; that is the
wrong way round (it would make any cover with $M$ in it refined by one small set). The usual definition, and the intended one, is: every element of the refinement lies in
some element of the original cover. Reconstructed, hence the [?].
\end{coursenote}
So: if a locally finite refinement exists, one finds for each point a neighbourhood meeting only finitely many open sets of it \ts{5}{1}{33:23}\ts{5}{1}{33:43}. That
definition is quite a mouthful, but it needs to be made in this way in order to be useful \ts{5}{1}{33:43}. A subcover is a refinement, but not the other way around
\ts{5}{1}{31:31}\ts{5}{1}{31:52}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:iv-compact-para}
Compact implies paracompact \ts{5}{1}{33:43}\ts{5}{1}{34:04}\ts{5}{1}{34:24}.
\end{corollary}
Hence paracompactness is a weaker notion than compactness. (Proof, not written in class: a finite subcover is a locally finite open refinement.)

\begin{theorem}[Stone]\label{thm:iv-stone}
Every metrizable space is paracompact \ts{5}{1}{34:24}\ts{5}{1}{34:52}.
\end{theorem}
\emph{Metrizable} means that one can find a metric whose induced topology is the given one; which metric is not specified, since different metrics may give the
same topology, as the open balls can have different shapes (compare the TV screens of \cref{sec:to-standard}). The statement relies on the topology, so one has to make
the connection between metrizability and the topology in this way \ts{5}{1}{35:16}\ts{5}{1}{40:50}\ts{5}{1}{41:11}\ts{5}{1}{41:31}\ts{5}{1}{41:52}. This shows how generic paracompactness is;
there are spaces that are not metrizable, on which one cannot put a metric, but the example comes in a second and is very exotic and artificial. For most spaces in physics
paracompactness is not a strong condition, though it has a very important implication \ts{5}{1}{35:16}\ts{5}{1}{35:37}\ts{5}{1}{35:57}.
\begin{history}[Stone]
\emph{Checked.} A.~H.~Stone, ``Paracompactness and product spaces'', \emph{Bull.\ Amer.\ Math.\ Soc.} 54 (1948) 977--982: every metric space is paracompact. Dieudonn\'e introduced
paracompact spaces in 1944.
\end{history}

\begin{example}[Paracompact and non-paracompact spaces]\label{ex:iv-para}
One positive and one negative case:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\R^d$ with the standard topology is paracompact: it has a norm, which induces a metric and the standard topology, so it is metrizable, and Stone's theorem
        applies \ts{5}{1}{37:21}.
  \item The \textbf{Alexandrov long line} is not paracompact; a counterexample that gives a taste of how artificial such spaces are \ts{5}{1}{37:41}\ts{5}{1}{38:02}. The idea
        is only sketched: the real line is obtained by taking the interval $[0,1)$ (left closed, right open) and attaching another to it, and another, and another: a
        \emph{countable} product $\mathbb Z\times[0,1)$, a ruler put next to a ruler, which gives back the real line, and it is paracompact. The long line $L$ takes the same
        interval but \emph{uncountably} often, which uses the theory of ordinal numbers (the smallest uncountable ordinal number [?: as heard, ``the smallest such ordinal number that
        is not finite''; the first uncountable ordinal $\omega_1$ is meant]); with the induced topology one gets a non-paracompact space
        \ts{5}{1}{38:02}\ts{5}{1}{38:22}\ts{5}{1}{38:43}\ts{5}{1}{39:03}\ts{5}{1}{39:26}\ts{5}{1}{39:48}\ts{5}{1}{40:08}.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}
Other examples of non-paracompact spaces known to Schuller are also rather artificial: paracompactness is something rather natural \ts{5}{1}{40:08}\ts{5}{1}{40:29}. A student asks
how one can imagine the long line, given that for the real line one stacks rulers: Schuller's intuition escapes him a little too, it is already difficult in both directions to infinity;
maybe think of the old ideas of potential infinity; the difference is a countable versus an uncountable product, and that destroys paracompactness. The proofs need the first uncountable
ordinal $\Omega_1$ and so on \ts{5}{1}{41:52}.

\begin{remark}[Levels of structure on $\R^d$]\label{rem:iv-levels}
Whenever the topology on $\R^d$ is not specified, the standard one is meant (a standing convention, which he will probably forget to mention). $\R^d$ can be just a set of $d$-tuples; with
an addition it is an additive group; with scalar multiplication a vector space; with a norm a normed vector space; from the norm one makes a topology, so a topological space; one should always
think of the different levels of structure and remind oneself, in each construction, how many of them are really used. In physics one often says $\R^d$ has all of them at the same time, but we will not
always meet $\R^d$ with so much structure \ts{5}{1}{35:57}\ts{5}{1}{36:18}\ts{5}{1}{36:39}\ts{5}{1}{37:00}\ts{5}{1}{37:21}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Products with a compact factor]\label{thm:iv-para-prod}
Let $(M,\mathcal O_M)$ be paracompact and $(N,\mathcal O_N)$ compact (not just paracompact). Then $M\times N$ is paracompact \ts{5}{1}{44:17}\ts{5}{1}{44:37}.
\end{theorem}
Strictly, the Cartesian product is only a set; unless said otherwise, the Cartesian product of two sets that carry a topology is understood to carry the product topology
\ts{5}{1}{44:37}\ts{5}{1}{44:58}. Paracompactness thus does not behave so well under products: one of the factors must be compact; so to a paracompact space one can
attach only a finite number of compact spaces and be guaranteed to stay paracompact \ts{5}{1}{45:18}\ts{5}{1}{45:38}\ts{5}{1}{45:59}.

\section{Partitions of unity}\label{sec:iv-pou}

Again: compactness is abstractly defined through covers and finite subcovers, and intuitively well understood for metric spaces through Heine--Borel. For paracompactness there is even a
necessary and sufficient criterion, sometimes taken to be the definition, which will play an important role in manifold theory \ts{5}{1}{45:59}\ts{5}{1}{46:19}\ts{5}{1}{46:40}.

\begin{theorem}[Partitions of unity]\label{thm:iv-pou}
Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a \emph{Hausdorff} topological space (the statement is not valid for every paracompact space). Then $M$ is paracompact if and only if every open cover $C$ of $M$ admits a
\textbf{partition of unity subordinate to $C$} \ts{5}{1}{46:40}\ts{5}{1}{47:01}\ts{5}{1}{47:30}\ts{5}{1}{47:54}\ts{5}{1}{48:15}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{definition}[New concepts: partition of unity subordinate to a cover]\label{def:iv-pou}
A \textbf{partition of unity} subordinate to the cover $C$ is a set $\mathcal F$ of continuous functions $f\colon M\to[0,1]$ (with $[0,1]$ carrying the topology induced from $\R$, so that
continuity makes sense) such that \ts{5}{1}{48:15}\ts{5}{1}{48:36}\ts{5}{1}{49:01}\ts{5}{1}{49:23}\ts{5}{1}{49:44}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] for every $f\in\mathcal F$ there is $U\in C$ with: $f(p)\ne0\Rightarrow p\in U$; i.e.\ $f$ has support only on that set $U$ \ts{5}{1}{49:44}\ts{5}{1}{50:09}\ts{5}{1}{50:29}\ts{5}{1}{50:50};
  \item[(b)] for every $p\in M$ there is an open neighbourhood $V$ of $p$ such that only finitely many $f_1,\dots,f_n\in\mathcal F$ are non-zero on $V$ and these
        sum to one on $V$: $\sum_{i=1}^nf_i=1$ on $V$ \ts{5}{1}{50:50}\ts{5}{1}{51:14}\ts{5}{1}{51:38}\ts{5}{1}{51:59}\ts{5}{1}{52:19}\ts{5}{1}{52:41}\ts{5}{1}{53:05}\ts{5}{1}{53:29}\ts{5}{1}{53:49}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[A slip on the board]
Condition~(b) was first written with an intersection $V\cap U\ne\emptyset$ for only finitely many $U\in C$; Schuller stopped: that is already taken care of by the refinement, and the
finiteness has to be on the functions: only finitely many are non-zero on $V$ (``otherwise it didn't make sense'') \ts{5}{1}{52:41}\ts{5}{1}{53:05}\ts{5}{1}{53:29}. The version above is the corrected one.
\end{coursenote}
The whole business with finiteness, local finiteness of paracompactness mirrored in the functions, is there to have a \emph{finite} upper limit in the sum: this way one avoids issues of convergence;
with an infinity one would have to worry about it, and later, when paracompactness is used, one cannot afford to worry about convergence here: paracompactness with its local finiteness ``saves the day''
\ts{5}{1}{53:49}\ts{5}{1}{54:10}\ts{5}{1}{54:31}.

\begin{intuition}[Why one wants partitions of unity: patching integrals]\label{int:iv-pou}
Good examples come later, once there are manifolds and one wants integration on them; an outlook \ts{5}{1}{54:31}\ts{5}{1}{54:52}. If one has a cover, its open sets will be \emph{charts}: on each, one
can go to coordinates and define integration through the coordinates. But the sets overlap, and the integrals on different sets have to be patched together: one cannot just add, as the overlap would count
twice. The partition of unity puts weights on the contributions coming from different overlapping subsets and smooths the results into each other \ts{5}{1}{54:52}\ts{5}{1}{55:13}\ts{5}{1}{55:33}\ts{5}{1}{1:01:29}.
Finiteness lets one exchange the sum with the integral, with no limit and no series to worry about; recall that exchanging limits and integrals is, very roughly speaking, allowed
if the convergence is uniform. ``The idea is extremely simple, and paracompactness guarantees that only finitely many such interpolating (meshing) functions occur: that is the whole point''
\ts{5}{1}{1:01:29}\ts{5}{1}{1:01:49}\ts{5}{1}{1:02:10}\ts{5}{1}{1:02:32}\ts{5}{1}{1:02:52}.
\end{intuition}

\begin{example}[A partition of unity on the real line]\label{ex:iv-pou}
$\R$ with the standard topology is paracompact, so every open cover admits a partition of unity \ts{5}{1}{55:54}\ts{5}{1}{56:15}. Take the cover by two open sets $U_1=(-\infty,b)$ and $U_2=(a,\infty)$
with $a<b$ [?: the picture on the board is not in the captions; the two sets overlap in $(a,b)$] \ts{5}{1}{56:15}\ts{5}{1}{56:37}. Define $f_2$ as $0$ for $x\le a$, rising linearly with slope $\frac1{b-a}$ to height $1$ at $x=b$
and staying at $1$ after; and $f_1:=1-f_2$, $1$ up to $a$ and falling linearly to $0$ at $b$. [?: ``slope plus and minus one'' in the lecture means the same picture with $b-a=1$]
\ts{5}{1}{56:58}\ts{5}{1}{57:22}\ts{5}{1}{57:42}\ts{5}{1}{58:02}\ts{5}{1}{58:23}\ts{5}{1}{58:44}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f_1$ is non-zero only on $U_1$, $f_2$ only on $U_2$: condition~(a) \ts{5}{1}{58:44}\ts{5}{1}{59:05}.
  \item They are continuous (with the induced topology on $[0,1]$ and the standard topology on $\R$): one has to check, and the answer is yes \ts{5}{1}{59:05}\ts{5}{1}{59:25}.
  \item There are only two functions, so finiteness is trivial (``the situation could be worse, but here it is trivial''), and on every neighbourhood $V$ of every point the sum is $1$:
        where one is $0$ the other is $1$, and where one decreases linearly the other increases linearly \ts{5}{1}{59:46}\ts{5}{1}{1:00:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:00:27}\ts{5}{1}{1:00:48}\ts{5}{1}{1:01:09}.
\end{itemize}
Condition~(a) does not say that the functions cannot overlap: they can be non-zero on another set too, they just must vanish outside the set assigned to them \ts{5}{1}{59:46}.
\end{example}

\section{Connectedness and path connectedness}\label{sec:iv-connected}

Two very useful properties: they tell whether a space consists of one piece or several \ts{5}{1}{1:02:52}\ts{5}{1}{1:03:23}. If a space consists of several pieces in a sense to be defined, one can study the individual pieces:
one decomposes the whole space into building blocks and understands the space from them, a basic principle \ts{5}{1}{1:03:23}\ts{5}{1}{1:03:44}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: connected]\label{def:iv-connected}
A topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ is called \textbf{connected} unless there exist two non-empty, non-intersecting open sets $A,B\in\mathcal O$ such that $M=A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B$
(a disjoint union; the dot on top emphasises that) \ts{5}{1}{1:03:44}\ts{5}{1}{1:04:08}\ts{5}{1}{1:04:28}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[$\R\setminus\{0\}$ is not connected]\label{ex:iv-not-conn}
Take the real line with the point $0$ taken out, with the topology induced by the standard topology \ts{5}{1}{1:04:48}\ts{5}{1}{1:05:09}. Let $A=(-\infty,0)$ and $B=(0,\infty)$. Both are non-empty, disjoint and open in the induced
topology $\mathcal O'$, and $M$ is clearly their union: so $M$ is not connected \ts{5}{1}{1:05:09}\ts{5}{1}{1:05:32}\ts{5}{1}{1:05:56}\ts{5}{1}{1:06:18}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[What was taken out]
The transcript says ``take out the empty set'', i.e.\ nothing, which would leave $\R$, which \emph{is} connected; from the sets $A,B$ used it must be the point $0$ that is removed.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:iv-interval}
The interval $[0,1]$ with the topology induced from $\R$ is connected \ts{5}{1}{1:06:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:06:43}.
\end{theorem}
A little work to prove, not given in class; once one knows it, one can show a number of other things. One aspect of looking only at connected parts of a space is that a rather weird property of topological spaces
is removed: having sets that are both open and closed at the same time. This is true on connected pieces, or on the individual building blocks if the space is not connected \ts{5}{1}{1:06:43}\ts{5}{1}{1:07:05}\ts{5}{1}{1:07:25}.
\begin{exercise}[{$[0,1]$} is connected]\label{ex:iv-interval-conn}
Prove \cref{thm:iv-interval} (not done in class). The long version is in \cref{exam:ch05}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}[Connectedness and clopen sets]\label{thm:iv-clopen}
A topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ is connected if and only if $\emptyset$ and $M$ are the only subsets that are both open and closed \ts{5}{1}{1:07:25}\ts{5}{1}{1:07:46}.
\end{theorem}
Recall that $\emptyset$ and $M$ are always both open and closed (\cref{prop:to-clopen}). To get some practice in working with the notion, prove both directions \ts{5}{1}{1:08:09}.
\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) Let $M$ be connected, and suppose by contradiction that there is a set $U\subseteq M$, open and closed, with $U\ne\emptyset$ and $U\ne M$ \ts{5}{1}{1:08:30}\ts{5}{1}{1:08:50}\ts{5}{1}{1:09:12}.
Then $M=U\mathbin{\dot\cup}(M\setminus U)$, always true for any subset, a disjoint union \ts{5}{1}{1:09:35}. $U$ is open and non-empty by assumption. $M\setminus U$ is open, since
$U$ is closed; it is non-empty, since it could only be empty if $U=M$, which is excluded \ts{5}{1}{1:09:35}\ts{5}{1}{1:09:56}\ts{5}{1}{1:10:17}. So we have decomposed $M$ into
two non-empty, non-intersecting open subsets: $M$ is not connected, contradicting the assumption. We assumed the negation of the conclusion and obtained the negation of the assumption,
which is exactly a proof by contradiction \ts{5}{1}{1:10:17}\ts{5}{1}{1:10:38}\ts{5}{1}{1:10:59}.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $M$ is not connected \ts{5}{1}{1:10:59}\ts{5}{1}{1:11:19}. Then there are non-empty, non-intersecting open $A,B$ with $M=A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B$, so $B=M\setminus A$
\ts{5}{1}{1:11:41}\ts{5}{1}{1:12:02}. Since $B$ is open, $A=M\setminus B$ is closed; $A$ is open by assumption. $A\ne\emptyset$, and $A\ne M$ since $B\ne\emptyset$
\ts{5}{1}{1:12:23}\ts{5}{1}{1:12:44}\ts{5}{1}{1:13:05}\ts{5}{1}{1:13:31}. So $A$ is open and closed and neither $\emptyset$ nor $M$ \ts{5}{1}{1:13:31}\ts{5}{1}{1:13:51}\ts{5}{1}{1:14:13}. By contraposition, if
the only open and closed sets are $\emptyset$ and $M$, then $M$ is connected.
\end{proof}
In a sense this reconciles the intuitive notion of open and closed as incompatible: for the extreme cases $\emptyset$ and $M$ they must be compatible, but for the others it is excluded if one looks only at a connected piece
\ts{5}{1}{1:14:13}\ts{5}{1}{1:14:33}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: path connected]\label{def:iv-path}
A topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ is called \textbf{path connected} if for every pair of points $p,q\in M$ there exists a continuous curve $\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M$ with $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\gamma(1)=q$
\ts{5}{1}{1:14:33}\ts{5}{1}{1:14:53}\ts{5}{1}{1:15:16}\ts{5}{1}{1:15:37}\ts{5}{1}{1:15:57}.
\end{definition}
Intuitively: one can walk from one place to the other along a continuous curve; this is a stronger notion of connectedness, also very important \ts{5}{1}{1:14:33}\ts{5}{1}{1:14:53}. The key point is that the curve is continuous, with
$[0,1]$ carrying the topology induced from $\R$ \ts{5}{1}{1:15:57}.

\begin{example}[$\R^d$ is path connected]\label{ex:iv-Rd-path}
For $p,q\in\R^d$ define $\gamma(\lambda):=p+\lambda(q-p)$, with the componentwise addition and scalar multiplication of $d$-tuples. This straight line is continuous in the standard topology, as can be checked quickly
\ts{5}{1}{1:15:57}\ts{5}{1}{1:16:19}\ts{5}{1}{1:16:39}\ts{5}{1}{1:16:59}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Connected but not path connected]\label{ex:iv-sine}
This one needs a little more work \ts{5}{1}{1:16:59}\ts{5}{1}{1:17:19}. Let
\[
  S:=\bigl\{(x,\sin\tfrac1x)\bigm|x\in(0,1]\bigr\}\cup\{(0,0)\}\ \subseteq\R^2
\]
[?: as heard, ``a graph $x,1/x$'' with $x\in(0,1]$ and $(0,0)$ added; see the note below], a subset of the plane, with the induced topology
\ts{5}{1}{1:17:19}\ts{5}{1}{1:17:43}\ts{5}{1}{1:18:03}\ts{5}{1}{1:18:25}\ts{5}{1}{1:18:46}. As $x\to0$ the graph ``looks crazy'' \ts{5}{1}{1:18:03}\ts{5}{1}{1:18:25}. This topological space is \emph{connected} but \emph{not path connected}
\ts{5}{1}{1:18:46}\ts{5}{1}{1:19:06}. So connectedness is weaker than path connectedness \ts{5}{1}{1:19:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:19:27}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[$\sin\frac1x$, not $\frac1x$]
With $\frac1x$ the point $(0,0)$ would be isolated, and the set not even connected; and the picture ``crazy as $x\to0$'' only fits an oscillating function. The standard example of this kind is the
topologist's sine curve with $\sin\frac1x$; I read the captions as having dropped the ``sin''. The claim is stated in class without proof.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:iv-path-conn}
A path connected topological space is connected; \cref{ex:iv-sine} shows that the converse does not hold in general \ts{5}{1}{1:19:27}\ts{5}{1}{1:19:49}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By contradiction \ts{5}{1}{1:19:49}\ts{5}{1}{1:20:12}. Suppose $(M,\mathcal O)$ is path connected but not connected \ts{5}{1}{1:20:12}\ts{5}{1}{1:20:32}. Not connected means there are non-empty, non-intersecting
open sets $A,B$ with $M=A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B$ \ts{5}{1}{1:20:32}\ts{5}{1}{1:20:53}. Since they are non-empty, choose $a\in A$ and $b\in B$ (``if they were empty that would be a problem'')
\ts{5}{1}{1:20:53}\ts{5}{1}{1:21:14}\ts{5}{1}{1:21:35}. By path connectedness there is a continuous curve $\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M$ with $\gamma(0)=a$ and $\gamma(1)=b$ \ts{5}{1}{1:21:35}\ts{5}{1}{1:21:59}.
Consider the pre-image of $M$ under $\gamma$: it is the whole domain $[0,1]$, and
\begin{align*}
  [0,1]&=\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(M)=\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B)\\
  &=\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(A)\cup\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(B),
\end{align*}
since the pre-image of a union is the union of the pre-images (``if in doubt write down the definition of the pre-image'') \ts{5}{1}{1:21:59}\ts{5}{1}{1:22:22}\ts{5}{1}{1:22:43}\ts{5}{1}{1:23:03}.
Because $\gamma$ is continuous and $A,B$ are open, both pre-images are open (\cref{def:to-cont}) \ts{5}{1}{1:23:03}\ts{5}{1}{1:23:24}\ts{5}{1}{1:23:45}. They are disjoint: a parameter cannot be mapped into $A$ and $B$ at the same time
\ts{5}{1}{1:23:45}\ts{5}{1}{1:24:18}. They are non-empty: $0\in\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(A)$ and $1\in\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(B)$ \ts{5}{1}{1:24:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:24:38}\ts{5}{1}{1:24:59}.
So $[0,1]$ is decomposed into two non-empty, non-intersecting open subsets, i.e.\ $[0,1]$ is not connected \ts{5}{1}{1:24:59}\ts{5}{1}{1:25:20}. This contradicts \cref{thm:iv-interval}. Hence a path connected space cannot be not connected
\ts{5}{1}{1:25:20}\ts{5}{1}{1:25:41}.
\end{proof}
Questions? There were none \ts{5}{1}{1:25:41}.

A notion of \emph{simple connectedness} exists too; Schuller could define it now, but moves it to the next section, since it leads to the fundamental group \ts{5}{1}{1:26:11}\ts{5}{1}{1:26:32}.
\begin{coursenote}[Simple connectedness]

\end{coursenote}

\section{Homotopic curves and the fundamental group}\label{sec:iv-pi1}

So far the topological properties were of yes--no character: Hausdorff or not, compact or not, connected or not, paracompact or not \ts{5}{1}{1:26:32}\ts{5}{1}{1:26:58}. Now \emph{group valued} properties: the property is not yes or no, but
a group with an operation \ts{5}{1}{1:26:58}\ts{5}{1}{1:27:18}. Again, if two topological spaces are associated with different groups, they cannot be homeomorphic; but equal groups, e.g.\ the same fundamental groups, do not imply
homeomorphic: ``I repeat this again and again, there is no such list of properties'' \ts{5}{1}{1:27:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:27:39}\ts{5}{1}{1:27:59}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: homotopic curves]\label{def:iv-homotopic}
Two curves $\gamma,\delta\colon[0,1]\to M$ into a topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ are called \textbf{homotopic}, written $\gamma\sim\delta$, if there is a continuous map $h\colon[0,1]\times[0,1]\to M$ (both
factors with the topology induced from $\R$, the product with the product topology, so that continuity makes sense) with
\[
  h(0,\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda),\qquad h(1,\lambda)=\delta(\lambda)\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in[0,1]
\]
\ts{5}{1}{1:27:59}\ts{5}{1}{1:28:20}\ts{5}{1}{1:28:46}\ts{5}{1}{1:29:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:29:29}\ts{5}{1}{1:29:50}\ts{5}{1}{1:30:10}.
\end{definition}
The second variable is still the parameter of the individual curves, the first one interpolates between the curves \ts{5}{1}{1:29:29}\ts{5}{1}{1:29:50}. Picture: two curves with the same start and end point,
$\gamma(0)=\delta(0)$ and $\gamma(1)=\delta(1)$, and lots of curves lying in between, such as $h(\tfrac12,\cdot)$: one can continuously deform one into the other \ts{5}{1}{1:30:10}\ts{5}{1}{1:30:31}\ts{5}{1}{1:30:51}\ts{5}{1}{1:31:13}.
Whenever somebody talks of a continuous deformation, it is formally encoded in one continuous map on this product space, an interpolation map \ts{5}{1}{1:31:13}\ts{5}{1}{1:31:33}\ts{5}{1}{1:31:55}.
\begin{coursenote}[Fixed end points]
The lecture says ``two curves with the same starting and end point'' but the definition above does not demand that the intermediate curves keep those end points. The standard definition does ($h(\sigma,0)=\gamma(0)$
and $h(\sigma,1)=\gamma(1)$ for all $\sigma$); it is the one needed for loops. Not said in class: I follow the lecture's wording and note it.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:iv-homotopy-equiv}
For curves with the same starting and end point, ``$\gamma$ is homotopic to $\delta$'' is an equivalence relation \ts{5}{1}{1:31:55}\ts{5}{1}{1:32:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:32:39}\ts{5}{1}{1:33:00}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Reflexive: every curve is homotopic to itself, by the constant-in-$\sigma$ map $h(\sigma,\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda)$. Symmetric: from $h$ between $\gamma$ and $\delta$, the map $(\sigma,\lambda)\mapsto h(1-\sigma,\lambda)$ goes from
$\delta$ to $\gamma$. Transitive: ``you can check, but it is intuitively clear'': if one can deform $\delta$ into $\gamma$ and $\gamma$ into $\epsilon$, one can deform $\delta$ into $\epsilon$
\ts{5}{1}{1:33:00}\ts{5}{1}{1:33:20}. (Formal proof of transitivity, gluing two homotopies with doubled speed in $\sigma$: not done in class.)
\end{proof}
The nice thing about equivalence relations is that one can put on glasses and see only the classes, no longer the representatives (\cref{def:cl-quotient}) \ts{5}{1}{1:33:20}\ts{5}{1}{1:33:41}. First a class of curves to apply this to:

\begin{definition}[New concepts: loops at $p$]\label{def:iv-loops}
Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a topological space and $p\in M$. The \textbf{space of loops at $p$} is
$\mathcal L_p:=\{\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M\mid \gamma\text{ continuous and }\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=p\}$ \ts{5}{1}{1:33:41}\ts{5}{1}{1:34:02}\ts{5}{1}{1:34:23}\ts{5}{1}{1:34:44}\ts{5}{1}{1:35:06}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: concatenation of loops]\label{def:iv-concat}
For $\gamma,\delta\in\mathcal L_p$ define $\gamma*\delta\in\mathcal L_p$ by
\[
  (\gamma*\delta)(\lambda):=\begin{cases}\gamma(2\lambda)&0\le\lambda<\tfrac12,\\ \delta(2\lambda-1)&\tfrac12\le\lambda\le1\end{cases}
\]
\ts{5}{1}{1:35:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:35:27}\ts{5}{1}{1:35:49}\ts{5}{1}{1:36:12}\ts{5}{1}{1:36:35}.
\end{definition}
Intuitively one runs through the first loop at double speed (``speed does not matter to the topology''), and then through the second: at $\lambda=\frac12$ one is at $p$ and runs through the second loop, and all together it is
again a loop, because start and end point are the same \ts{5}{1}{1:36:35}\ts{5}{1}{1:36:55}\ts{5}{1}{1:37:16}.

Picture of two loops: remove from the blackboard plane a point; one loop encloses the removed point and one does not. These two loops cannot be homotopic: one cannot continuously deform one into the other, since the hole in the plane prevents
going over it in a continuous way \ts{5}{1}{1:37:16}\ts{5}{1}{1:37:37}\ts{5}{1}{1:37:57}\ts{5}{1}{1:38:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:38:38}. So one wants to look not at the space of loops, but at the space of loops \emph{modulo homotopy}:
``we don't care where exactly the loop lies, as long as the situations are continuously connected'' \ts{5}{1}{1:38:38}\ts{5}{1}{1:39:00}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: fundamental group]\label{def:iv-pi1}
The \textbf{fundamental group} $\pi_1(M,p)$ (the name \emph{pi}, with the $1$ because higher groups can be built; see below) of a topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ at the point $p$ is the set
\[
  \pi_1(M,p):=\mathcal L_p/{\sim}
\]
of loops at $p$ modulo homotopy, i.e.\ the quotient set (\cref{def:cl-quotient}) of equivalence classes $[\gamma]$, together with the operation $\bullet\colon\pi_1(M,p)\times\pi_1(M,p)\to\pi_1(M,p)$ (``blob'')
\[
  [\gamma]\bullet[\delta]:=[\gamma*\delta]
\]
\ts{5}{1}{1:39:00}\ts{5}{1}{1:39:22}\ts{5}{1}{1:39:46}\ts{5}{1}{1:40:07}\ts{5}{1}{1:40:27}\ts{5}{1}{1:40:48}\ts{5}{1}{1:41:09}\ts{5}{1}{1:41:31}.
\end{definition}
The group name: one might call it $\pi_1$ because others (higher homotopy groups $\pi_n$) can be built, for $n=1$ from loops; ``we come to that''; here we will not [?: the explanation of the ``1'' is cut off in the captions].
The operation is defined through representatives, so one needs to check that it is well defined (\cref{rem:cl-wd}): ``I assure you it is, but you are invited to check at home'' \ts{5}{1}{1:41:31}\ts{5}{1}{1:41:51}.
\begin{exercise}[Well-definedness]\label{ex:iv-wd}
Check that $\bullet$ is well defined, i.e.\ independent of the representatives (home exercise; the full version with the group axioms is in \cref{exam:ch05}) \ts{5}{1}{1:41:51}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:iv-group}
$(\pi_1(M,p),\bullet)$ is a group \ts{5}{1}{1:41:51}\ts{5}{1}{1:42:12}\ts{5}{1}{1:44:04}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Sketch, as given in class]
The operation is associative \ts{5}{1}{1:42:12}. The neutral element is the class of the constant curve $\gamma_e(\lambda)=p$ for all $\lambda$: concatenating with it gives the same curve at least up to homotopy
\ts{5}{1}{1:42:12}\ts{5}{1}{1:42:36}\ts{5}{1}{1:42:56}. The inverse of $[\gamma]$ is the class of the curve run backwards, $\lambda\mapsto\gamma(1-\lambda)$: one wants $[\gamma]\bullet[\gamma^{-1}]=[\gamma_e]$
\ts{5}{1}{1:42:56}\ts{5}{1}{1:43:18}\ts{5}{1}{1:43:41}. Nothing is known about commutativity, but one has a group \ts{5}{1}{1:43:41}\ts{5}{1}{1:44:04}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[The sphere]\label{ex:iv-sphere}
Take the surface of the sphere: the unit sphere in $\R^3$ with the topology induced from $\R^3$, and then forget the unit business and think only of the topology \ts{5}{1}{1:44:04}\ts{5}{1}{1:44:24}\ts{5}{1}{1:44:45}.
Go to a point $p$ and make a loop. All loops are homotopic to each other, in particular to the constant curve at $p$; so there is only one class: $\pi_1(S^2,p)$ is a one-element group (it must have at least one element, the
neutral one, which is then its own inverse) \ts{5}{1}{1:44:45}\ts{5}{1}{1:45:05}\ts{5}{1}{1:45:29}\ts{5}{1}{1:45:49}.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: torus, cylinder]\label{def:iv-torus}
The \textbf{torus} is the Cartesian product $S^1\times S^1$ of two circles (\cref{sec:to-construct}) with the product topology; it can be drawn as the surface of a doughnut (``the surface of the stut'' in the captions [?: a doughnut]).
The \textbf{cylinder} is $\R\times S^1$ with the product topology \ts{5}{1}{1:45:49}\ts{5}{1}{1:46:10}\ts{5}{1}{1:46:31}\ts{5}{1}{1:47:33}\ts{5}{1}{1:47:54}.
\end{definition}
Shape is an intuition of ours but topology provides no shape: two spaces of different shape, like the sphere drawn round and drawn like a potato, are homeomorphic, and through the glasses of topology they are the same; it is just
an artifact of the presentation on the blackboard. Being homeomorphic, they share all the topological properties: compact, connected, path connected, trivial fundamental group \ts{5}{1}{1:46:31}\ts{5}{1}{1:46:51}\ts{5}{1}{1:47:12}.
\begin{coursenote}[$S^2$ and the potato]
The lecture draws ``the sphere like this'' on the board; the picture is not in the captions. Here: a closed surface of any shape that is homeomorphic to the round sphere.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{example}[The cylinder]\label{ex:iv-cyl}
The cylinder never ends. A loop at a point $p$ that does not go around can be contracted to the constant loop: homotopic to the identity \ts{5}{1}{1:47:54}\ts{5}{1}{1:48:14}. A loop that winds around the cylinder once cannot be deformed
into the constant one, as the cylinder is infinitely long [?: more precisely, as $S^1$ cannot be contracted, whatever the length]; a loop that winds twice cannot be deformed into the one that winds once; and winding in the other direction
is a further class: ``I don't think so, think about it'' \ts{5}{1}{1:48:14}\ts{5}{1}{1:48:36}\ts{5}{1}{1:48:56}\ts{5}{1}{1:49:17}\ts{5}{1}{1:49:39}. So the set $\pi_1$ is the integers (number of windings, with sign), the zero being the identity, and
concatenation adds the windings:
\[
  \pi_1(\R\times S^1,p)\ \cong\ (\mathbb Z,+)
\]
\ts{5}{1}{1:49:39}\ts{5}{1}{1:50:00}\ts{5}{1}{1:50:21}.
\end{example}
\begin{recall}[Isomorphism of groups]
An isomorphism of groups is a bijection that respects the group structure: a bijection $F\colon\pi_1\to\mathbb Z$ with $F(\alpha\bullet\beta)=F(\alpha)+F(\beta)$; the structure preserving map of
group theory \cref{int:cl-theme} \ts{5}{1}{1:50:21}\ts{5}{1}{1:50:42}\ts{5}{1}{1:51:03}.
\end{recall}
A student asks whether one could take a cylinder without constant radius: topologically it makes no difference; and a curve wrapping around at another place can be deformed into the first: ``think of a rubber band'', that is the intuition, but one should
always refer to the existence of a homotopy $h$ interpolating between the curves; topologically the difference is not seen \ts{5}{1}{1:51:03}\ts{5}{1}{1:51:25}\ts{5}{1}{1:51:45}\ts{5}{1}{1:52:06}.

\begin{example}[The torus]\label{ex:iv-torus}
One can wind any number of times around the inner hole, and independently any number of times around the other direction (around the tube); the fundamental group is the Cartesian product of the two, and the product $\bullet$ corresponds to
pairwise addition (the order does not matter): ``I think the fundamental group of the torus is''
\[
  \pi_1(S^1\times S^1,p)\ \cong\ \mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z
\]
\ts{5}{1}{1:52:06}\ts{5}{1}{1:52:27}\ts{5}{1}{1:52:48}\ts{5}{1}{1:53:08}\ts{5}{1}{1:53:29}.
\end{example}
Immediately: this group differs from the others. The sphere (trivial group), the cylinder ($\mathbb Z$) and the torus ($\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$) have different fundamental groups, which are constructed from the topology alone; so none of the three is homeomorphic to any other
\ts{5}{1}{1:53:29}\ts{5}{1}{1:53:51}.
\begin{exercise}[Fundamental groups]\label{ex:iv-pi1-ex}
Prove $\pi_1(S^2,p)=\{e\}$, $\pi_1(\R\times S^1,p)\cong\mathbb Z$ and $\pi_1(S^1\times S^1,p)\cong\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$ with an explicit homotopy for each class (``one should always refer to the existence of a homotopy'')
(not set in class: classical results, only argued intuitively in the lecture).
\end{exercise}

\subsection{No classification}\label{sec:iv-end}

Again: no collection of topological invariants (Hausdorff yes/no, compact yes/no, connected yes/no, fundamental group, not yes/no but group valued) is known such that for two topological spaces one could check that all invariants agree
and conclude that the spaces are homeomorphic. It would be great to have such a classification, but none is known \ts{5}{1}{1:53:51}\ts{5}{1}{1:54:15}\ts{5}{1}{1:54:37}\ts{5}{1}{1:54:57}\ts{5}{1}{1:55:17}\ts{5}{1}{1:55:38}.

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State $T_1$ and $T_2$; which is weaker? Which topology on a set with two points is not $T_1$?
  \item Why is a limit unique in a Hausdorff space and not in the chaotic topology?
  \item Define open cover, finite subcover, compact. Why is $\R$ not compact and $[0,1]$ compact?
  \item Define refinement, locally finite, paracompact. Why does compact imply paracompact? Which spaces are paracompact?
  \item What is a partition of unity subordinate to a cover, and why is local finiteness needed in it?
  \item Define connected and path connected; which implies which? Prove connected $\Leftrightarrow$ only $\emptyset$, $M$ clopen.
  \item Define homotopic curves, loops, $*$, $\pi_1(M,p)$. What are $\pi_1$ of the sphere, the cylinder, the torus?
  \item Why does no list of invariants decide homeomorphism?
\end{enumerate}
