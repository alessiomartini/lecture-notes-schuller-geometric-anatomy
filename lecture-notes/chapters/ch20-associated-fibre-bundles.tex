\section{The associated bundle}\label{sec:ab-def}

``Good morning, happy new year and welcome back'': today the associated bundles (section 5.3 of the lecture) \ts{20}{1}{0:00}. An associated bundle is a bundle that is associated, in a certain precise sense, with a principal fibre bundle. So far it may have remained obscure how principal bundles relate to the constructions of physics; one needs the associated bundles in order to start grasping the importance of principal bundles \ts{20}{1}{0:26} \ts{20}{1}{0:46}.

The data: a principal $G$-bundle $(P,\pi,M)$, that is (\cref{def:pb-principal}) a bundle which is isomorphic to $P\to P/G$, with a right $G$-action $\ract$ on $P$; and in addition a smooth manifold $F$ with a \emph{left} $G$-action
\[
  \lact\colon G\times F\to F ,
\]
``a symbol that looks very similar but points to the other direction, a totally different map'', with the properties of \cref{def:pb-left} \ts{20}{1}{1:08} \ts{20}{1}{1:29} \ts{20}{1}{1:51} \ts{20}{1}{2:13} \ts{20}{1}{2:34} \ts{20}{1}{2:55}. From these data (the principal bundle, the manifold $F$, the left action) one defines the associated bundle \ts{20}{1}{3:17}: it needs a total space, called $P_F$ because it is constructed from $F$ and the principal bundle, and a projection $\pi_F\colon P_F\to M$ down to the \emph{same} base space \ts{20}{1}{3:37} \ts{20}{1}{3:58}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: associated bundle $P_F$]\label{def:ab-assoc}
On the Cartesian product $P\times F$ define the relation $\sim_G$ by
\[
  (p',f')\sim_G(p,f)\ :\Longleftrightarrow\ \exists g\in G:\ p'=p\ract g\ \text{ and }\ f'=g^{-1}\lact f .
\]
(One has to be careful: the right action acts on the big bundle space, the left action on $F$, and the inverse enters.) This is an equivalence relation, the \textbf{associated bundle} is the quotient set
\[
  P_F:=(P\times F)/\!\sim_G ,
\]
whose elements are the equivalence classes $[p,f]$ with $p\in P$, $f\in F$ (sometimes the brackets are left out), and the \textbf{associated projection} is
\[
  \pi_F\colon P_F\to M,\qquad\pi_F([p,f]):=\pi(p) .
\]
The name: the bundle is \emph{associated} with the principal bundle because it is built from it and from the action of the same group. \ts{20}{1}{4:18} \ts{20}{1}{4:41} \ts{20}{1}{5:01} \ts{20}{1}{5:22} \ts{20}{1}{5:42} \ts{20}{1}{6:03} \ts{20}{1}{6:28} \ts{20}{1}{6:52} \ts{20}{1}{8:40} \ts{20}{1}{9:00} \ts{20}{1}{9:21} \ts{20}{1}{9:42}
\end{definition}
That $\sim_G$ is an equivalence relation is ``quickly seen'' \ts{20}{1}{6:03}. (Not said in class: reflexive with $g=\mathbb 1$; if $(p',f')=(p\ract g,g^{-1}\lact f)$ then $(p,f)=(p'\ract g^{-1},(g^{-1})^{-1}\lact f')$; transitive with the product, using $(p\ract g_1)\ract g_2=p\ract(g_1\bullet g_2)$ and $g_2^{-1}\lact(g_1^{-1}\lact f)=(g_1\bullet g_2)^{-1}\lact f$.) The intuition: the fibres of $P$ are the group, so if one then mods out the group, of the first factor essentially only the base manifold is left, and to it one attaches the fibre $F$. So one expects $P_F$ to be the total space of a bundle over $M$ with typical fibre $F$, and it is ``tied to the principal bundle by virtue of the action'' \ts{20}{1}{6:52} \ts{20}{1}{7:14} \ts{20}{1}{7:35} \ts{20}{1}{7:56} \ts{20}{1}{8:17}.

\textbf{The projection is well defined} \ts{20}{1}{9:42}. One takes an equivalence class $[p,f]$ and sends it to $\pi(p)$, using one representative (but not the whole pair). We must check that another representative gives the same result. Any other representative has the form $(p\ract g,\,g^{-1}\lact f)$, and
\[
  \pi_F([p\ract g,g^{-1}\lact f])=\pi(p\ract g)=\pi(p)=\pi_F([p,f]),
\]
because $g$ only moves one within the fibre of the principal bundle. Hence $\pi_F$ is well defined \ts{20}{1}{10:06} \ts{20}{1}{10:27} \ts{20}{1}{10:48} \ts{20}{1}{11:08} \ts{20}{1}{11:29} \ts{20}{1}{11:49}.

So the construction yields a bundle \ts{20}{1}{11:49}. That it is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$ is to be shown on the problem sheet (``simple enough'').
\begin{theorem}[$P_F$ is a fibre bundle]\label{thm:ab-bundle}
$(P_F,\pi_F,M)$ is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$. \ts{20}{1}{12:11} \ts{20}{1}{12:34} \ts{20}{1}{12:57}
\end{theorem}
(The lecture gives no proof; see \cref{ex:ab-full}. Not said in class: the idea is that, over a small $U\subseteq M$ with a local section $\sigma$ of $P$, the map $U\times F\to\pi_F^{-1}(U)$, $(x,f)\mapsto[\sigma(x),f]$ is a bijection, which provides the local trivialisations.)

\section{Examples}\label{sec:ab-examples}

\subsection{Example A: the tangent bundle as an associated bundle of the frame bundle}\label{sec:ab-tangent}
Take $P=LM$, the frame bundle (\cref{def:pb-frame}), with projection $\pi$ and the right $GL(d,\R)$-action
\[
  (e_1,\dots,e_d)\ract g=\bigl(g^m{}_1e_m,\dots,g^m{}_de_m\bigr),
\]
where $d=\dim M$ is the dimension of the base space. ``As physicists would say, you have a basis of the tangent space, you act on it with a general linear transformation, and this is how the frame vectors transform'': discussed last time, when the lecturer said one would grasp the full meaning only with the associated bundles \ts{20}{1}{13:25} \ts{20}{1}{13:46} \ts{20}{1}{14:06} \ts{20}{1}{14:27} \ts{20}{1}{14:49} \ts{20}{1}{15:11} \ts{20}{1}{15:31} \ts{20}{1}{15:53}.

As fibre take $F=\R^d$ (the same $d$). We want an associated bundle with fibre $\R^d$ over $M$: we know such a bundle, the tangent bundle, but here we only say that the fibre is $\R^d$, and for the construction we provide as third ingredient a \emph{left} $GL(d,\R)$-action on $\R^d$ \ts{20}{1}{16:13} \ts{20}{1}{16:34} \ts{20}{1}{16:55} \ts{20}{1}{17:16} \ts{20}{1}{17:37}. The lecture writes it first as
\[
  (g\lact f)^a:=(g^{-1})^a{}_bf^b ,
\]
``the inverse GL transformation'': we model intuitively what physicists do all the time, if one changes the frame by a transformation one must change the components of an element of the tangent space the opposite way \ts{20}{1}{17:58} \ts{20}{1}{18:20} \ts{20}{1}{18:40} \ts{20}{1}{19:01}. After a clarification at the end of the lecture (below) this has to be corrected to
\[
  (g\lact f)^a=g^a{}_bf^b ,
\]
the ordinary matrix action on column vectors, and \emph{this} is the left action to use with the relation $(p\ract g,g^{-1}\lact f)\sim(p,f)$, which then supplies the inverse transformation of the components. \ts{20}{1}{55:44} \ts{20}{1}{56:05} \ts{20}{1}{56:27} \ts{20}{1}{56:47} \ts{20}{1}{57:08} \ts{20}{1}{57:32} \ts{20}{1}{57:52}
\begin{coursenote}[The inverse in the left action]
The lecture clarifies, after the break, a confusion: with the equivalence relation of \cref{def:ab-assoc} (which already contains $g^{-1}$) the left action on $\R^d$ must be defined \emph{without} the inverse, otherwise the inverse would act twice. Not said in class, a direct check: $f\mapsto(g^{-1})f$ is not a left action at all, since $g_2\lact(g_1\lact f)=g_2^{-1}g_1^{-1}f=(g_1g_2)^{-1}f\ne(g_2g_1)^{-1}f$, whereas $f\mapsto gf$ is. With the correct action one gets, for the map $u$ below, $u[e\ract g,g^{-1}\lact f]=(g^{-1})^a{}_bf^b\,g^m{}_ae_m=f^me_m$ as required. The lecturer remarks that in the other examples the action was written the right way. \ts{20}{1}{55:44}
\end{coursenote}
Now we are done: we have provided a principal bundle ($LM$), a fibre ($\R^d$) and a left action of the group of the principal bundle on it; so $(LM_{\R^d},\pi_{\R^d},M)$ is the associated bundle \ts{20}{1}{19:21} \ts{20}{1}{19:41} \ts{20}{1}{20:02} \ts{20}{1}{20:24}. ``The example ends after you provide the ingredients for the construction.'' The following is a \emph{claim} \ts{20}{1}{20:45}:
\begin{proposition}[Tangent bundle]\label{prop:ab-tangent}
There is a bundle isomorphism (over the identity of $M$) from $LM_{\R^d}$ to the tangent bundle $(TM,\pi_{TM},M)$, given by
\[
  u\colon LM_{\R^d}\to TM,\qquad u\bigl([e,f]\bigr):=f^ae_a .
\]
\ts{20}{1}{21:07} \ts{20}{1}{21:29} \ts{20}{1}{21:50} \ts{20}{1}{22:15} \ts{20}{1}{22:40} \ts{20}{1}{23:01} \ts{20}{1}{23:24} \ts{20}{1}{23:48}
\end{proposition}
Indeed, an element of the associated bundle is an equivalence class of a frame $e$ and an element $f$ of the fibre; one sends it to $f^ae_a$, which lies in some tangent space, so it is an element of the disjoint union of all tangent spaces. \emph{Well defined:} if one changes the frame by $g$, the fibre element is changed in the opposite direction and this cancels; so $f^ae_a$ is invariant under the action of $G$ as expressed by the equivalence class: ``that is precisely what we mean as physicists if we say if you change the frame you have to change the components the opposite way'' \ts{20}{1}{23:48} \ts{20}{1}{24:10} \ts{20}{1}{24:30}. This is all pretty trivial and not worth the effort here, since we know how it works anyway; but the interpretation is the other way around: here one does not \emph{conclude} that the components transform like this, one \emph{imposes} that they do. Whenever one has this chance, one can do it differently \ts{20}{1}{24:50} \ts{20}{1}{25:11}. (If one defines the action differently, one would probably not end up with the tangent bundle but something else; the point is that here the construction reproduces something known, and next we do something that is not yet known \ts{20}{1}{24:50}.)
\begin{answer}{Is $u$ invertible, and why can one choose an arbitrary basis?}[Inverse of $u$]
A student asks whether the map can be constructed backwards: yes. For any $X\in T_xM$ (of the tangent bundle, with a base point $x$) one can choose a frame $e$ at $x$ such that $X=f^ae_a$, so that the $f^a$ are the components of $X$ in the chosen basis, and one associates with $X$ the equivalence class $[e,f]$ (not the pair). One has made a big choice, the choice of frame; but the choices differ by a $GL$ transformation, and the equivalence relation identifies exactly all these choices, so the result is independent of the choice of frame, and this provides the inverse $u^{-1}$ \ts{20}{1}{25:11} \ts{20}{1}{25:31} \ts{20}{1}{25:52} \ts{20}{1}{26:12} \ts{20}{1}{26:33} \ts{20}{1}{26:54} \ts{20}{1}{27:15} \ts{20}{1}{27:37} \ts{20}{1}{27:59} \ts{20}{1}{28:20}. This is the answer, from the point of view of the associated bundle, to why one may choose an arbitrary basis to express tangent vectors: the one a less geometric course on tensors gives as ``a tensor is whatever transforms like a tensor'', with the group $GL$ unless one restricts it \ts{20}{1}{28:41} \ts{20}{1}{29:02} \ts{20}{1}{29:23}
\end{answer}
Some more comments. What is new in the associated bundle point of view is the \emph{involvement of the group} $GL(d,\R)$: in the tangent bundle there is no talk of any group. In physics one says ``this is a $GL(d)$ tensor'', ``an $SO(3)$ tensor'' (a tensor with respect to rotations), ``a tensor with respect to Lorentz transformations'', or a vector, and then the group plays a big role \ts{20}{1}{29:43} \ts{20}{1}{30:04} \ts{20}{1}{30:25}. So far we looked at tensors transforming under $GL(d)$; what if one restricts to Lorentz transformations, as is routinely done in special relativity for the tangent vectors to world lines? On the tangent bundle one would have to say from outside ``please only use Lorentz transformations'', whereas to make this precise one goes to the frame bundle and restricts the group of the underlying principal bundle to whatever group one likes, if that is possible at all (see \cref{sec:ab-restriction}) \ts{20}{1}{30:45} \ts{20}{1}{31:06} \ts{20}{1}{31:27} \ts{20}{1}{31:48}. This is a point where the formulation as associated bundles is important \ts{20}{1}{31:48} \ts{20}{1}{32:08}.

\subsection{Example B: the $(p,q)$ tensor bundle}\label{sec:ab-tensor}
A straightforward extension: again $P=LM$ with the same right action and group $G=GL(d,\R)$, but as fibre $p$ copies of $\R^d$ and $q$ copies of the dual,
\[
  F=\underbrace{\R^d\times\dots\times\R^d}_{p}\times\underbrace{(\R^d)^*\times\dots\times(\R^d)^*}_{q} ,
\]
the space of arrays of numbers $f^{i_1\dots i_p}{}_{j_1\dots j_q}$ (the convention is reintroduced: indices up for vector components, down for co-vector components, ``but this is just a collection of numbers at this point'') \ts{20}{1}{32:08} \ts{20}{1}{32:29} \ts{20}{1}{32:50} \ts{20}{1}{33:11} \ts{20}{1}{33:31} \ts{20}{1}{33:53} \ts{20}{1}{34:14} \ts{20}{1}{34:35}. The left action is
\[
  (g\lact f)^{i_1\dots i_p}{}_{j_1\dots j_q}=g^{i_1}{}_{\tilde\imath_1}\cdots g^{i_p}{}_{\tilde\imath_p}\,(g^{-1})^{\tilde\jmath_1}{}_{j_1}\cdots(g^{-1})^{\tilde\jmath_q}{}_{j_q}\,f^{\tilde\imath_1\dots\tilde\imath_p}{}_{\tilde\jmath_1\dots\tilde\jmath_q}
\]
(summed over the twiddled indices): $g$ for every up index, $g^{-1}$ for every down index. The lecturer first worries whether the inverses shouldn't stand on the other indices, but this is the form in which it will be used \ts{20}{1}{34:55} \ts{20}{1}{35:16} \ts{20}{1}{35:37} \ts{20}{1}{35:58} \ts{20}{1}{36:18}. Again this is the definition one \emph{imposes}: one constructs a bundle of objects defined by components (a Cartesian product), such that these components transform under the group acting on the frame bundle in the way one wants; this is the transformation law of the components of a $(p,q)$ tensor \ts{20}{1}{36:18} \ts{20}{1}{36:38} \ts{20}{1}{36:59}. Then
\[
  LM_F\ \cong\ T^p_qM\quad\text{as bundles,}
\]
the $(p,q)$ tensor bundle (of \cref{ch:l10}): ``this is not a big surprise, just the extension of the previous example'' \ts{20}{1}{36:59} \ts{20}{1}{37:21} \ts{20}{1}{37:43}.
\begin{answer}{Indices of the $g$'s}[Reading the formula]
A student is confused by the lower indices of the $g$'s: the first $g$ has $i_1$ on the top and $i_1$ again, the second $\tilde\jmath_1$ below. The lecturer: no, the indices run $i_p,\tilde\imath_p$ and then only $j$'s twiddled and untwiddled: the confusion came from his handwriting of the index $p$, which looked like a $j$ \ts{20}{1}{38:05} \ts{20}{1}{38:29} \ts{20}{1}{38:50} \ts{20}{1}{39:10}
\end{answer}
One more thing on the construction \ts{20}{1}{39:10}. In physics one thinks of a vector, a direction, and says: rotate the system, or go to another frame by a certain amount. This is done in the principal bundle: one chooses a $g$ and rotates the frame. If one understands the $(p,q)$ tensor bundle as an associated bundle of the \emph{same} frame bundle $LM$, as one understood the tangent bundle, then \emph{every associated bundle must follow suit}: if one rotates a vector in this way, all the tensors are rotated by the same transformation. One could also take the spinor bundle, with fibre $\C^4$ for Dirac spinors, for instance; one first has to restrict the group to a rotation group (``that we cannot do yet''), and then spinors transform the same way \ts{20}{1}{39:32} \ts{20}{1}{39:52} \ts{20}{1}{40:13} \ts{20}{1}{40:34}. If we took the frame bundle for $SO(1,3)$ or $SL(2,\C)$ over spacetime, one could say: if one rotates vectors this way then one has to rotate spinors that other way; it is always provided by the left action of the group on the various associated bundles one defined \ts{20}{1}{40:55} \ts{20}{1}{41:16}. Otherwise: who tells you that, if you change the basis in your tangent space, you must change the basis in the space of $(p,q)$ tensors in the same way, since they are different spaces and you can choose bases as you like? If one wants to impose this formally, one considers all these tensor spaces as the associated bundles over the same frame bundle: this ties together lots of stuff that physicists always say ``do this at the same time'' \ts{20}{1}{41:36} \ts{20}{1}{41:57} \ts{20}{1}{42:17}. ``All of this just reproduces stuff we already knew'' \ts{20}{1}{42:17} \ts{20}{1}{42:39}.

\subsection{Example C: tensor densities}\label{sec:ab-density}
The third example amounts to a definition of \emph{tensor densities}. Again $P=LM$, $G=GL(d,\R)$ and the fibre $F$ as in example B, so that the elements of the fibre look precisely like there \ts{20}{1}{42:39} \ts{20}{1}{43:01} \ts{20}{1}{43:23} \ts{20}{1}{43:46}. But now the left action is modified:
\begin{definition}[New concepts: tensor density bundle]\label{def:ab-density}
For $\omega\in\mathbb Z$ define on $F$ the left $GL(d,\R)$-action
\[
  (g\lact_\omega f)^{i_1\dots i_p}{}_{j_1\dots j_q}:=(\det g^{-1})^{\omega}\ g^{i_1}{}_{\tilde\imath_1}\cdots(g^{-1})^{\tilde\jmath_q}{}_{j_q}\,f^{\tilde\imath_1\dots\tilde\jmath_q},
\]
that is, the action of example B with an extra factor $(\det g^{-1})^\omega$ in front. The associated bundle $(LM_F,\pi_F,M)$ for this action is the \textbf{$(p,q)$ tensor $\omega$-density bundle} (the \textbf{tensor density bundle of weight $\omega$}); its sections are the $(p,q)$ tensor $\omega$-densities. \ts{20}{1}{43:46} \ts{20}{1}{44:07} \ts{20}{1}{44:28} \ts{20}{1}{44:50} \ts{20}{1}{45:11} \ts{20}{1}{45:34} \ts{20}{1}{45:55} \ts{20}{1}{46:16} \ts{20}{1}{46:37} \ts{20}{1}{46:59}
\end{definition}
(Traditionally one chooses $\omega$ an integer; some people now consider arbitrary rational or real numbers \ts{20}{1}{45:34}. It is a left action since $\det((g_2g_1)^{-1})=\det g_2^{-1}\det g_1^{-1}$. If the factor were left out, one would get a bundle isomorphic to the tensor bundle; with it, one cannot claim to get a bundle one already knows: ``we do not know that bundle yet'' \ts{20}{1}{45:55} \ts{20}{1}{46:16}.) For $\omega=0$ the factor goes away and one recovers the tensor bundle: a $(p,q)$ tensor is a $(p,q)$ $0$-density. Another limit case: the fibre is just $\R$, one real number, and the action only scales it by the determinant,
\[
  g\lact f:=\det(g^{-1})\,f\qquad(f\in\R),
\]
which gives the \textbf{scalar density bundle} (formally this does not fall exactly under the construction above, so it is written as a last example). Sections of this bundle are scalar density fields, sections of the former are $(p,q)$ tensor $\omega$-density fields \ts{20}{1}{47:20} \ts{20}{1}{47:41} \ts{20}{1}{48:04} \ts{20}{1}{48:25} \ts{20}{1}{48:48} \ts{20}{1}{49:12}. This is the precise bundle-theoretic way to think about densities.

\emph{Why densities matter, and why one does not see them in special relativity.} The determinant of $g^{-1}$ is not necessarily $1$, unless one restricts the group to one where the determinant is already one: for instance if one uses, instead of $GL(d,\R)$, the orthogonal transformations (the lecture: ``they have determinant one'', which is true for $SO$, whereas $O(d)$ also has determinant $-1$), then one does not see the tensor property. This is why densities do not seem to appear in special relativity, where one restricts to the Lorentz group, orthogonal with respect to the Minkowski inner product: many objects treated there as tensors are actually tensor densities, and only because the group is restricted does one not pick up the determinant factor \ts{20}{1}{49:12} \ts{20}{1}{49:32} \ts{20}{1}{49:53} \ts{20}{1}{50:13}.
\begin{answer}{Where does one really see densities in physics?}[Densities in physics]
A student asks whether there are specific cases in physics where one really sees this. The lecturer: certainly, when one integrates a function over a manifold. (Informally; it will be done precisely later.) Write $\int\!\mathrm d^dx\,f(x^1,\dots,x^d)$ over a manifold covered by one chart (else one needs a partition of unity). If one chooses a different chart one picks up, by the rules of analysis on $\R^d$, the Jacobian determinant of the new chart map with respect to the old one: a disaster, since the value of the integral changes. What one needs is to cancel it: the transformation of the chart is the $g$ here, and if $f$ is not a function but a function \emph{density}, it picks up exactly $\det g^{-1}$, which cancels the Jacobian. One cannot integrate a function, only a function density. To make this really precise one integrates forms, and a volume form is automatically a density; this is done later. The Lagrangian density of field theory is indeed a density, not a scalar, because one integrates over it \ts{20}{1}{50:34} \ts{20}{1}{50:54} \ts{20}{1}{51:15} \ts{20}{1}{51:36} \ts{20}{1}{51:56} \ts{20}{1}{52:17} \ts{20}{1}{52:38} \ts{20}{1}{52:58} \ts{20}{1}{53:19} \ts{20}{1}{53:39}
\end{answer}
\emph{Example: the Einstein action} \ts{20}{1}{53:39} \ts{20}{1}{54:00}. If one writes the Einstein action in general relativity, one writes $\det G$ (something like this) times the Ricci scalar. The point is that this $\det G$, the determinant of the space-time metric $G_{ab}$ (a capital $G$ here, so as not to mix it up with the group element $g$), is a density of weight two: under a general linear transformation $G_{ab}\mapsto g^m{}_ag^n{}_bG_{mn}$, the determinant is multiplied by $(\det g)^2$. The $\omega$ is called the \textbf{weight} of the density, here ``a density of weight two'' \ts{20}{1}{54:00} \ts{20}{1}{54:22} \ts{20}{1}{54:42} \ts{20}{1}{55:02} \ts{20}{1}{55:24}. This appears everywhere one integrates over manifolds and pushes to a chart; the very precise definition of integrals requires integrating forms \ts{20}{1}{55:24} \ts{20}{1}{55:44}.
\begin{coursenote}[Weights and signs]
Not said in class: with the convention of the lecture, $(\det g^{-1})^\omega$, the metric determinant has weight $\omega=-2$ or $+2$ depending on whether one lets $g$ act on frames or components (the lecture says ``weight two'' and does not fix the sign); and the integrand of the Einstein--Hilbert action is $\sqrt{|\det G|}\,R$, a scalar density of weight $1$, which is what makes the integral chart independent. Conventions on the sign of the weight differ between books. \ts{20}{1}{54:42}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Associated bundle maps}\label{sec:ab-maps}

What did we do? We defined a new construction for bundles: starting from a principal bundle, a fibre and a left action of the principal bundle's group on the fibre, we construct a fibre bundle with fibre $F$. Associated bundles are special bundles because they are constructed from a principal bundle: there is more structure, and whenever there is more structure one looks at the structure preserving maps and must take it into account \ts{20}{1}{58:13} \ts{20}{1}{58:34} \ts{20}{1}{58:55}. The tangent bundle considered as an associated bundle of the frame bundle has more structure, the structure group. Therefore bundle maps are again redefined \ts{20}{1}{59:16}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: associated bundle map]\label{def:ab-map}
Let $(P,\pi,M)$ and $(P',\pi',M')$ be principal $G$-bundles with right actions $\ract,\ract'$ (the same group, different bases and actions), $F$ a manifold with a left $G$-action, and $(P_F,\pi_F,M)$, $(P'_F,\pi'_F,M')$ the associated bundles. Let $(u,h)$, $u\colon P\to P'$, $h\colon M\to M'$, be a \emph{principal bundle map} (\cref{def:pb-pbmap}): $\pi'\circ u=h\circ\pi$ and $u(p\ract g)=u(p)\ract'g$. An \textbf{associated bundle map} (between associated bundles sharing the same fibre $F$ and the same group, but associated to different principal bundles) is the pair $(\tilde u,h)$ with
\[
  \tilde u\colon P_F\to P'_F,\qquad\tilde u\bigl([p,f]\bigr):=[u(p),f] ,
\]
that is, the bundle map $(\tilde u,h)$ induced by a principal bundle map in this specific way. In particular $\pi'_F\circ\tilde u=h\circ\pi_F$: the diagram

\end{definition}
In words: an associated bundle map is a bundle map which can be constructed in a specific way from a principal bundle map, so that it takes into account the very specific structure of an associated bundle \ts{20}{1}{1:06:37} \ts{20}{1}{1:06:57} \ts{20}{1}{1:07:20} \ts{20}{1}{1:07:52}. \textbf{Remarks} \ts{20}{1}{1:08:33}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[1.] Two $F$-fibre bundles may be isomorphic as bundles (there is a bundle isomorphism between them) and at the same time fail to be isomorphic \emph{as associated bundles} (no associated bundle isomorphism between them). ``That is the whole point of the previous definition'': if this were not so, the definition would be vacuous, one could always use the plain bundle morphisms. A fibre bundle which arises as an associated bundle carries more structure than a plain fibre bundle \ts{20}{1}{1:08:55} \ts{20}{1}{1:09:19} \ts{20}{1}{1:09:45} \ts{20}{1}{1:10:09} \ts{20}{1}{1:10:31} \ts{20}{1}{1:10:52} \ts{20}{1}{1:11:13}.
  \item[2.] Recall that an $F$-fibre bundle was called \emph{trivial} if there exists a bundle isomorphism to $M\times F$ with the projection onto the first factor (\cref{def:mf-trivial}); and a principal $G$-bundle was called trivial if there exists a \emph{principal bundle map} to $M\times G$ (\cref{def:pb-trivial}), a stronger criterion. For an associated bundle one does not define it by a similar structure: \textbf{an associated bundle is called trivial if the underlying principal bundle is trivial.} \ts{20}{1}{1:11:35} \ts{20}{1}{1:11:57} \ts{20}{1}{1:12:19} \ts{20}{1}{1:12:39} \ts{20}{1}{1:13:00} \ts{20}{1}{1:13:22} \ts{20}{1}{1:13:42} \ts{20}{1}{1:14:05} \ts{20}{1}{1:14:25} \ts{20}{1}{1:14:50}
\end{enumerate}
\begin{theorem}[Trivial associated bundles]\label{thm:ab-trivial}
A trivial associated bundle is a trivial fibre bundle: if the principal bundle $P$ is trivial, then $P_F$ is isomorphic, as a fibre bundle, to $M\times F$. The converse does not hold in general. \ts{20}{1}{1:14:50} \ts{20}{1}{1:15:14} \ts{20}{1}{1:15:34} \ts{20}{1}{1:15:55} \ts{20}{1}{1:16:18}
\end{theorem}
(This looks like a tautology: ``a trivial associated bundle is a trivial fibre bundle''. It is not: if we say ``trivial associated bundle'' we mean that the \emph{underlying principal bundle} is trivial; as a fibre bundle one then shows that $P_F$ is isomorphic to $M\times F$. The converse does generally not hold: given a trivial fibre bundle, one cannot even tell which associated bundle it is, and even if one knew, the principal bundle need not be trivial. The proof is an instruction on the problem sheet \ts{20}{1}{1:15:34} \ts{20}{1}{1:15:55} \ts{20}{1}{1:16:18} \ts{20}{1}{1:16:38}.)
\begin{proof}
\emph{(The lecture leaves it to the problem sheet; the argument is not said in class.)} By \cref{thm:pb-trivial-section} $P$ has a section $\sigma$ and, with $\chi_\sigma\colon P\to G$ of its proof, $p=\sigma(\pi(p))\ract\chi_\sigma(p)$. Define
\[
  \Phi\colon P_F\to M\times F,\quad[p,f]\mapsto\bigl(\pi(p),\chi_\sigma(p)\lact f\bigr).
\]
It is well defined: $\chi_\sigma(p\ract g)\lact(g^{-1}\lact f)=(\chi_\sigma(p)\bullet g\bullet g^{-1})\lact f$. It is bijective with inverse $(x,f)\mapsto[\sigma(x),f]$ (since $[p,f]=[\sigma(x)\ract\chi_\sigma(p),f]=[\sigma(x),\chi_\sigma(p)\lact f]$), and commutes with the projections. \ts{20}{1}{1:16:38}
\end{proof}
Warning: whenever one is handed a bundle and somebody claims it is trivial, one asks: is this just a bundle, a principal bundle, or a specific associated bundle? In each of the three cases triviality has another meaning. ``This is the usual business of refining the maps between objects according to the structure they carry'' \ts{20}{1}{1:16:59} \ts{20}{1}{1:17:21}.

\section{Sections of associated bundles are functions on the principal bundle}\label{sec:ab-sections}

Now an important theorem, practically important, which clarifies what precisely is the difference between a section on some bundle and a function on some bundle, and which works only for associated bundles (which, as we saw, is the case we are very often interested in) \ts{20}{1}{1:17:41} \ts{20}{1}{1:18:02}.
\begin{theorem}[Sections of $P_F$ and $F$-valued functions on $P$]\label{thm:ab-sections}
The sections $\sigma\colon M\to P_F$ of an associated bundle $(P_F,\pi_F,M)$ are in one-to-one correspondence with the $F$-valued functions $\varphi\colon P\to F$ on the underlying principal bundle satisfying
\[
  \varphi(p\ract g)=g^{-1}\lact\varphi(p)\qquad(p\in P,\ g\in G).
\]
\ts{20}{1}{1:18:02} \ts{20}{1}{1:18:26} \ts{20}{1}{1:18:47} \ts{20}{1}{1:19:07} \ts{20}{1}{1:19:28} \ts{20}{1}{1:19:48} \ts{20}{1}{1:20:09} \ts{20}{1}{1:20:31}
\end{theorem}
For instance, sections of the tangent bundle are the vector fields; if one considers the tangent bundle as an associated bundle, one adds some structure, thinking about how the frames transform, and one can think of vector fields in yet another way \ts{20}{1}{1:19:07} \ts{20}{1}{1:19:28}. We always said that sections are not functions: if the bundle is locally trivial one may write a section locally as a function, but globally it is not a function, a section is a section. But if one has an associated bundle, one can understand a section as an $F$-valued function on the underlying principal bundle. For a vector field one cannot necessarily think of it as a vector-valued function on the manifold (``at every point there is a vector, but a section is not that''); if one goes to the principal bundle and attaches to every \emph{point of the principal bundle} an $F$, i.e.\ a vector, such a function translates directly to a section of the associated bundle \ts{20}{1}{1:19:48} \ts{20}{1}{1:20:09} \ts{20}{1}{1:20:31} \ts{20}{1}{1:20:51} \ts{20}{1}{1:21:12} \ts{20}{1}{1:21:33}.
\begin{coursenote}[The equivariance condition]
The lecture states the theorem as ``$F$-valued functions on the principal bundle'' and does not write the condition $\varphi(p\ract g)=g^{-1}\lact\varphi(p)$; without it the construction below is not well defined (and the correspondence would not be one-to-one). The condition is the content of ``a vector field is a function on the frame bundle whose value, the components, changes the opposite way as the frame''. Added here, not said in class. \ts{20}{1}{1:18:02}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(The proof is a problem-sheet exercise; the lecture only gives the key idea, and the argument below is added, not said in class.)} \ts{20}{1}{1:21:33} \ts{20}{1}{1:21:53} Given $\varphi$ (equivariant), define the section
\[
  \sigma_\varphi(x):=[p,\varphi(p)]\quad\text{for any }p\in\pi^{-1}(x)
\]
\ts{20}{1}{1:22:17} \ts{20}{1}{1:22:37} \ts{20}{1}{1:22:58}. It is well defined: another point of the fibre is $p\ract g$ and $[p\ract g,\varphi(p\ract g)]=[p\ract g,g^{-1}\lact\varphi(p)]=[p,\varphi(p)]$. Conversely, given a section $\sigma$ and $p\in P$, the point $\sigma(\pi(p))$ lies in the fibre of $P_F$ over $\pi(p)$, hence has the form $[p,f]$ for a unique $f=:\varphi_\sigma(p)\in F$ (existence: it is $[q,f_0]$ with $q=p\ract g$, so $[q,f_0]=[p,g\lact f_0]$; uniqueness: $[p,f]=[p,f']$ gives $p\ract g=p$, so $g=\mathbb 1$ and $f=f'$, by freeness). Equivariance of $\varphi_\sigma$: $[p\ract g,\varphi_\sigma(p\ract g)]=\sigma(\pi(p))=[p,\varphi_\sigma(p)]=[p\ract g,g^{-1}\lact\varphi_\sigma(p)]$, and uniqueness. The two constructions are inverse to each other; smoothness is checked in local trivialisations. \ts{20}{1}{1:23:18} \ts{20}{1}{1:23:39}
\end{proof}
The lecture stresses that this theorem is so important that it is worth doing the proof oneself, so as to remember it in the future; the problem sheet will also provide examples of applications \ts{20}{1}{1:22:58} \ts{20}{1}{1:23:18} \ts{20}{1}{1:23:39}.
\begin{example}[Vector fields as equivariant functions on $LM$]\label{ex:ab-vf}
Not said in class, as an instance of the theorem: for $P=LM$ and $F=\R^d$ as in example A, a vector field $X$ corresponds to the function $\varphi_X\colon LM\to\R^d$, $\varphi_X(e)=$ the components of $X_{\pi(e)}$ in the frame $e$, $X_{\pi(e)}=\varphi_X(e)^ae_a$; it satisfies $\varphi_X(e\ract g)=g^{-1}\varphi_X(e)$.
\end{example}

\section{Restriction and extension of principal bundles}\label{sec:ab-restriction}

The final definition and theorem for associated bundles: restriction and extension of bundles. We deal with associated bundles with respect to different underlying principal bundles \ts{20}{1}{1:24:00} \ts{20}{1}{1:24:22} \ts{20}{1}{1:24:42}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: restriction and extension of a principal bundle]\label{def:ab-restriction}
Let $H$ be a \emph{closed subgroup} of the Lie group $G$, $(P,\pi,M)$ a principal $G$-bundle and $(P',\pi',M)$ a principal $H$-bundle over the same base space. If there exists a bundle map $u\colon P'\to P$ (over the identity of $M$, $\pi\circ u=\pi'$) such that
\[
  u(p'\ract h)=u(p')\ract h\qquad\text{for all }p'\in P',\ h\in H ,
\]
(on the left the right action of $H$ on $P'$, on the right the restriction to $H\subseteq G$ of the right action of $G$ on $P$), then $(P,\pi,M)$ is called a \textbf{$G$-extension} of the $H$-principal bundle $(P',\pi',M)$, and $(P',\pi',M)$ an \textbf{$H$-restriction} of the $G$-principal bundle $(P,\pi,M)$. \ts{20}{1}{1:24:42} \ts{20}{1}{1:25:03} \ts{20}{1}{1:25:24} \ts{20}{1}{1:26:11} \ts{20}{1}{1:26:52} \ts{20}{1}{1:27:13} \ts{20}{1}{1:27:34} \ts{20}{1}{1:27:54} \ts{20}{1}{1:28:17} \ts{20}{1}{1:28:37} \ts{20}{1}{1:28:58} \ts{20}{1}{1:29:18} \ts{20}{1}{1:29:44} \ts{20}{1}{1:30:06} \ts{20}{1}{1:30:28} \ts{20}{1}{1:30:49} \ts{20}{1}{1:31:15}
\end{definition}
Why this way: a principal bundle map in the sense of \cref{def:pb-pbmap} has been defined only for the same Lie group, or for two groups with a given homomorphism $\rho$; here one is given two Lie groups, one a closed subgroup of the other, but a subgroup can lie in the other group in various ways, and unless one specifies how by a homomorphism one does not know how. So with the definition so far there are not enough data. What one \emph{can} do is use the fact that $H$ is a subgroup: one requires only a bundle map (not a principal bundle map), and requires the compatibility with the action only for the elements of the subgroup $H$ (for all $p'\in P'$): ``an appropriate weakening of a principal bundle map''. If one is the restriction, the other is the extension \ts{20}{1}{1:25:51} \ts{20}{1}{1:26:11} \ts{20}{1}{1:26:32} \ts{20}{1}{1:28:58} \ts{20}{1}{1:31:15} \ts{20}{1}{1:31:36}.

So one can only make restrictions to (or extensions from) closed subgroups, if there exists such a bundle map, and the question is when it exists \ts{20}{1}{1:31:36}. \textbf{Example}: $H=SO(d)$ as a closed subgroup of $G=GL(d,\R)$: one may want to study principal bundles where the transformations are only rotations, and to see whether the frame bundle with $GL(d,\R)$ has a restriction to one with $SO(d)$. Maybe this is not always possible for any base, in fact it is; the possibility of restricting $GL(d,\R)$ to $SO(d)$ amounts to the question: can one equip the smooth manifold with a Riemannian metric? It turns out that this can always be done \ts{20}{1}{1:31:56} \ts{20}{1}{1:32:17} \ts{20}{1}{1:32:38} \ts{20}{1}{1:32:58} \ts{20}{1}{1:33:19}. What about the smaller group $SO(1,d-1)$, also a closed subgroup of $GL(d,\R)$ (the lecturer writes $SO(1,d)$ first and remarks that he went one dimension up, ``that was inconsistent'' \ts{20}{1}{1:34:22})? Can one restrict to the Lorentz group? It turns out no, not always: certain \emph{obstructions} can appear. Not every smooth manifold can be equipped with a Lorentzian metric, but every one can be equipped with a Riemannian metric \ts{20}{1}{1:33:40} \ts{20}{1}{1:34:01} \ts{20}{1}{1:34:22}.
\begin{theorem}[Extension]\label{thm:ab-extension}
Any $H$-principal bundle can be extended to a $G$-principal bundle, if $H$ is a closed subgroup of $G$. \ts{20}{1}{1:34:44} \ts{20}{1}{1:35:06} \ts{20}{1}{1:35:26} \ts{20}{1}{1:35:48}
\end{theorem}
(The closed-subgroup condition is part of the definition; it is emphasised again. Very roughly: extensions are always possible, there is no constraint on how one can extend, one can always make the group bigger \ts{20}{1}{1:35:26}.) Not said in class: the extension is $P'\times_HG$, the associated bundle for the action of $H$ on $G$ by left multiplication.
\begin{theorem}[Restriction]\label{thm:ab-restriction}
A $G$-principal bundle $(P,\pi,M)$ can be restricted to an $H$-principal bundle if $H$ is a closed subgroup of $G$ (again obvious, but meaningful to emphasise) and \emph{if and only if} the quotient bundle
\[
  P/H\ \longrightarrow\ M
\]
(where $P/H$ is the orbit space of the action of $H\subseteq G$ on $P$, with the projection $\pi'$ obtained from $P\to P/G$ by the canonical isomorphism) has a cross-section. \ts{20}{1}{1:35:48} \ts{20}{1}{1:36:12} \ts{20}{1}{1:36:33} \ts{20}{1}{1:36:53} \ts{20}{1}{1:37:15} \ts{20}{1}{1:37:42} \ts{20}{1}{1:38:02}
\end{theorem}
Remember the earlier theorem: a principal bundle is trivial iff it has a cross-section (\cref{thm:pb-trivial-section}); here the interesting thing is that the \emph{quotient} bundle has a section \ts{20}{1}{1:37:42} \ts{20}{1}{1:38:02}.
\begin{example}[Metrics and restrictions]\label{ex:ab-metric}
For the frame bundle of examples A and B (as stated in the lecture): (1) $LM/SO(d)\to M$ \emph{always} has a cross-section (the lecture writes $SO(3)$, meaning $SO(d)$); (2) $LM/SO(1,d-1)\to M$ \emph{may or may not} have one. For $M=S^2$ the bundle $LS^2/SO(1,1)$ with the subgroup $SO(1,1)\subseteq GL(2,\R)$ has no cross-section: one cannot equip the sphere $S^2$ with a Lorentzian metric. The relation with the metric: if one equips a manifold with a Lorentzian metric one can construct an orthogonal frame bundle from it, and this works always; but for some manifolds it cannot work, and then they cannot be associated with a Lorentzian metric. \ts{20}{1}{1:38:32} \ts{20}{1}{1:38:53} \ts{20}{1}{1:39:16} \ts{20}{1}{1:39:38} \ts{20}{1}{1:39:59} \ts{20}{1}{1:40:20} \ts{20}{1}{1:40:41} \ts{20}{1}{1:41:03}
\end{example}
The conclusion is ``a little bit mysterious'': these statements can be proven, but it is not so simple to study the obstructions, so these examples are not easily checked; nevertheless they are facts, and this is the key theorem that tells whether or not one can restrict the group \ts{20}{1}{1:41:03} \ts{20}{1}{1:41:23}. Given how often one does this in special relativity, and in general relativity in order to define spinors, where one chooses $SL(2,\C)$ instead of $SO(1,3)$, in four dimensions, this is an important theorem. There are manifolds that carry a Lorentzian metric and still the corresponding frame bundle has no ``cross-section with $SL(2,\C)$'': such manifolds are Lorentzian but cannot carry spinors. So not every smooth manifold carries a Lorentzian metric, and not every Lorentzian manifold, even if one has made that hurdle, carries spinors: these are the important questions of whether a manifold has a \emph{spin structure}, and so admits the definition of fermions or not \ts{20}{1}{1:41:23} \ts{20}{1}{1:41:44} \ts{20}{1}{1:42:04}.
\begin{coursenote}[Facts behind the examples]
Not said in class: (1) the Riemannian case is the construction of a metric by a partition of unity, which is why a section of $LM/SO(d)$ always exists; a section of $LM/O(1,d-1)$ is the same as a Lorentzian metric. (2) For a \emph{compact} manifold a Lorentzian metric exists if and only if the Euler characteristic vanishes, so $S^2$ is excluded, which is again the hairy ball theorem of \cref{ex:pb-S2}. (3) A spin structure is not a section of a quotient $LM/SL(2,\C)$ ($SL(2,\C)$ is not a subgroup of $GL(4,\R)$); it is a lift of the orthonormal frame bundle through the double cover $SL(2,\C)\to SO^+(1,3)$, a principal bundle map of the type of \cref{sec:pb-pbmap}. The lecture's wording at this point is informal. \ts{20}{1}{1:41:44}
\end{coursenote}
