\section{Lie groups}\label{sec:ll-groups}

Today a topic of the utmost importance in theoretical physics and for the further development of differential geometry: Lie theory, the last big chapter of the course. From the point of view of differential geometry one starts with \emph{Lie groups}; to every Lie group there is an associated \emph{Lie algebra}, which one may have met in physics. So: what is a Lie group? One has certainly met several without knowing it
\ts{13}{1}{0:08} \ts{13}{1}{0:44}.

A \textbf{group} $(G,\bullet)$ is a set $G$ with an operation $\bullet\colon G\times G\to G$ (``blob'') which is associative, has a neutral element $e$ with $e\bullet g=g$, and an inverse $g^{-1}$ for every $g$ with $g\bullet g^{-1}=e$. Only sometimes there is also a commutative law: then the group is \textbf{commutative} or \textbf{abelian} (synonyms). Nothing spectacular so far \ts{13}{1}{1:10} \ts{13}{1}{1:43} \ts{13}{1}{2:16}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: Lie group]\label{def:ll-lie-group}
A \textbf{Lie group} is a triple $(G,\mathcal O,\mathcal A)$ [the lecture writes ``$G$ with $\bullet$''] such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] $(G,\bullet)$ is a group;
  \item[(ii)] the underlying set $G$ is a smooth manifold (a topology and a maximal smooth atlas are given);
  \item[(iii)] the two maps encoding the group structure are smooth: the multiplication $\mu\colon G\times G\to G$, $(g_1,g_2)\mapsto g_1\bullet g_2$, and the inversion $i\colon G\to G$, $g\mapsto g^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
Here $G\times G$ is the product manifold (\cref{def:mf-product}): the Cartesian product of the topological manifolds inherits a smooth atlas from that of $G$, so it is again a smooth manifold and smoothness of $\mu$ makes sense \ts{13}{1}{2:45} \ts{13}{1}{3:23} \ts{13}{1}{4:00} \ts{13}{1}{4:29} \ts{13}{1}{5:00} \ts{13}{1}{5:34} \ts{13}{1}{5:59}.
\end{definition}
It is to be expected: when a group has extra structure on its underlying set one wants a compatibility condition between the two; here the multiplication, and the inversion which is used a lot, are required to be smooth. The name comes from Sophus Lie, who studied continuous groups of transformations (as said in class: not checked).

\begin{example}[The translation group $(\R^n,+)$]\label{ex:ll-translation}
Take $G=\R^n$ as a smooth manifold (standard topology and atlas understood) and as group operation the componentwise addition. The inverse is taking the opposite vector, and both operations are smooth: a \emph{commutative} Lie group, sometimes called the $n$-dimensional \textbf{translation group} \ts{13}{1}{6:24} \ts{13}{1}{6:51} \ts{13}{1}{7:29}.
\end{example}
\begin{aside}[Momenta generate translations]
In quantum mechanics the momentum operator generates translations of the wave function; in classical mechanics the Poisson bracket $\{p,\cdot\}$ with a momentum generates translations of observables. These generators belong to the \emph{Lie algebra} of the group, as will turn out; what the group itself does, if one exponentiates a finite amount, is to shift. The lecture stresses that here no action on a space is considered: one just composes translations, shifting first in one direction and then in the other, or the other way round \ts{13}{1}{7:58} \ts{13}{1}{8:20} \ts{13}{1}{8:48}.
\end{aside}

\begin{example}[The circle group $S^1=U(1)$]\label{ex:ll-u1}
Let $G=S^1=\{z\in\C\mid |z|=1\}$, the unit circle in the complex plane, with the multiplication of complex numbers as operation. Multiplying two numbers of modulus one multiplies the radii (both $1$) and adds the angles: $e^{i\varphi}e^{i\psi}=e^{i(\varphi+\psi)}$. Every element has an inverse, so this is again a commutative Lie group, usually called $U(1)$. It is the group under which one wants wave functions to be invariant, at least the final results of the calculations: ``one does not want an overall phase to count''
\ts{13}{1}{9:14} \ts{13}{1}{9:45} \ts{13}{1}{10:15} \ts{13}{1}{10:44} \ts{13}{1}{11:13}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The general linear group $GL(n,\R)$]\label{ex:ll-gl}
Let $GL(n,\R)$ be the set of all linear maps $\R^n\to\R^n$ that are \emph{invertible}, i.e.\ have non-vanishing determinant, with the composition of maps as operation: the identity map is the neutral element, and the inverse exists because the determinant is non-zero. This is \emph{not} commutative ($\varphi\circ\psi\ne\psi\circ\varphi$ in general): the \textbf{general linear group}, a Lie group \ts{13}{1}{11:55} \ts{13}{1}{12:22} \ts{13}{1}{12:51}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[Smoothness, not proved]
That $GL(n,\R)$ is a smooth manifold is the subject of the question below; that the composition (matrix multiplication, polynomial in the entries) and the inversion (Cramer's rule: rational in the entries with non-vanishing denominator) are smooth is not said in class. 
\end{coursenote}

One more example, because it occurs so often \ts{13}{1}{13:35}. Let $V$ be a $d$-dimensional $\R$-vector space with a \textbf{pseudo-inner product}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: pseudo-inner product]\label{def:ll-pseudo}
A \textbf{pseudo-inner product} on $V$ is a map $(\cdot,\cdot)\colon V\times V\to\R$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(1)] it is bilinear;
  \item[(2)] it is symmetric: $(v,w)=(w,v)$ for all $v,w\in V$;
  \item[(3)] it is \textbf{non-degenerate}: if $(v,w)=0$ for all $w\in V$, then $v=0$.
\end{enumerate}
A stronger requirement replaces (3) by (3$'$) \textbf{positive definiteness}: $(v,v)\ge0$ for all $v$, and $(v,v)=0$ implies $v=0$. With (3$'$) the map is an \textbf{inner product}; the ``pseudo'' is owed to having weakened positive definiteness to non-degeneracy \ts{13}{1}{14:21} \ts{13}{1}{14:57} \ts{13}{1}{15:29} \ts{13}{1}{15:58} \ts{13}{1}{16:33} \ts{13}{1}{17:07}.
\end{definition}
By Sylvester's classification of symmetric bilinear maps there is a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of the pseudo-inner product is diagonal with $p$ entries $+1$ followed by $q$ entries $-1$: the pair $(p,q)$ is the \textbf{signature}, with $p+q=d$. Up to isomorphism there are only as many pseudo-inner products on $V$ as there are signatures. Positive definiteness means signature $(d,0)$; in relativity one needs $(1,d-1)$, which is why the general case is introduced \ts{13}{1}{17:34} \ts{13}{1}{18:13} \ts{13}{1}{18:44} \ts{13}{1}{19:16}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the orthogonal group $O(p,q)$]\label{def:ll-opq}
$O(p,q)$ is the set of all linear maps $\varphi\colon V\to V$ that leave the pseudo-inner product invariant,
\[
  (\varphi(v),\varphi(w))=(v,w)\qquad\text{for all }v,w\in V,
\]
with $V=\R^{p+q}$. It is a subgroup of $GL(p+q,\R)$ (the group operation inherited is the composition), generically not commutative: the \textbf{orthogonal group} with respect to the pseudo-inner product, a Lie group. For instance $O(1,3)$ is the \textbf{Lorentz group} of relativity and $O(3,0)$ the \textbf{rotation group} (the lecture says ``$O(3,0)$ is just the rotation group''; strictly the rotations are its connected part $SO(3)$ [?]) \ts{13}{1}{19:54} \ts{13}{1}{20:22} \ts{13}{1}{21:01} \ts{13}{1}{21:38}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Smoothness of $O(p,q)$, and a slip]
The lecture calls $O(p,q)$ a Lie group without proof; that it is a smooth submanifold of $GL(p+q,\R)$ (a closed subgroup) is a theorem not given in class. Also: the lecture writes $GL(p+q,\R)$ and ``the R vector space'' for $V$, meaning $V=\R^{p+q}$ with its fixed pseudo-inner product. \ts{13}{1}{20:22}
\end{coursenote}

All this was seen before, but is now recognised as Lie groups. The general linear group was already in action, hidden, in differential geometry: at a point of a manifold, under a change of coordinates, the components of a vector transform with an invertible linear matrix (\cref{ex:tv-change}), an action of $GL(n,\R)$. It will come back with the frame bundle and principal bundles; and the promised bundle definition of a \emph{tensor density} (\cref{app:te-density}) needs this language of principal bundles and associated vector bundles, with Lie groups playing a very important role \ts{13}{1}{22:23} \ts{13}{1}{22:53}.

\begin{answer}{Why is $GL(n,\R)$ a manifold? And what about the angles on the rotation group?}[$GL(n,\R)$ as an open subset]
Linear maps $\R^n\to\R^n$ are represented by $n^2$ numbers, their components, so the set of all of them is $\R^{n^2}$, a smooth manifold we know how to construct. $GL(n,\R)$ is this space without the matrices of determinant zero. But $\det\ne0$ is an \emph{open condition}: what remains is an \emph{open subset} of $\R^{n^2}$. An open subset of a smooth manifold inherits charts (the induced charts) and topology, and is again a smooth manifold (an open submanifold). So the group is a manifold. \ts{13}{1}{23:28} \ts{13}{1}{24:04} \ts{13}{1}{24:35}\par
For the rotation group one often meets the parametrisation by three angles, with the problem that at $\theta\to0$ the other angles lose their meaning (one ``should not look at that point''). But a group that is a manifold is covered by charts and has no single chart covering everything; the three angles are one chart covering a part of the group, and the bad point is no problem: another chart covers it, and nothing is special about it. Choosing charts one returns to the parametrisation by angles one always saw. The lecturer will see whether to put a problem on the sheet \ts{13}{1}{25:00} \ts{13}{1}{25:28} \ts{13}{1}{25:58} \ts{13}{1}{26:26}.
\end{answer}

\section{Left translations and left-invariant vector fields}\label{sec:ll-left}

Next: the Lie algebra of a Lie group. Some preparation is needed .
\begin{definition}[New concepts: left translation]\label{def:ll-left}
Let $(G,\bullet)$ be a Lie group. For every $g\in G$ the \textbf{left translation} by $g$ is the map
\[
  L_g\colon G\to G,\qquad L_g(h):=g\bullet h.
\]
It does nothing but fix the first factor of the multiplication $\mu$ and see how it acts on any other element \ts{13}{1}{27:04} \ts{13}{1}{27:40} \ts{13}{1}{28:13}.
\end{definition}
Each $L_g$ is a bijection \ts{13}{1}{28:44}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item injective: if $L_g(h)=L_g(h')$ then $g\bullet h=g\bullet h'$, and multiplying from the left by $g^{-1}$ gives $h=h'$ \ts{13}{1}{29:23} \ts{13}{1}{29:52};
  \item surjective: for any $h\in G$ one has $L_g(g^{-1}\bullet h)=g\bullet g^{-1}\bullet h=h$ by associativity \ts{13}{1}{30:24} \ts{13}{1}{30:58}.
\end{itemize}
It is \emph{not} a group isomorphism: $L_g(h_1\bullet h_2)=g\bullet h_1\bullet h_2$, while $L_g(h_1)\bullet L_g(h_2)=g\bullet h_1\bullet g\bullet h_2$, and these differ in general \ts{13}{1}{31:26} \ts{13}{1}{32:00} \ts{13}{1}{32:43}. But it is a \textbf{diffeomorphism} of the underlying manifold $G$ onto itself \ts{13}{1}{33:27}.
\begin{coursenote}[Why $L_g$ is a diffeomorphism]
The lecture states it without argument. It follows from the smoothness of $\mu$: $L_g=\mu(g,\cdot)$ is the composition of $h\mapsto(g,h)$ (smooth) with $\mu$, and its inverse is $L_{g^{-1}}$, smooth for the same reason. Not said in class. In the lecture ``not a Lie algebra isomorphism'' means ``not a group isomorphism''. 
\end{coursenote}

\begin{recall}[Pull-back and push-forward of fields]
The pull-back, defined for covectors at a point, extends to covector fields by doing it at every point; \cref{def:gr-pullback} for forms. The push-forward $h_*$ of \cref{def:tb-push} is defined on vectors. 
\end{recall}
\emph{Why does this matter?} One cannot do the same for vector fields: the push-forward of a vector cannot be extended to a push-forward of a vector field, \emph{unless the underlying smooth map is a diffeomorphism} \ts{13}{1}{34:23} \ts{13}{1}{34:53} \ts{13}{1}{35:50}. The reason is worth making a point of, rather than hiding it in the formalism. Let $h\colon M\to N$ be smooth. A map assigns to \emph{every} point of $M$ exactly \emph{one} point of $N$. For the pull-back of a covector field $\omega$ on $N$ this is fantastic: at each point $p\in M$ one looks at $\omega$ at $h(p)$ and pulls it back; the values of $\omega$ outside the image are simply never seen. For the push-forward of a vector field $X$ on $M$ two problems arise:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item if $h$ is not surjective, no vector is defined at the points of $N$ outside the image, since no point of $M$ is mapped there;
  \item if $h$ is not injective, two different points of $M$ may go to the same point of $N$, and the vectors pushed there from the two points differ in general: restricting to the image does not help, the result is ill-defined because one does not know which of the images to choose.
\end{enumerate}
In the direction of the map one needs everything mapped to exactly one value; going the other way one needs injectivity and surjectivity, i.e.\ bijectivity, and then an inverse exists. So one needs a bijection that is smooth in both directions: a diffeomorphism \ts{13}{1}{36:20} \ts{13}{1}{36:45} \ts{13}{1}{37:25} \ts{13}{1}{37:49} \ts{13}{1}{38:24} \ts{13}{1}{38:51} \ts{13}{1}{39:21} \ts{13}{1}{39:44} \ts{13}{1}{40:14} \ts{13}{1}{40:44}.

On a Lie group the left translations \emph{are} diffeomorphisms, so they can be used to push forward vector fields on $G$ to vector fields on $G$. One considers vector fields on the group manifold (``why on earth would you do that?'': it will turn out to be the key to the Lie algebra) \ts{13}{1}{41:14} \ts{13}{1}{41:40}.
\begin{definition}[Push-forward of a vector field along $L_g$]\label{def:ll-push-field}
For a smooth vector field $X$ on $G$ and $g\in G$ define the vector field $(L_g)_*X$ pointwise by
\[
  \bigl((L_g)_*X\bigr)_{g\bullet h}:=(L_g)_*\bigl(X_h\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }h\in G,
\]
that is, the vector $X_h\in T_hG$ is pushed forward to a vector in $T_{gh}G$ and placed at the point $gh$. This is the push-forward of a vector field defined pointwise \ts{13}{1}{42:26} \ts{13}{1}{42:51} \ts{13}{1}{43:23} \ts{13}{1}{44:02}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Point of evaluation]
The lecture first says ``the point $h$'' for the left-hand side and then corrects to $gh$; the formula above has it. \ts{13}{1}{42:51}
\end{coursenote}

One is not interested in all vector fields but in a special class: those which, when pushed forward, \emph{look the same} as before. Intuition in the plane $(\R^2,+)$: a homogeneous vector field, pushed forward along an addition (a shift in some direction), looks the same: it is invariant under the push-forward induced by the left translations. The analogue on any Lie group is the following \ts{13}{1}{44:29} \ts{13}{1}{44:56} \ts{13}{1}{45:23}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: left-invariant vector field]\label{def:ll-left-inv}
Let $(G,\bullet)$ be a Lie group. A vector field $X\in\Gamma(TG)$ is \textbf{left-invariant} if for every $g\in G$
\[
  (L_g)_*X=X.
\]
Nothing changes under the push-forward induced by left translation  \ts{13}{1}{46:22} \ts{13}{1}{46:57}.
\end{definition}
There are three equivalent formulations :
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] the vector field equation $(L_g)_*X=X$ for all $g$;
  \item[(b)] the pointwise vector equation: for all $g,h\in G$
  \[
    (L_g)_*\bigl(X_h\bigr)=X_{g\bullet h},
  \]
  which is (a) written at each point, since the push-forward of $X_h$ is a vector at $gh$ and must coincide with the value of the field there \ts{13}{1}{47:37} \ts{13}{1}{48:07} \ts{13}{1}{48:35};
  \item[(c)] for all $f\in C^\infty(G)$:
  \[
    X(f\circ L_g)=\bigl(Xf\bigr)\circ L_g.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\begin{proof}[Proof of the equivalence of (b) and (c)]
Equal vectors act equally on every smooth function, so (b) is equivalent to
\[
  \bigl((L_g)_*X_h\bigr)f=X_{gh}\,f\quad\forall f,\,h.
\]
By the definition of the push-forward (\cref{def:tb-push}) the left side is $X_h(f\circ L_g)$. On the right one may let the whole field act and then evaluate at $gh$. Then
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  (X(f\circ L_g))(h)&=X_h(f\circ L_g)\\
  &=(Xf)(g\bullet h)\\
  &=\bigl((Xf)\circ L_g\bigr)(h),
  \end{aligned}
\]
and since it holds for all $h$ this is an identity of functions, i.e.\ (c). \ts{13}{1}{49:02} \ts{13}{1}{49:36} \ts{13}{1}{50:17} \ts{13}{1}{50:43} \ts{13}{1}{51:22} \ts{13}{1}{51:56}
\end{proof}
So a left-invariant vector field acts on $f\circ L_g$ by acting only on $f$ and then composing with $L_g$, ``ignoring'' $L_g$: it pulls out. A general vector field would need some chain rule here, which one would not even know how to write; for a left-invariant one it is simple \ts{13}{1}{52:24} \ts{13}{1}{52:54}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $L(G)$]\label{def:ll-LG}
The set of all left-invariant vector fields on $G$ is denoted $L(G)$. So far it is only a set: a subset of $\Gamma(TG)$, the sections of the tangent bundle, the vector fields with a particular property \ts{13}{1}{53:22} \ts{13}{1}{54:01} \ts{13}{1}{54:25}.
\end{definition}
Is it a sub-module of the $C^\infty(G)$-module $\Gamma(TG)$? One has to check closure under addition and under multiplication with smooth functions; the former is clear (the push-forward is linear). The lecture then argues: the push-forward is also $C^\infty$-linear ``because the $k$ pulls out'', hence $L(G)$ is a $C^\infty(G)$-module; and recalls that $\Gamma(TM)$ need not have a basis (the $S^2$ has no non-vanishing vector field), so that the question of dimension is open: as an $\R$-vector space it is infinite-dimensional, while $L(G)$ will turn out to be finite-dimensional \ts{13}{1}{54:57} \ts{13}{1}{55:20} \ts{13}{1}{55:45} \ts{13}{1}{56:12} \ts{13}{1}{56:38} \ts{13}{1}{57:18} \ts{13}{1}{57:45}.
\begin{coursenote}[An error: $L(G)$ is not a $C^\infty(G)$-submodule]
The step ``the push-forward is $C^\infty$-linear, so $L(G)$ is closed under multiplication by functions'' is wrong. At a point, $(L_g)_*(kX)_{h}$ gives $k(h)\,(L_g)_*X_h$, placed at the point $gh$, whereas $(kX)_{gh}=k(gh)X_{gh}$: the function $k$ is evaluated at \emph{different} points, so $kX$ is left-invariant only if $k$ is constant ($k\circ L_g=k$ for all $g$, so constant on the connected component of $e$ [?]). Only multiplication by constants is allowed: $L(G)$ is just an $\R$-vector subspace of $\Gamma(TG)$ (closed under real linear combinations), which is all one needs and consistent with the later statement that it is finite-dimensional (a non-zero $C^\infty$-module of vector fields could not be, for $\dim G\ge1$). Not said in class (the lecturer's conclusion, ``finite dimensional as an $\R$-vector space'', is right). \ts{13}{1}{55:45} \ts{13}{1}{56:38}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Abstract Lie algebras}\label{sec:ll-algebra}

Before going on, some terminology that does not depend on a Lie group: an \emph{abstract} Lie algebra \ts{13}{1}{58:10} \ts{13}{1}{58:43}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: abstract Lie algebra]\label{def:ll-lie-algebra}
An \textbf{abstract Lie algebra} $(L,+,\cdot,[\![\cdot,\cdot]\!])$ is a $K$-vector space $L$ ($K=\R$ or $\C$, it does not matter) equipped with a \textbf{Lie bracket}, a bilinear map $[\![\cdot,\cdot]\!]\colon L\times L\to L$ (two elements go to an element of $L$) such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] it is antisymmetric: $[\![x,y]\!]=-[\![y,x]\!]$ for all $x,y\in L$;
  \item[(ii)] it satisfies the \textbf{Jacobi identity}: for all $x,y,z\in L$
  \[
    [\![x,[\![y,z]\!]]\!]+[\![y,[\![z,x]\!]]\!]+[\![z,[\![x,y]\!]]\!]=0.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
To memorise the Jacobi identity: write the same pattern three times and \emph{cyclically permute} $x,y,z$, pushing everything left, what falls off on the left coming in from the right; the sum is zero. By bilinearity, antisymmetry would be implied by (and for char.\ $\ne2$ is equivalent to) the requirement $[\![x,x]\!]=0$  \ts{13}{1}{59:10} \ts{13}{1}{59:52} \ts{13}{1}{1:00:21} \ts{13}{1}{1:00:55}.
\end{definition}
(The double bracket only distinguishes this abstract bracket from the commutator of vector fields.)

\begin{example}[All vector fields form an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra]\label{ex:ll-vf-algebra}
Let $M$ be any smooth (real) manifold and $L=\Gamma(TM)$, an $\R$-vector space (even a $C^\infty(M)$-module, but the weaker structure suffices). As abstract Lie bracket take the commutator of vector fields (\cref{def:gr-commutator}). It is $\R$-bilinear (``bilinear'' here refers to the vector space structure, so it means $\R$-linearity, not $C^\infty$-linearity, which it does not have: $[X,fY]\ne f[X,Y]$), antisymmetric, obvious from the definition, and it satisfies the Jacobi identity (as in \cref{ex:tv-lie,ex:tv-jacobi}). So $(\Gamma(TM),[\cdot,\cdot])$ is an \emph{infinite-dimensional} Lie algebra, now no longer abstract but concretely constructed \ts{13}{1}{1:01:25} \ts{13}{1}{1:01:53} \ts{13}{1}{1:02:27} \ts{13}{1}{1:02:55} \ts{13}{1}{1:03:17} \ts{13}{1}{1:03:50}.
\end{example}

\begin{answer}{Why talk about $\R$-vector space when it is a $C^\infty$-module?}[Modules and vector spaces]
A $C^\infty(M)$-module can be scaled with any smooth function, in particular with the constant functions, which mimic multiplication by real numbers. So being closed under constant functions is a \emph{weaker} requirement than being closed under all functions, and every $C^\infty(M)$-module over a real manifold is in particular an $\R$-vector space: restrict the ring to the constants, which is a field. The implication goes only this way: an $\R$-vector space need not be scalable by functions. In the lecture ``this is lots of terminology about very little'': for the vector fields one checks directly that they can be added and scaled by real numbers and that the vector space axioms hold, with no need to go through the module  \ts{13}{1}{1:04:24} \ts{13}{1}{1:04:51} \ts{13}{1}{1:05:21} \ts{13}{1}{1:05:46} \ts{13}{1}{1:06:35} \ts{13}{1}{1:07:12} \ts{13}{1}{1:07:42}.
\end{answer}

\section{$L(G)$ is a Lie algebra}\label{sec:ll-sub}

\begin{theorem}[$L(G)$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\Gamma(TG)$]\label{thm:ll-subalgebra}
The set $L(G)$ of left-invariant vector fields, equipped with the commutator bracket of vector fields, is a Lie subalgebra of $\bigl(\Gamma(TG),[\cdot,\cdot]\bigr)$  \ts{13}{1}{1:08:17} \ts{13}{1}{1:08:49}.
\end{theorem}
One knows $L(G)\subset\Gamma(TG)$ and that it is an $\R$-subspace; the bracket is just the restricted one. The question is whether the bracket \emph{closes} on $L(G)$: the commutator of two left-invariant fields is a vector field, but a priori the bracket could destroy left-invariance \ts{13}{1}{1:09:16}.
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:ll-subalgebra}]
Let $X,Y\in L(G)$. By formulation (c) of \cref{def:ll-left-inv} it suffices to show $[X,Y](f\circ L_g)=\bigl([X,Y]f\bigr)\circ L_g$ for all $f$ and $g$. By the definition of the commutator
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  [X,Y](f\circ L_g)&=X\bigl(Y(f\circ L_g)\bigr)\\
  &\quad-Y\bigl(X(f\circ L_g)\bigr)\\
  &=X\bigl((Yf)\circ L_g\bigr)\\
  &\quad-Y\bigl((Xf)\circ L_g\bigr)\\
  &=\bigl(X(Yf)\bigr)\circ L_g\\
  &\quad-\bigl(Y(Xf)\bigr)\circ L_g\\
  &=\bigl([X,Y]f\bigr)\circ L_g.
  \end{aligned}
\]
The second equality uses that $Y$ (resp.\ $X$) is left-invariant. In the third, $Yf$ (resp.\ $Xf$) is again a function, so the left-invariance of $X$ (resp.\ $Y$) can be used a second time. Hence $[X,Y]\in L(G)$ \ts{13}{1}{1:09:54} \ts{13}{1}{1:10:42} \ts{13}{1}{1:11:10} \ts{13}{1}{1:11:46} \ts{13}{1}{1:12:16} \ts{13}{1}{1:12:56} \ts{13}{1}{1:13:28} \ts{13}{1}{1:14:18}.
\end{proof}
So the left-invariant vector fields close under the Lie bracket and $L(G)$ is a Lie subalgebra of the infinite-dimensional $\Gamma(TG)$. The question is its dimension: is it also infinite-dimensional \ts{13}{1}{1:14:58} \ts{13}{1}{1:15:28}?

\begin{theorem}[$L(G)\cong T_eG$]\label{thm:ll-iso}
Let $G$ be a Lie group with neutral element $e$. The vector space $L(G)$ of left-invariant vector fields is isomorphic, as a vector space, to the tangent space $T_eG$ to the group at the identity  \ts{13}{1}{1:16:02} \ts{13}{1}{1:16:25}.
\end{theorem}
So there are as many left-invariant vector fields as there are tangent vectors at the identity. The immediate consequence:
\begin{corollary}[Dimension]\label{cor:ll-dim}
$\dim_{\R}L(G)=\dim T_eG=\dim G$, the dimension of the underlying manifold; in particular $L(G)$ is finite-dimensional if the manifold is \ts{13}{1}{1:16:57} \ts{13}{1}{1:17:24}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:ll-iso}]
One has to construct a linear isomorphism $j\colon T_eG\to L(G)$ (lecture: ``$J$''). For $A\in T_eG$ define the vector field $j(A)$ by
\[
  j(A)_g:=(L_g)_*A\in T_gG,\qquad g\in G,
\]
i.e.\ push $A$ forward with all possible $L_g$ and so obtain a vector everywhere. \ts{13}{1}{1:17:55} \ts{13}{1}{1:18:19} \ts{13}{1}{1:18:51} \ts{13}{1}{1:19:18} \ts{13}{1}{1:19:46} \ts{13}{1}{1:20:18}\par
(1) \emph{$j(A)$ is left-invariant} (here $j(A)$ is a vector field: smooth by smoothness of $\mu$ [?, not said]). Use formulation (b): for $h,g\in G$
\[
  (L_h)_*\bigl(j(A)_g\bigr)=(L_h)_*(L_g)_*A=(L_{hg})_*A=j(A)_{hg}.
\]
The middle step: for any vector $v$ and $f$, using the definition of the push-forward twice,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \bigl((L_h)_*(L_g)_*v\bigr)f&=\bigl((L_g)_*v\bigr)(f\circ L_h)\\
  &=v\bigl(f\circ L_h\circ L_g\bigr)\\
  &=v\bigl(f\circ L_{hg}\bigr),
  \end{aligned}
\]
which is $((L_{hg})_*v)f$, because $L_h\circ L_g=L_{hg}$. \ts{13}{1}{1:20:43} \ts{13}{1}{1:21:10} \ts{13}{1}{1:21:43} \ts{13}{1}{1:22:27} \ts{13}{1}{1:22:57} \ts{13}{1}{1:23:29} \ts{13}{1}{1:23:52} \ts{13}{1}{1:24:29} \ts{13}{1}{1:24:59}\par
(2) \emph{$j$ is linear}: $(L_g)_*$ is $\R$-linear, so scalars pull out and sums go to sums: $j(\lambda A+B)=\lambda j(A)+j(B)$. \ts{13}{1}{1:25:27} \ts{13}{1}{1:25:54} \ts{13}{1}{1:26:17} \ts{13}{1}{1:26:42}\par
(3) \emph{$j$ is injective}: if $j(A)=j(B)$, they agree at every point, in particular at $e$: $(L_e)_*A=(L_e)_*B$, and since $L_e=\mathrm{id}$ this is $A=B$. \ts{13}{1}{1:27:13} \ts{13}{1}{1:27:44} \ts{13}{1}{1:28:14} \ts{13}{1}{1:28:46}\par
(4) \emph{$j$ is surjective}: let $X\in L(G)$ be arbitrary and put $A_X:=X_e\in T_eG$. Then
\[
  j(A_X)_g=(L_g)_*X_e=X_{g\bullet e}=X_g
\]
by formulation (b) of left-invariance, so $j(A_X)=X$. \ts{13}{1}{1:29:25} \ts{13}{1}{1:30:10} \ts{13}{1}{1:30:53} \ts{13}{1}{1:31:17} \ts{13}{1}{1:31:49} \ts{13}{1}{1:32:21}\par
Hence $T_eG\cong L(G)$ as vector spaces. \ts{13}{1}{1:32:51}
\end{proof}
So left-invariant vector fields, a rather abstract notion, can be understood by looking only at the vectors at the identity, which is much simpler. But $L(G)$ has the additional structure of a Lie algebra.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Lie algebra of a Lie group]\label{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}
$L(G)$ with the commutator is a Lie algebra; the tangent space $T_eG$ is, so far, just a vector space: it cannot inherit the commutator of vector fields, since the latter needs two vector fields (one acts on the other, so the other must vary from point to point), not two vectors. One would like a bracket $[\![\cdot,\cdot]\!]\colon T_eG\times T_eG\to T_eG$ for which $j$ is an isomorphism of Lie algebras:
\[
  j\bigl([\![A,B]\!]\bigr)=[j(A),j(B)].
\]
There is no choice but to \emph{define} it this way:
\[
  [\![A,B]\!]:=j^{-1}\bigl([j(A),j(B)]\bigr),
\]
which makes sense because $[j(A),j(B)]\in L(G)$ (\cref{thm:ll-subalgebra}) and $j^{-1}$ exists (\cref{thm:ll-iso}). Then $(T_eG,[\![\cdot,\cdot]\!])$ is isomorphic as a Lie algebra to $\bigl(L(G),[\cdot,\cdot]\bigr)$, and one can forget the machinery of left-invariant fields and commutators and work with the nice, finite-dimensional (if $G$ is) tangent space at the identity with its ``innocent'' bracket. This is called the \textbf{Lie algebra of the Lie group} $G$  \ts{13}{1}{1:33:25} \ts{13}{1}{1:33:53} \ts{13}{1}{1:34:31} \ts{13}{1}{1:35:14} \ts{13}{1}{1:35:51} \ts{13}{1}{1:36:31} \ts{13}{1}{1:37:01} \ts{13}{1}{1:37:43} \ts{13}{1}{1:38:13} \ts{13}{1}{1:38:51}.
\end{definition}
\begin{aside}[Is it the commutator of quantum mechanics?]
Sometimes one asks whether this bracket is the commutator met in quantum mechanics. In quantum mechanics on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space one also gets commutator brackets. The bracket on $T_eG$ is not a bracket of vector fields (there is no vector field business on a vector space), but for the Lie algebra of a Lie group it ultimately comes from the commutator of vector fields on the left-invariant fields. ``Lots of machinery, but essentially it is all encapsulated here'' \ts{13}{1}{1:39:28} \ts{13}{1}{1:39:55}.
\end{aside}

\section{Outlook}\label{sec:ll-outlook}

Next time one looks more closely at this Lie algebra and defines the \textbf{Killing form}: not a form in the earlier sense (it is not antisymmetric) but a \emph{symmetric} bilinear map; it says much about the type of Lie algebra, and then Lie algebras can be classified. For instance the \emph{semi-simple} Lie algebras can be fully classified, a classic result, ``by Cartan or so'' (as said in class: not checked), by means of \textbf{Dynkin diagrams}, very beautiful mathematics and important in physics in many places (\cref{ch:l14})  \ts{13}{1}{1:40:23}.

Then the opposite question: now one starts with the Lie group and gets the Lie algebra, but fix an abstract Lie algebra: does a unique Lie group correspond to it? The answer is \emph{no}: generically several Lie groups have the same Lie algebra. There is also the issue of complex algebras and groups versus real ones: the complex algebras are classified very easily, but one is often interested in the real ones, and there are many details and pitfalls; the aim is to be able, whenever one meets a Lie group or algebra, to know where the issue is \ts{13}{1}{1:40:52} \ts{13}{1}{1:41:28} \ts{13}{1}{1:42:00}.

Finally: how does a Lie group \emph{act} on a manifold (the rotation group whirling around the points of some other manifold)? It is more how physicists think: there is a group, and it acts on something. That will play an important role later in bundle theory, with principal bundles. Lie groups are so important structurally, and for several applications to come, that the lecture should be revised and studied in detail \ts{13}{1}{1:42:25} \ts{13}{1}{1:42:52}.
