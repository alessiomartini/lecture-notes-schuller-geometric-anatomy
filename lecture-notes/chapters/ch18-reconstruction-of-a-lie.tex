\section{Integral curves and completeness}\label{sec:rc-curves}

The lecture finally completes the discussion of Lie groups. So far one started from a Lie group $G$ and studied its Lie algebra, first as the set of all left-invariant vector fields (``because we went from the group to the vector fields we invoked some differentiation of sorts'') \ts{18}{1}{0:08} \ts{18}{1}{0:28}. Today's question: why are these fields so interesting? If one wants to study Lie groups, one can derive them from there, but what does one learn about the group once one knows the algebra? So the question is how to go \emph{back up} \ts{18}{1}{0:48}. The result can be anticipated: \emph{at least in a neighbourhood of the identity one can fully recover every Lie group from its Lie algebra}; how big this neighbourhood is depends and will be looked at; and the going up is achieved by the \textbf{exponential map} \ts{18}{1}{1:09} \ts{18}{1}{1:29}. The section is ``Recovering (some, maybe all) of the Lie group from its Lie algebra'' \ts{18}{1}{1:50}.

Down there one has the isomorphism with the tangent space at the identity (\cref{thm:ll-iso}), but to understand the exponential map one must invoke the left-invariant vector fields again \ts{18}{1}{2:11}. First a definition ``that we could have brought much earlier''.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: integral curve]\label{def:rc-integral}
Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $Y$ a smooth vector field on $M$. A smooth curve $\gamma\colon(a,b)\to M$, with $a<b$ real numbers, is an \textbf{integral curve} of $Y$ if for every parameter value $\lambda\in(a,b)$ the tangent vector to the curve at the point $\gamma(\lambda)$ is the vector of the field at the very same point:
\[
  \dot\gamma(\lambda)=Y_{\gamma(\lambda)}\qquad(\text{the tangent vector }\dot\gamma\text{ as in \cref{ch:l10}}).
\]
(The name: the curve is obtained by ``integrating'' the field, that is, by solving the differential equation $\dot\gamma=Y\circ\gamma$.) \ts{18}{1}{2:32} \ts{18}{1}{2:54} \ts{18}{1}{3:16} \ts{18}{1}{3:36} \ts{18}{1}{3:57}
\end{definition}
It is clear that there is a \emph{unique} integral curve through each point of the manifold; this follows from the local existence and uniqueness of solutions of ordinary differential equations: one goes to an arbitrary point, where there is a vector, and finds a curve whose tangent vectors coincide with the vectors already given; locally around the point, if the field is smooth (this is important) it is unique \ts{18}{1}{4:20} \ts{18}{1}{4:40} \ts{18}{1}{5:01} \ts{18}{1}{5:21} \ts{18}{1}{5:44}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: complete integral curve]\label{def:rc-complete}
An integral curve $\gamma$ of a vector field $Y$ is called \textbf{complete} if its domain (the parameter range) can be extended to all of $\R$. A vector field is called complete if all its integral curves are. \ts{18}{1}{6:05} \ts{18}{1}{6:26} \ts{18}{1}{6:48} \ts{18}{1}{7:08}
\end{definition}
This looks at first sight not so important, but it plays a big role also in the singularity theorems of general relativity. There is a result: on a \emph{compact} manifold, every (smooth) vector field is complete; only on a non-compact manifold this need not be the case \ts{18}{1}{7:29} \ts{18}{1}{7:49}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: an incomplete field]
On $M=\R$ the field $Y=x^2\partial_x$ has the integral curve $\gamma(\lambda)=1/(1-\lambda)$ through $1$ at $\lambda=0$, which tends to $\infty$ as $\lambda\to1$ and cannot be continued: it is defined only for $\lambda<1$. \ts{18}{1}{10:36}
\end{coursenote}
\textbf{Example: the sphere}, to withstand the temptation to look not closely enough \ts{18}{1}{8:10}. On $S^2$ one cannot comb the sphere (a fact used in the non-existence of bases of the module of vector fields, \cref{ch:l11}), so a smooth vector field must vanish somewhere; near that point it is small and, going away, it may grow. Take an integral curve through a point close to it: it runs towards the zero and the picture suggests that it stops there, so it cannot be complete, because one cannot go on in the same direction (the tangent vector would have to be opposite) \ts{18}{1}{8:31} \ts{18}{1}{8:52} \ts{18}{1}{9:12} \ts{18}{1}{9:33}. But the sphere is compact and the curve \emph{is} complete: one is not talking about the image of the curve but about the parameter. The vectors become shorter and shorter, so the curve must become slower and slower so that its tangent vectors coincide with them, and the zero is reached only at infinite parameter value (``or you could also say you never arrive there'') \ts{18}{1}{9:53} \ts{18}{1}{10:15}. One cannot decide completeness by looking at the point set that is the image of the curve. On a non-compact manifold one can have a curve which comes through a point and for a finite parameter value $b$ cannot be continued any more \ts{18}{1}{10:36} \ts{18}{1}{10:57}.

This was for general smooth manifolds $M$; it is introduced because for the special case of a Lie group the situation is much better, even if the group is non-compact:
\begin{theorem}[Completeness of left-invariant fields]\label{thm:rc-complete}
Every left-invariant vector field on a Lie group $G$ is complete. \ts{18}{1}{11:18} \ts{18}{1}{11:45}
\end{theorem}
The lecture gives no proof (``it is not so difficult to prove, but a little lengthy'') and calls it a key result of Lie theory, ``one of the most important results although it appears so innocent here'': the reason is the construction that follows \ts{18}{1}{12:06} \ts{18}{1}{12:28}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: idea of the proof]
By the local existence theorem there is an $\varepsilon>0$ and an integral curve $\gamma\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to G$ of a left-invariant field $X$ through the identity. Left translations map integral curves of $X$ to integral curves (left-invariance), so the \emph{same} $\varepsilon$ works at every point of $G$. One can then continue $\gamma$ to the interval $(-\frac{3\varepsilon}2,\frac{3\varepsilon}2)$ by $\gamma(\lambda+\frac\varepsilon2):=\gamma(\frac\varepsilon2)\bullet\gamma(\lambda)$ (an integral curve through $\gamma(\frac\varepsilon2)$), and so on to all of $\R$. \ts{18}{1}{11:45}
\end{coursenote}

\section{The exponential map}\label{sec:rc-exp}

Let $A\in T_{\mathbb 1}G$, an element of the tangent space at the identity (``what we decided would be the convenient space where we think the elements of the Lie algebra lie'') \ts{18}{1}{12:52}. It uniquely determines a left-invariant vector field $X^A$ by
\[
  X^A_g:=(L_g)_*A\qquad\text{for }g\in G ,
\]
the push-forward of $A$ by the left translation (\cref{def:ll-left-inv,def:tb-push}): from a single vector in the tangent space at the identity one makes an entire left-invariant vector field on the whole group \ts{18}{1}{13:16} \ts{18}{1}{13:36} \ts{18}{1}{13:58}. Let $\gamma^A\colon\R\to G$ be the integral curve of $X^A$ through the identity, $\gamma^A(0)=\mathbb 1$; it is defined on all of $\R$ because of \cref{thm:rc-complete} (this is the point where completeness is used). So one starts with an $A$ in the tangent space and gets a particular curve through the identity, following the direction of $A$: its derivative at the identity is exactly $A$ \ts{18}{1}{14:20} \ts{18}{1}{14:44} \ts{18}{1}{15:04} \ts{18}{1}{15:25} \ts{18}{1}{15:46}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the exponential map]\label{def:rc-exp}
The \textbf{exponential map} of the Lie group $G$ is
\[
  \exp\colon T_{\mathbb 1}G\to G,\qquad\exp(A):=\gamma^A(1) .
\]
Why $1$: one could put a $\lambda$ in front of $A$ (``because one can scale these vectors''), so it suffices to define it at parameter value $1$; indeed $\exp(\lambda A)=\gamma^A(\lambda)$. (The name is explained below, in the matrix case: there $\exp$ is the exponential power series.) \ts{18}{1}{16:09} \ts{18}{1}{16:30} \ts{18}{1}{16:51} \ts{18}{1}{17:12}
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[A slip, and why $\exp(\lambda A)=\gamma^A(\lambda)$]
The lecture says that $\exp$ ``takes any element from the tangent space at the identity and maps it into the Lie algebra'': it maps it into the \emph{group}, of course (``this is the path we want to go from the algebra up to the Lie group''). Not said in class: since $X^{\lambda A}=\lambda X^A$, the curve $t\mapsto\gamma^A(\lambda t)$ is the integral curve of $X^{\lambda A}$ through $\mathbb 1$, hence $\gamma^{\lambda A}(1)=\gamma^A(\lambda)$. \ts{18}{1}{16:30}
\end{coursenote}
The map has the following properties; ``the theorem comes in several parts'' \ts{18}{1}{17:34}.
\begin{theorem}[Properties of the exponential map]\label{thm:rc-exp}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] $\exp$ is a \emph{local diffeomorphism}: there is a neighbourhood $V$ of the zero vector in $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ such that $\exp|_V\colon V\to\exp(V)\subseteq G$ is bijective, and $\exp|_V$ and $(\exp|_V)^{-1}$ are smooth.
  \item[(ii)] If $G$ is compact, then $\exp$ is surjective.
  \item[(iii)] If $G$ is non-compact, $\exp$ may or may not be surjective, may or may not be injective; it can also be bijective: nothing can be said in general.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
No proofs are given in class \ts{18}{1}{17:56} \ts{18}{1}{18:17} \ts{18}{1}{18:37} \ts{18}{1}{18:59}. Part (i) means: by knowing the Lie algebra one understands the neighbourhood of the identity of the group, ``some neighbourhood, which could be a large one'' \ts{18}{1}{19:21}. Part (ii): for compact groups one gets every group element back by exponentiating \ts{18}{1}{19:48} \ts{18}{1}{20:08}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: why (i), and connectedness in (ii)]
(i) follows from the inverse function theorem: the derivative of $\exp$ at $0$ is the identity of $T_{\mathbb 1}G$, since $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\gamma^A(\lambda)|_0=A$. In (ii) the group must also be \emph{connected}: the image of $\exp$ lies in the connected component of the identity (it is the continuous image of a connected space containing $\mathbb 1$). For example $O(3)$ is compact, but $\exp$ does not reach the elements of determinant $-1$. \ts{18}{1}{19:48}
\end{coursenote}
\textbf{Comparing domain and target for compact $G$} \ts{18}{1}{20:29}. Once one has a surjection one can compare the two spaces: the target $G$ is compact by assumption, the domain, a vector space, is not compact. The lecture argues: a smooth surjective map which were also injective would be a diffeomorphism, hence in particular a homeomorphism and would send a compact space to a compact space, ``so it cannot be injective''; therefore \emph{for compact $G$ the map $\exp$ cannot be injective}: one gets the whole group back, but hits elements (at least) twice \ts{18}{1}{20:53} \ts{18}{1}{21:13} \ts{18}{1}{21:34} \ts{18}{1}{21:54} \ts{18}{1}{22:15}.
\begin{answer}{A continuous bijection non-compact to compact?}[Continuity of $\exp$]
A student asks whether the argument means: if the map is surjective and goes from a non-compact to a compact space (it must be at least continuous). The lecturer: ``of course'', continuity is part of it, the exponential map is smooth. \ts{18}{1}{23:58}
\end{answer}
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: the argument is not complete as stated]
A bijective smooth map need not be a diffeomorphism ($t\mapsto t^3$), and a continuous bijection from a non-compact space onto a compact one exists ($[0,2\pi)\to S^1$). So the shown argument is only a heuristic; the conclusion is nevertheless right for $\dim G>0$, as the circle shows: every compact connected Lie group contains a circle subgroup, along which $\exp$ is periodic. \ts{18}{1}{21:34}
\end{coursenote}
\textbf{Example: the circle} \ts{18}{1}{22:37}. $SO(2)$ is, as a manifold, $S^1$. Let $\mathbb 1$ be the identity and $T_{\mathbb 1}S^1$ the tangent space at it, the real line, which is not compact. Exponentiating a vector $A$ of the tangent line takes one to a point of the circle; doubling the vector, to another one; a sufficiently long vector brings one back to the starting point; an even longer one, to the same points for a second time, and likewise in the negative direction. So $\exp$ is surjective, but each point is hit infinitely often \ts{18}{1}{22:57} \ts{18}{1}{23:17} \ts{18}{1}{23:37}. (In the notation of \cref{sec:rp-group}: the rotation by $\phi$, the matrices $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&\sin\phi\\-\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}$, is $\exp$ of $\phi$ times the generator.)

\textbf{Non-compact groups} \ts{18}{1}{24:18}. If $G$ is non-compact, $\exp$ need not be surjective (a non-result, stated as such): it may now be injective, because one no longer runs into the trouble above, and it may be both, bijective; part (iii) ``says nothing'': it just warns that nothing of what was concluded for compact groups applies \ts{18}{1}{24:43} \ts{18}{1}{25:04}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: $SL(2,\C)$ and $\exp$]
For the group of \cref{ch:l15}, $\exp\colon\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)\to SL(2,\C)$ is not surjective: the matrix $M=-\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$ but is not in the image. If $M=e^{B}$ with $\mathrm{tr}\,B=0$ then $B$ has eigenvalues $\pm\mu$ with $e^{\mu}=-1$, so $\mu\ne0$, the eigenvalues of $B$ are distinct, $B$ is diagonalisable and so would be $M$, which it is not. \ts{18}{1}{24:43}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{exercise}[The circle]\label{ex:rc-s1}
Show that for $G=SO(2)$, with the generator $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ of the algebra, $\exp(\phi E)$ (the power series) is the rotation matrix of \cref{sec:rp-group}, and find all $\phi$ with $\exp(\phi E)=\mathbb 1$. (\cref{exam:ch18}.)
\end{exercise}

\subsection{Physics: the Lorentz algebra and its exponential parametrisation}\label{sec:rc-lorentz}
An interesting non-compact group in physics is the Lorentz group $SO(1,3)$ \ts{18}{1}{25:25}. Choosing a basis $A_1,\dots,A_{\dim G}$ of the tangent space at the identity provides a convenient parametrisation, a coordinatisation, of the Lie group \ts{18}{1}{25:47} \ts{18}{1}{26:08}.

Take $T_{\mathbb 1}SO(1,3)$ (the lecture writes ``$T_e\,SO(1,3)$''). It has a basis of elements $M^{\mu\nu}$ with $\mu,\nu=0,\dots,3$, antisymmetric in the two indices, so six of them, which is right: the tangent space is six-dimensional \ts{18}{1}{26:33} \ts{18}{1}{26:54} \ts{18}{1}{27:15}. They have to satisfy an algebra; the lecturer recalls it ``if I remember correctly'' as a sum of four terms of the type $\eta\,M\pm\eta\,M\mp\eta\,M\mp\eta\,M$ (with the Minkowski metric $\eta$; one only has to distribute the indices correctly, and it can be computed from first principles; ``look it up'') \ts{18}{1}{27:36} \ts{18}{1}{27:57} \ts{18}{1}{28:18}:
\[
  [M^{\mu\nu},M^{\rho\sigma}]=\eta^{\nu\rho}M^{\mu\sigma}-\eta^{\mu\rho}M^{\nu\sigma}-\eta^{\nu\sigma}M^{\mu\rho}+\eta^{\mu\sigma}M^{\nu\rho}
\]
(\emph{not said in class}; the signs depend on conventions [?]). This is the Lorentz algebra. Exponentiating, one does not get all of $SO(1,3)$ but only the proper orthochronous Lorentz group (the transcript has ``autochthonous''; ``please check this again''): ``where you don't have mirroring in the time direction, you keep future-pointing vectors'' \ts{18}{1}{28:39} \ts{18}{1}{29:00} \ts{18}{1}{29:23} \ts{18}{1}{29:45}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: why only a part]
$\exp(T_{\mathbb 1}G)$ lies in the identity component (see above); for $SO(1,3)$ this is the group of Lorentz transformations that preserve the orientation (proper, $\det=1$) and the time direction (orthochronous). Mirror images in space or in time are in other components. \ts{18}{1}{29:23}
\end{coursenote}
Since the $M^{\mu\nu}$ are a basis, every element of the tangent space is a linear combination with six real coefficients $\omega_{\mu\nu}$, $\omega_{\mu\nu}M^{\mu\nu}$ (summed, so each independent pair $\mu<\nu$ counts), and one can exponentiate. In physics one says very often that \emph{these} are the elements of the group: the parametrisation of the group as coordinates on the group manifold \ts{18}{1}{30:05}. Written as a $4\times4$ matrix, the coefficients are antisymmetric: zeros on the diagonal; in the first row the three numbers $\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3$, and in the spatial block the three rotation angles, with the antisymmetric pattern $\varphi_3,-\varphi_2,\dots$; the $\psi$'s are the \emph{boost angles}, the hyperbolic angles which tell the boost in that direction, and the $\varphi$'s the rotation done at the same time, so that the element is a combined boost and rotation \ts{18}{1}{30:26} \ts{18}{1}{30:47} \ts{18}{1}{31:07}. Because one understands these parameters intuitively, as rotations and boosts, this is a very convenient coordinatisation. The basis elements are called the \textbf{generators} of the group: they are nothing but a basis of the algebra, and the group elements are \emph{generated} in this way by the exponential map \ts{18}{1}{31:28}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: antisymmetric or symmetric?]
The matrix is antisymmetric in $(\mu\nu)$ with both indices of the same kind; as a map on $\R^4$, $\omega^\mu{}_\nu=\eta^{\mu\alpha}\omega_{\alpha\nu}$, the boost entries become symmetric and the rotation entries stay antisymmetric. \ts{18}{1}{30:26}
\end{coursenote}
This is the abstract version. If one now \emph{represents} the algebra, a representation $\rho$ of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ on $\R^4$ (\cref{def:rp-rep}), determined on a basis by linearity, each $\rho(M^{\mu\nu})$ is a $4\times4$ matrix $(\rho(M^{\mu\nu}))^a{}_b$ with $a,b$ running over the four basis directions. The lecturer thinks that it is given by a combination of deltas and etas, ``something like $\eta^{\mu a}\delta^\nu{}_b$ plus or minus similar terms'' \ts{18}{1}{31:49} \ts{18}{1}{32:10} \ts{18}{1}{32:31} \ts{18}{1}{32:52}: this is the vector representation, and $\rho(M^{01})$ is something like a boost in one direction \ts{18}{1}{33:13}. Once one has a representation one plugs it into the exponential: since the endomorphisms can be composed, $\exp$ becomes the usual exponential series applied to matrices,
\[
  \exp(\rho(A))=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{1}{n!}\rho(A)^n ,
\]
where the $n$-th power is the composition of the endomorphism with itself $n$ times \ts{18}{1}{33:34} \ts{18}{1}{33:55}. This coincides with the exponential map one knows; but $\exp$ as defined here is fully abstract, via the integral curves of the left-invariant vector fields: ``these are little different levels of abstraction'' on which one can discuss. After the choice of a representation everything becomes very elementary, and this is why in physics one often thinks of groups and algebras always in terms of some representation; in order to get the background theorems and use them, one had to do what was done \ts{18}{1}{34:16} \ts{18}{1}{34:36}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: why the two exponentials agree]
This is the last theorem of this chapter (\cref{thm:rc-hom}) applied to the group homomorphism $R\colon G\to GL(V)$ of a representation: $R(\exp A)=\exp_{GL(V)}(\rho(A))$, and $\exp_{GL(V)}$ is the power series. We do not prove that for $GL(V)$ the abstract $\exp$ is the power series (the integral curve of the left-invariant field of $B$ is $\lambda\mapsto\sum_n\lambda^nB^n/n!$, since $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}$ of it is $\gamma\cdot B$). \ts{18}{1}{33:34}
\end{coursenote}

\section{One-parameter subgroups}\label{sec:rc-1ps}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: one-parameter subgroup]\label{def:rc-1ps}
A \textbf{one-parameter subgroup} (or one-dimensional subgroup) of a Lie group $(G,\bullet)$ is a Lie group homomorphism $\psi\colon\R\to G$, where $\R$ is a Lie group with ordinary addition (an abelian group); that is, $\psi$ is smooth and
\[
  \psi(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)=\psi(\lambda_1)\bullet\psi(\lambda_2)\qquad\text{for all }\lambda_1,\lambda_2\in\R :
\]
one may first compose the two parameters by the group law of $\R$ and map, or map each of them and compose in $G$. It is ``the embedding of a one-parameter group into the big group'' (just terminology). \ts{18}{1}{34:57} \ts{18}{1}{35:17} \ts{18}{1}{35:39} \ts{18}{1}{36:00} \ts{18}{1}{36:24} \ts{18}{1}{36:45} \ts{18}{1}{37:06}
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[One-parameter subgroups are exponentials]\label{thm:rc-1ps}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] For every $A\in T_{\mathbb 1}G$ the map $\psi^A\colon\R\to G$, $\psi^A(\lambda):=\exp(\lambda A)$, is a one-parameter subgroup of $G$.
  \item[(ii)] Every one-parameter subgroup of $G$ is of this form, for a unique $A\in T_{\mathbb 1}G$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(The lecture gives no proof; the argument added here is not said in class.)} Let $\gamma:=\gamma^A$, so $\psi^A=\gamma$, and fix $s\in\R$. The two curves
\[
  c_1(\lambda)=\gamma(s)\bullet\gamma(\lambda),\qquad c_2(\lambda)=\gamma(\lambda+s)
\]
are both integral curves of $X^A$ (the first is the image of the integral curve $\gamma$ under the left translation $L_{\gamma(s)}$, which preserves the left-invariant field), and both pass through $\gamma(s)$ at $\lambda=0$. By uniqueness of integral curves $c_1=c_2$, that is, $\psi^A(s+\lambda)=\psi^A(s)\bullet\psi^A(\lambda)$. For (ii) let $\psi$ be a one-parameter subgroup and $A:=\dot\psi(0)$. Differentiating $\psi(s+\lambda)=\psi(\lambda)\bullet\psi(s)$ in $s$ at $s=0$ gives $\dot\psi(\lambda)=(L_{\psi(\lambda)})_*A=X^A_{\psi(\lambda)}$: $\psi$ is the integral curve of $X^A$ through $\mathbb 1$, so $\psi=\gamma^A$.
\ts{18}{1}{37:27} \ts{18}{1}{37:48} \ts{18}{1}{38:09}
\end{proof}
The first part is ``not so spectacular'' \ts{18}{1}{38:32}. For $\lambda=0$ one has $\exp(0)=\mathbb 1$: the one-parameter subgroup always goes through the identity, so its image $\psi^A(\R)\subseteq G$ contains the identity; and it contains inverses: the inverse of $\psi^A(\lambda)$ is $\psi^A(-\lambda)$, since $\psi^A(\lambda)\bullet\psi^A(-\lambda)=\psi^A(0)=\mathbb 1$. (If one thinks back to the circle: the one-parameter subgroup is the circle itself, run through at constant speed.) The index $A$ is just the chosen element of the tangent space \ts{18}{1}{38:52} \ts{18}{1}{39:13} \ts{18}{1}{39:37}. The second part: \emph{every} one-parameter (one-dimensional) subgroup of $G$ is of this form; hence the Lie algebra allows us to understand all one-parameter subgroups of the Lie group \ts{18}{1}{40:00}.

\section{The exponential map and group homomorphisms}\label{sec:rc-hom}

The conclusion of the lecture is a theorem ``as one would expect'' \ts{18}{1}{40:23}. Let $G$ and $H$ be Lie groups and $f\colon G\to H$ a smooth group homomorphism, $f(g_1\bullet_Gg_2)=f(g_1)\bullet_Hf(g_2)$ \ts{18}{1}{40:46} \ts{18}{1}{41:08}. A homomorphism takes the identity to the identity, which is not difficult to show, so the push-forward at the identity is a linear map between the two Lie algebras, $f_*\colon T_{\mathbb 1_G}G\to T_{\mathbb 1_H}H$ \ts{18}{1}{41:31} \ts{18}{1}{41:52} \ts{18}{1}{42:14} \ts{18}{1}{42:34}.
\begin{theorem}[Exponential map and homomorphisms]\label{thm:rc-hom}
The diagram
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,node distance=2.6cm,baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
\node (G) {$G$};\node (H) [right=of G] {$H$};
\node (TG) [above=of G] {$T_{\mathbb 1}G$};\node (TH) [above=of H] {$T_{\mathbb 1}H$};
\draw[->] (G)--node[below,font=\small]{$f$}(H);
\draw[->] (TG)--node[above,font=\small]{$f_*$}(TH);
\draw[->] (TG)--node[left,font=\small]{$\exp_G$}(G);
\draw[->] (TH)--node[right,font=\small]{$\exp_H$}(H);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
commutes, that is, for every $A\in T_{\mathbb 1}G$
\[
  f\bigl(\exp_G(A)\bigr)=\exp_H\bigl(f_*A\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{(Not said in class: the lecture only states the theorem.)} From $f\circ L_g=L_{f(g)}\circ f$ one gets
\[
  f_*\bigl(X^A_g\bigr)=(L_{f(g)})_*f_*A=X^{f_*A}_{f(g)}.
\]
Hence $f\circ\gamma^A$ is an integral curve of $X^{f_*A}$ through $\mathbb 1_H$, so by uniqueness $f\circ\gamma^A=\gamma^{f_*A}$. Evaluating at $1$ gives the claim.
\ts{18}{1}{42:55} \ts{18}{1}{43:16}
\end{proof}
We know what ``the diagram commutes'' means: one can go either from $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ to $H$ the one way or the other, which is spelled out for practice as the equation \ts{18}{1}{42:55}. This is convenient: the exponential map ``in a sense commutes with Lie group homomorphisms'', but in the algebra it has to be replaced by the push-forward associated with the homomorphism \ts{18}{1}{43:16}.

At this point all that remains is to solve some problems on an upcoming problem sheet over Christmas, and this finishes the formal discussion of Lie groups and their algebras \ts{18}{1}{43:58}.
\begin{aside}[Where this is used]
Not said in class: with the exponential map one can state what ``generated'' means for physicists, and the theorem on homomorphisms is what makes a representation of the group differentiate to a representation of the algebra (\cref{ch:l17}).
\end{aside}
