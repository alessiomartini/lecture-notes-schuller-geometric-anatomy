\section{Where we stand: the bracket on $T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$}\label{sec:sa-recap}

The lecture continues the study of $SL(2,\C)$, the relativistic spin group, and in particular of its Lie algebra, with the analysis developed in the second previous lecture: the Dynkin diagram of the algebra \ts{16}{1}{0:08} \ts{16}{1}{0:28}.
\begin{recall}[The three left-invariant fields]
From the coordinate vectors $\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}$ at the identity (chart $x$, $x^1=a$, $x^2=b$, $x^3=c$, $d=\frac{1+bc}{a}$) the left translations produce the fields of \cref{thm:sl-fields}: $\Lambda_1=a\partial_a-b\partial_b+c\partial_c$, $\Lambda_2=a\partial_b$, $\Lambda_3=b\partial_a+d\,\partial_c$ (``$M^m{}_i\,\partial_m$ with $m$ the row and $i$ the column'').
\end{recall}
That was the aim: one has $T_{\mathbb 1}G\cong L(G)$ as vector spaces (\cref{thm:ll-iso}), where $L(G)$ is a subset of the sections of the tangent bundle (an infinite-dimensional vector space over $\R$ or $\C$) and finite-dimensional itself; but $L(G)$ \emph{inherits a bracket} from the differential-geometric commutator, while on $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ the bracket has to be induced; it is much more handy to deal with the tangent space at the identity \ts{16}{1}{0:49} \ts{16}{1}{1:10} \ts{16}{1}{1:30} \ts{16}{1}{1:50} \ts{16}{1}{2:11} \ts{16}{1}{2:32} \ts{16}{1}{2:53} \ts{16}{1}{3:14} \ts{16}{1}{3:34} \ts{16}{1}{3:55} \ts{16}{1}{4:15}.

The bracket on $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ (double bracket, for distinction) is defined on the basis by its action on a function $f$:
\[
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1},\partial_j|_{\mathbb 1}\bigr]\!\bigr]f:=\bigl[\Lambda_i,\Lambda_j\bigr]f\ \Big|_{\text{at }\mathbb 1},
\]
the differential-geometric commutator of the two fields obtained by pushing the vectors to a point $g=(a,b,c,d)$ and varying $g$ \ts{16}{1}{4:36} \ts{16}{1}{4:57} \ts{16}{1}{5:19} \ts{16}{1}{5:41}. The commutator alone gives a vector at $g$, i.e.\ a derivative of $f$ \emph{everywhere}; last time the remedy was to push it back with $L_{g^{-1}}$; an even easier way to obtain the relevant vector at the identity is to \emph{evaluate the whole commutator at the identity} before it is applied to $f$ \ts{16}{1}{6:02} \ts{16}{1}{6:23}. This fully defines the Lie bracket on the tangent space; to study the structure one has to calculate. ``There is no better method than to just carry through the calculation'' \ts{16}{1}{6:44}.
\begin{recall}[Why evaluating at the identity is legitimate]
$j^{-1}(Y)=Y_{\mathbb 1}$ (\cref{def:ll-lie-algebra-of-G}), and the commutator of two left-invariant fields is left-invariant (\cref{thm:ll-subalgebra}), so $(L_{g^{-1}})_*[\Lambda_i,\Lambda_j]_g=[\Lambda_i,\Lambda_j]_{\mathbb 1}$; see the course note in \cref{sec:sl-bracketsetup}.
\end{recall}

\section{The Lie bracket of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$}\label{sec:sa-bracket}

Three brackets are needed. In the coordinates $(a,b,c)$ of the chart $x$ the lecture writes $\partial_a,\partial_b,\partial_c$ for $\partial/\partial x^1,\partial/\partial x^2,\partial/\partial x^3$ (``it is convenient to label by the coordinate names when one calculates''): \ts{16}{1}{7:06} \ts{16}{1}{7:27} \ts{16}{1}{7:48} \ts{16}{1}{8:09}
\[
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_a,\partial_b\bigr]\!\bigr],\qquad
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_a,\partial_c\bigr]\!\bigr],\qquad
  \bigl[\!\bigl[\partial_b,\partial_c\bigr]\!\bigr]\qquad(\text{all at }\mathbb 1).
\]
Each is evaluated by acting on a function with the commutator of the corresponding fields: the first column of $M$ gives $\Lambda_1=a\partial_a-b\partial_b+c\partial_c$, the second one $\Lambda_2=a\partial_b$ (``much simpler''), the third $\Lambda_3=b\partial_a+\frac{1+bc}{a}\partial_c$ \ts{16}{1}{8:29} \ts{16}{1}{8:50} \ts{16}{1}{9:11} \ts{16}{1}{9:31} \ts{16}{1}{9:53} \ts{16}{1}{10:14} \ts{16}{1}{10:35}. One has to remember that $\partial_i f$ is not the naive partial derivative but $\partial_i(f\circ x^{-1})$ at $x(g)$ (the notation convention of \cref{def:tv-ddx}).

\begin{theorem}[The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$]\label{thm:sa-bracket}
Write $X_1:=\partial_a|_{\mathbb 1}$, $X_2:=\partial_b|_{\mathbb 1}$, $X_3:=\partial_c|_{\mathbb 1}$, a basis of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)=T_{\mathbb 1}SL(2,\C)$. Then
\[
  [\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2,\qquad[\![X_1,X_3]\!]=-2X_3,\qquad[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1 .
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{The bracket $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]$} (the simplest, ``we learn there what in principle we already know''). One applies the commutator of $\Lambda_2=a\partial_b$ and $\Lambda_3=b\partial_a+d\,\partial_c$, $d=\frac{1+bc}{a}$, to $f$: $\Lambda_2(\Lambda_3f)-\Lambda_3(\Lambda_2f)$. For the first term the derivative $\partial_b$ acts, with the product rule, on $b$ and on $d$ (since $\partial_bd=\frac ca$):
\[
  \Lambda_2(\Lambda_3f)=a\,\partial_af+ab\,\partial_b\partial_af+c\,\partial_cf+ad\,\partial_b\partial_cf .
\]
For the second,
\[
  \Lambda_3(\Lambda_2f)=b\,\partial_bf+ab\,\partial_a\partial_bf+ad\,\partial_c\partial_bf .
\]
In the difference the \emph{second derivatives cancel}: $\partial_a\partial_b=\partial_b\partial_a$ and $\partial_b\partial_c=\partial_c\partial_b$ by the Schwarz lemma (it applies, ``because our manifold is sufficiently often continuously differentiable'': the derivatives refer to $f\circ x^{-1}$). It is not a surprise but a check: the commutator of two fields is a vector field, so no second derivatives may appear. What remains is
\[
  [\Lambda_2,\Lambda_3]f=a\,\partial_af-b\,\partial_bf+c\,\partial_cf=\Lambda_1f .
\]
At the identity ($a=1$, $b=c=0$) only $\partial_a$ survives: $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1$ \ts{16}{1}{10:56} \ts{16}{1}{11:17} \ts{16}{1}{11:37} \ts{16}{1}{12:00} \ts{16}{1}{12:21} \ts{16}{1}{12:42} \ts{16}{1}{13:02} \ts{16}{1}{13:23} \ts{16}{1}{13:44} \ts{16}{1}{14:05} \ts{16}{1}{14:25} \ts{16}{1}{14:46} \ts{16}{1}{15:07} \ts{16}{1}{15:28} \ts{16}{1}{15:49} \ts{16}{1}{16:09} \ts{16}{1}{16:30} \ts{16}{1}{16:51} \ts{16}{1}{17:11} \ts{16}{1}{17:32} \ts{16}{1}{17:53} \ts{16}{1}{18:14}.\par
\emph{The bracket $[\![X_1,X_2]\!]$}: from now on the second-derivative terms are dropped at once, since they must cancel. $\Lambda_1(\Lambda_2f)$: $\Lambda_1=a\partial_a-b\partial_b+c\partial_c$ acts non-trivially on the factor $a$ of $\Lambda_2f=a\,\partial_bf$, which gives $a\,\partial_bf$ (the other terms only give second derivatives). The other order, $\Lambda_2(\Lambda_1f)=a\,\partial_b(a\partial_af-b\partial_bf+c\partial_cf)$, contributes $-a\,\partial_bf$ through the $\partial_b$ acting on the factor $-b$. Hence
\[
  [\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2]f=a\,\partial_bf+a\,\partial_bf=2a\,\partial_bf,
\]
and at the identity $[\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2$ (``although the bracket is more complicated, we achieve this by dropping the second derivatives'') \ts{16}{1}{18:34} \ts{16}{1}{18:55} \ts{16}{1}{19:16} \ts{16}{1}{19:37} \ts{16}{1}{19:57} \ts{16}{1}{20:18} \ts{16}{1}{20:39}.\par
\emph{The bracket $[\![X_1,X_3]\!]$}: more terms, to be organised. In $\Lambda_1(\Lambda_3f)=\Lambda_1(b\,\partial_af+d\,\partial_cf)$ the first-order terms are $a\,(\partial_ad)\,\partial_cf$ (with $\partial_ad=-\frac da$), $-b\,\partial_af$ (from $-b\partial_b$ acting on the factor $b$), $-b\,(\partial_bd)\,\partial_cf=-\frac{bc}a\partial_cf$ and $c\,(\partial_cd)\,\partial_cf=\frac{bc}{a}\partial_cf$; the last two cancel and one is left with $-d\,\partial_cf-b\,\partial_af$. In $\Lambda_3(\Lambda_1f)=\Lambda_3(a\partial_af-b\partial_bf+c\partial_cf)$ the first-order terms are $b\,\partial_af$ (from $b\partial_a$ on the factor $a$) and $d\,\partial_cf$ (from $d\partial_c$ on the factor $c$). Subtracting,
\[
  [\Lambda_1,\Lambda_3]f=-2\bigl(b\,\partial_af+d\,\partial_cf\bigr)=-2\Lambda_3f,
\]
so at the identity ($d=1$, $b=0$): $[\![X_1,X_3]\!]=-2X_3$ \ts{16}{1}{21:00} \ts{16}{1}{21:21} \ts{16}{1}{21:41} \ts{16}{1}{22:02} \ts{16}{1}{22:22} \ts{16}{1}{22:43} \ts{16}{1}{23:04} \ts{16}{1}{23:24} \ts{16}{1}{23:46} \ts{16}{1}{24:07} \ts{16}{1}{24:28} \ts{16}{1}{24:49} \ts{16}{1}{25:10}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: the relations hold at every point, and the matrices]
The same computation shows $[\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2]=2\Lambda_2$, $[\Lambda_1,\Lambda_3]=-2\Lambda_3$, $[\Lambda_2,\Lambda_3]=\Lambda_1$ as relations between \emph{fields}, at every point, as it must be by left-invariance. If one identifies $X_1,X_2,X_3$ with the matrices $H=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, $E=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $F=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (\cref{sec:sl-pushforward}) these are the familiar relations $[H,E]=2E$, $[H,F]=-2F$, $[E,F]=H$ of the matrix commutator. The lecture insists: this is just the definition we worked with, ``a Lie algebra of abstract tangent-space vectors that has nothing to do with $2\times2$ traceless matrices'' (matrices are a \emph{representation}, coming later) \ts{16}{1}{27:07}.
\end{coursenote}
\begin{aside}[Bracket or commutator?]
The lecture keeps correcting itself: one should not say ``commutation relations'': they are relations of the abstract Lie bracket; only in a representation by matrices or linear maps do they become commutators \ts{16}{1}{1:19:59} \ts{16}{1}{1:39:29}.
\end{aside}

\subsection{The structure constants}\label{sec:sa-structure}
For future reference, with $X_i=\partial_i|_{\mathbb 1}$: \ts{16}{1}{25:31} \ts{16}{1}{26:04} \ts{16}{1}{26:25} \ts{16}{1}{26:47}. By \cref{def:cl-structure} the structure constants are read off from $[\![X_i,X_j]\!]=C^k{}_{ij}X_k$: the only non-zero ones are
\[
  C^2{}_{12}=2,\qquad C^3{}_{13}=-2,\qquad C^1{}_{23}=1,
\]
together with those obtained from antisymmetry in the lower indices, $C^k{}_{ji}=-C^k{}_{ij}$ (this comes from the antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity inherited from the differential-geometric commutator); all others vanish. They depend on the basis: with other coordinates on the group one would obtain another basis of $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ and other structure constants, but one knows how to transform between them, so in some basis they look like this \ts{16}{1}{27:29} \ts{16}{1}{27:49} \ts{16}{1}{28:09} \ts{16}{1}{28:30} \ts{16}{1}{28:51} \ts{16}{1}{29:13}. Since $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is a complex Lie algebra these are complex numbers (here real, even integers).

\section{The Killing form: semisimplicity}\label{sec:sa-killing}

The Killing form (\cref{def:cl-killing}) of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is $K(A,B)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_A\circ\mathrm{ad}_B)$ and in the chosen basis its components are given by the structure constants taken \emph{twice} (\cref{prop:cl-components}) \ts{16}{1}{29:34} \ts{16}{1}{29:55} \ts{16}{1}{30:16}:
\[
  K_{ij}=C^m{}_{in}\,C^n{}_{jm}.
\]
Why this index structure: there are six indices with two free ones ($i,j$) and the other four are summed in the only possible pairing, upper $m$ with lower $m$ and upper $n$ with lower $n$ (one cannot contract three indices with two); one must be careful not to exchange lower indices without exchanging the other pair, which would only give an overall sign. Exchanging the two numbers gives $C^n{}_{jm}C^m{}_{in}=K_{ji}$: the Killing form is symmetric \ts{16}{1}{30:37} \ts{16}{1}{30:57} \ts{16}{1}{31:18} \ts{16}{1}{31:39} \ts{16}{1}{32:00}.
\begin{coursenote}[A mis-hearing]
Around this point the lecture says one can exchange the two numbers ``taking into account the complex conjugation between these coefficients''; this is probably a mis-hearing of ``the complex numbers commute'' (the $C$'s are plain complex numbers: no conjugation enters) [?]. \ts{16}{1}{31:18}
\end{coursenote}
Why do we want $K$? One thing it decides is \emph{semisimplicity}. The claim: $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$, the relativistic spin algebra, is semisimple. By Cartan's criterion (\cref{thm:cl-cartan-criterion}) this is equivalent to $K$ being non-degenerate, i.e.\ the matrix $(K_{ij})$ having non-zero determinant (``strictly speaking the determinant is defined for endomorphisms, this is a bilinear form, but one does it anyway'': the determinant of a bilinear form's matrix is a density, and its being non-zero is basis-independent) \ts{16}{1}{32:20} \ts{16}{1}{32:41} \ts{16}{1}{33:02} \ts{16}{1}{33:23}.

The matrix is symmetric $3\times3$, so six numbers have to be computed; the lecture shows $K_{11}$ as a model, with ``a disciplined execution of the Einstein summation convention'': plug in $i=j=1$ and do the two sums one after the other, first over $m$ and then over $n$ \ts{16}{1}{33:44} \ts{16}{1}{34:04} \ts{16}{1}{34:25} \ts{16}{1}{34:46}:
\[
  K_{11}=C^1{}_{1n}C^n{}_{11}+C^2{}_{1n}C^n{}_{12}+C^3{}_{1n}C^n{}_{13}.
\]
The first term vanishes because $C^n{}_{11}=0$ (antisymmetry in the lower indices); in the second only $C^2{}_{12}$ is non-zero among the $C^2{}_{1n}$ (so $n=2$) and then $C^2{}_{12}=2$ again: $2\cdot2=4$; in the third only $C^3{}_{13}=-2$ survives and $C^3{}_{13}=-2$ again: $(-2)(-2)=4$. Total: $K_{11}=8$ (``non-trivial structure constants: $C^2{}_{12}$, $C^3{}_{13}$, $C^1{}_{23}$ and those related to these by the antisymmetry, all others vanish'') \ts{16}{1}{35:07} \ts{16}{1}{35:28} \ts{16}{1}{35:48} \ts{16}{1}{36:08} \ts{16}{1}{36:29} \ts{16}{1}{36:49} \ts{16}{1}{37:11} \ts{16}{1}{37:31} \ts{16}{1}{37:51}.

The other entries are ``too boring to go through'' and the lecturer assures the result: one finds two things \ts{16}{1}{38:12}: (a) $K$ is non-degenerate, so $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is \textbf{semisimple}, which by the discussion of \cref{ch:l14} means it is a finite direct sum of simple Lie algebras $L_1\oplus\dots\oplus L_n$ \ts{16}{1}{38:33} \ts{16}{1}{38:54}; (b) a side remark about the signature, next.
\begin{proposition}[The Killing form of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$]\label{prop:sa-killing}
In the basis $X_1,X_2,X_3$,
\[
  (K_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}8&0&0\\0&0&4\\0&4&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det K=-128\ne0 .
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Not said in class beyond $K_{11}=8$.} The adjoint maps in the basis $(X_1,X_2,X_3)$ follow from \cref{thm:sa-bracket}: $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}=\mathrm{diag}(0,2,-2)$, $\mathrm{ad}_{X_2}$ sends $X_1\mapsto-2X_2$, $X_2\mapsto0$, $X_3\mapsto X_1$, and $\mathrm{ad}_{X_3}$ sends $X_1\mapsto2X_3$, $X_2\mapsto-X_1$, $X_3\mapsto0$. Then $K_{11}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}^2)=4+4=8$; $\mathrm{ad}_{X_2}^2$ sends $X_1\mapsto0$, $X_3\mapsto-2X_2$ and $X_2\mapsto0$, trace $0=K_{22}$ and likewise $K_{33}=0$; $\mathrm{ad}_{X_2}\mathrm{ad}_{X_3}$ sends $X_1\mapsto2X_1$, $X_2\mapsto2X_2$, $X_3\mapsto0$, trace $4=K_{23}$; $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}\mathrm{ad}_{X_2}$ and $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}\mathrm{ad}_{X_3}$ have trace $0$. The determinant is $8\cdot(0-16)=-128$.
\end{proof}
\begin{answer}{Is the Killing form negative definite? What does that say about compactness?}[Signature of $K$ and compactness]
The lecture comes back to the claim of the earlier lecture that the Killing form is negative definite and says it should turn out so, but ``no, it is not'': negative definiteness would imply that the Lie group is \emph{compact}, i.e.\ that the underlying manifold is compact as a topological space, and $SL(2,\C)$ is \emph{not} compact; so $K$ is not negative definite (indeed $K_{11}=8>0$ above). The correct statement behind it: for a \emph{real} Lie algebra of a compact semisimple Lie group $K$ is negative definite. Not said in class: $SL(2,\C)$ is not compact since it is unbounded in $\C^4$ (the elements $\mathrm{diag}(t,t^{-1})$, $t\in\R$, have arbitrarily large entries); and over $\C$ the question of definiteness does not even arise; seen as a \emph{real} six-dimensional Lie algebra, its Killing form is $2\,\mathrm{Re}\,K$ and has signature $(3,3)$ [?: not checked here] \ts{16}{1}{39:16} \ts{16}{1}{39:37} \ts{16}{1}{39:57} \ts{16}{1}{40:18}.
\end{answer}

\section{$\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is simple}\label{sec:sa-simple}

The next question: semisimple, but is it already \emph{simple}, or a sum of several simple algebras? The claim: it is already one simple algebra \ts{16}{1}{40:39}.
\begin{recall}[Ideal, simple Lie algebra]
An ideal $I\subseteq L$ is a subspace with $[I,L]\subseteq I$; $\{0\}$ and $L$ are the trivial ideals; $L$ is simple if it is non-abelian and has only the trivial ones (\cref{def:cl-simple}).
\end{recall}
One must show that there is no other ideal \ts{16}{1}{41:02} \ts{16}{1}{41:24} \ts{16}{1}{41:45} \ts{16}{1}{42:06} \ts{16}{1}{42:26} \ts{16}{1}{42:47}. The lecture's route: consider the set $I$ of all $\alpha X_1+\beta X_2+\gamma X_3$ with the complex numbers restricted so that $I$ is neither $\{0\}$ (all three zero) nor everything (no restriction); by bilinearity it suffices to bracket a general element with the basis vectors \ts{16}{1}{43:09} \ts{16}{1}{43:30} \ts{16}{1}{43:53} \ts{16}{1}{44:14} \ts{16}{1}{44:34} \ts{16}{1}{44:55}. With $x=\alpha X_1+\beta X_2+\gamma X_3$:
\[
  [\![x,X_1]\!]=-2\beta X_2+2\gamma X_3,\qquad
  [\![x,X_2]\!]=2\alpha X_2-\gamma X_1,\qquad
  [\![x,X_3]\!]=-2\alpha X_3+\beta X_1 .
\]
(For the first, $[\![X_1,X_1]\!]=0$, $[\![X_2,X_1]\!]=-2X_2$, $[\![X_3,X_1]\!]=2X_3$, ``the opposite order, so a minus''.) \ts{16}{1}{45:15} \ts{16}{1}{45:36} \ts{16}{1}{45:56} \ts{16}{1}{46:17}. These must again lie in $I$. The lecture argues: if none of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ vanishes, the three right-hand sides are three linearly independent vectors (combinations of $X_2X_3$, $X_1X_2$, $X_1X_3$), so $I$ would be everything; hence at least one of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ vanishes, and one goes through the cases \ts{16}{1}{46:38} \ts{16}{1}{46:59} \ts{16}{1}{47:23} \ts{16}{1}{47:46} \ts{16}{1}{48:07} \ts{16}{1}{48:27} \ts{16}{1}{48:51}. \emph{Case $\alpha=0$}: $I$ lies in the $X_2X_3$-plane, but $[\![x,X_2]\!]$ contains $-\gamma X_1$, which lies outside the plane, so $\gamma=0$; and $[\![x,X_3]\!]$ contains $\beta X_1$, so $\beta=0$: $I=\{0\}$ \ts{16}{1}{49:11} \ts{16}{1}{49:32} \ts{16}{1}{49:53}. \emph{Case $\beta=0$}: $I$ lies in the $X_1X_3$-plane; $[\![x,X_2]\!]=2\alpha X_2-\gamma X_1$ has a component in $X_2$, so $\alpha=0$; then $I$ is in the $X_3$-line and $[\![x,X_1]\!]$ has $2\gamma X_3$ but $[\![x,X_2]\!]=-\gamma X_1$ lies outside, so $\gamma=0$: $I$ trivial \ts{16}{1}{50:13} \ts{16}{1}{50:34} \ts{16}{1}{50:54}. The last case ($\gamma=0$) is ``exactly the same''. Hence the only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $L$: $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is not only semisimple but \textbf{simple} \ts{16}{1}{51:15} \ts{16}{1}{51:36}.
\begin{coursenote}[Two gaps in the lecture's argument]
(1) The three vectors $[\![x,X_i]\!]$ are \emph{never} linearly independent: $\alpha[\![x,X_1]\!]+\beta[\![x,X_2]\!]+\gamma[\![x,X_3]\!]=[\![x,x]\!]=0$ (check with the formulas above), so they span at most a plane. (2) ``One of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ must vanish'' treats the ideal as cut out by setting coordinates to zero, whereas an ideal could a priori be a subspace cut out by a linear relation, such as $\mathrm{span}\{X_1+X_2\}$. The conclusion is right; the clean proof is the next one, added here. Not said in class. \ts{16}{1}{46:38} \ts{16}{1}{47:46}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{theorem}[$\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is simple]\label{thm:sa-simple}
\emph{(With the proof added to the lecture's case analysis; not said in class.)} The only ideals of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ are $\{0\}$ and the whole algebra; and $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is not abelian.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Non-abelian: $[\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2\ne0$. Let $I\ne\{0\}$ be an ideal and $x=\alpha X_1+\beta X_2+\gamma X_3\in I\setminus\{0\}$. Since $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}x=2\beta X_2-2\gamma X_3\in I$ and $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}^2x=4\beta X_2+4\gamma X_3\in I$, one finds $\beta X_2=\frac18(\mathrm{ad}^2_{X_1}x+2\,\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}x)\in I$, $\gamma X_3=\frac18(\mathrm{ad}^2_{X_1}x-2\,\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}x)\in I$ and then $\alpha X_1=x-\beta X_2-\gamma X_3\in I$: so $I$ contains each of the non-zero components of $x$, that is, at least one basis vector. If $X_1\in I$ then $[\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2\in I$ and $[\![X_1,X_3]\!]=-2X_3\in I$, so $I=L$. If $X_2\in I$ then $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1\in I$, hence $I=L$ by the previous case. If $X_3\in I$ then likewise $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1\in I$. In every case $I=L$.
\end{proof}

\section{Roots and the Dynkin diagram of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$}\label{sec:sa-roots}

Now the roots and fundamental roots of the relativistic spin algebra \ts{16}{1}{52:00} \ts{16}{1}{52:20}. From the relations $[\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2$, $[\![X_1,X_3]\!]=-2X_3$, $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1$ one observes:
\begin{proposition}[A Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$]\label{prop:sa-cartan}
$H:=\mathrm{span}_{\C}\{X_1\}$ is a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ (\cref{def:cl-cartan}), with the eigenvectors $X_2,X_3$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}X_2=[\![X_1,X_2]\!]=2X_2$ and $\mathrm{ad}_{X_1}X_3=-2X_3$: the basis $(X_1;X_2,X_3)$ of $L=\mathrm{span}_{\C}\{X_1,X_2,X_3\}$ consists of a basis of $H$ and of eigenvectors of $\mathrm{ad}_h$ for every $h\in H$; every $h$ has the form $sX_1$, $s\in\C$, since $H$ is one-dimensional, hence $\mathrm{ad}_hX_2=2sX_2$, $\mathrm{ad}_hX_3=-2sX_3$. (That $H$ is maximal and abelian is clear: it is one-dimensional, and a bigger subalgebra would contain $X_2$ or $X_3$ and generate everything.) \ts{16}{1}{52:20} \ts{16}{1}{52:40} \ts{16}{1}{53:01} \ts{16}{1}{53:23} \ts{16}{1}{53:45} \ts{16}{1}{54:10} \ts{16}{1}{54:31}.
\end{proof}
The eigenvalue equations are, as in \cref{def:cl-cartan}, read as linear functionals on $H$: for the lecture's labelling, $\lambda_2,\lambda_3\in H^*$ are the functionals associated with $X_2$ and $X_3$. Since a linear map on a one-dimensional vector space is determined by the image of one basis vector,
\[
  \lambda_2(X_1)=2,\qquad\lambda_3(X_1)=-2 .
\]
These are the \textbf{roots} of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ (\cref{def:cl-roots}), the root set is $\Phi=\{\lambda_2,\lambda_3\}$ with $\lambda_3=-\lambda_2$, as the abstract result requires \ts{16}{1}{54:52} \ts{16}{1}{55:12} \ts{16}{1}{55:34} \ts{16}{1}{55:54} \ts{16}{1}{56:15} \ts{16}{1}{56:36} \ts{16}{1}{56:56} \ts{16}{1}{57:16} \ts{16}{1}{57:38}. (A notation issue: in the abstract theory the basis was $H_1,\dots,H_m,E_1,\dots$; here the roots are numbered after the vectors $X_2,X_3$ they belong to; ``let us stick with one type of notation''.) \ts{16}{1}{55:12}

\textbf{Fundamental roots.} A set $\Pi\subsetneq\Phi$ (\cref{def:cl-fundamental}) is linearly independent and every root is $\varepsilon\sum n_i\pi_i$ with $\varepsilon=\pm1$ and non-negative integers $n_i$ (they may be $0$). Here the choice is easy: $\Pi=\{\lambda_2\}$, since $\lambda_3=(-1)\cdot1\cdot\lambda_2$. The choice is not unique: $\lambda_3$ would do equally well \ts{16}{1}{57:58} \ts{16}{1}{58:18} \ts{16}{1}{58:40} \ts{16}{1}{59:00} \ts{16}{1}{59:21} \ts{16}{1}{59:43} \ts{16}{1}{1:00:04} \ts{16}{1}{1:00:24}.

\textbf{Weyl group.} One checks that the Weyl transformations of \cref{def:cl-weyl} regenerate the root set: only $s_{\lambda_2}$ is needed, and
\[
  s_{\lambda_2}(\lambda_2)=\lambda_2-2\,\frac{K^*(\lambda_2,\lambda_2)}{K^*(\lambda_2,\lambda_2)}\lambda_2=-\lambda_2=\lambda_3,
\]
where $K^*$ is the dual Killing form on $H^*_{\R}$ (\cref{def:cl-dual-killing}). The Weyl group acting on the fundamental root produces the whole root set, as the general theorem (\cref{thm:cl-weyl}) says (``had it not, we would have calculated wrongly'') \ts{16}{1}{1:00:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:01:08} \ts{16}{1}{1:01:29} \ts{16}{1}{1:01:49} \ts{16}{1}{1:02:10} \ts{16}{1}{1:02:32} \ts{16}{1}{1:02:52} \ts{16}{1}{1:03:13}.
\begin{coursenote}[Not said in class: the numbers]
$H=\mathrm{span}\{X_1\}$ and $K_{11}=8$ give $K^*(\lambda_2,\lambda_2)=\lambda_2(X_1)^2/K(X_1,X_1)=4/8=\tfrac12>0$ (positive definite on $H^*_{\R}$, \cref{thm:cl-positive}); the Weyl group is $\{\mathrm{id},s_{\lambda_2}\}\cong\mathbb Z_2$; and $\dim L=\dim H+|\Phi|=1+2=3$. \ts{16}{1}{1:01:49}
\end{coursenote}
\textbf{Cartan matrix and Dynkin diagram.} With one fundamental root the Cartan matrix is the $1\times1$ matrix with the diagonal entry $C_{11}=2$ (always $2$; ``that is where it pays off that we checked this'': the algebra is simple); and the Dynkin diagram is a single circle, one for every fundamental root, and no bonds. In the classification (\cref{thm:cl-classification}) this is the diagram $A_1$: \emph{the Lie algebra of the group $SL(2,\C)$ is $A_1$} \ts{16}{1}{1:03:35} \ts{16}{1}{1:03:56} \ts{16}{1}{1:04:16} \ts{16}{1}{1:04:37}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=-0.5ex]
\draw[fill=white] (0,0) circle (0.14);
\node[right=6pt] at (0.14,0) {$A_1$\quad(Cartan matrix $(2)$,\ roots $\pm\lambda_2$)};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\section{From a Dynkin diagram to a Lie algebra: the example $A_2$}\label{sec:sa-a2}

So far one went from the group to the algebra and to the Dynkin diagram. Now the opposite way, \emph{from the diagram to the Lie algebra} (``at least that''); not with the diagram above (``too boring'') but with the next one \ts{16}{1}{1:05:00} \ts{16}{1}{1:05:24} \ts{16}{1}{1:05:44}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=-0.5ex]
\draw[fill=white] (0,0) circle (0.14);\draw[fill=white] (1.2,0) circle (0.14);
\draw (0.14,0)--(1.06,0);
\node[below=3pt,font=\scriptsize] at (0,0) {$\pi_1$};\node[below=3pt,font=\scriptsize] at (1.2,0) {$\pi_2$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\textbf{Step 1: the fundamental roots.} One circle for every fundamental root: two roots $\pi_1,\pi_2$, joined by one bond (the diagram is symmetric, the numbering does not matter) \ts{16}{1}{1:06:05} \ts{16}{1}{1:06:26}.
\begin{recall}[Cartan matrix and bond number]
$C_{ij}=2K^*(\pi_i,\pi_j)/K^*(\pi_i,\pi_i)$, not symmetric; the off-diagonal entries are $\le0$; the bond number is $n_{ij}=C_{ij}C_{ji}=4\cos^2\theta_{ij}$ (\cref{def:cl-cartan-matrix,def:cl-bond}); the real span $H^*_{\R}$ of $\Pi$ contains all roots, $K^*$ is positive definite there (\cref{def:cl-hr,thm:cl-positive}); $\Pi\subsetneq\Phi\subset H^*_{\R}\subset H^*=\mathrm{span}_{\C}\Pi$.
\end{recall}
The bond number is $n_{12}=1$, hence $1=4\cos^2\theta_{12}$, so $|\cos\theta_{12}|=\frac12$, and there are two angles with this absolute value: $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ (by taking the square root one lost the sign) \ts{16}{1}{1:06:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:07:08} \ts{16}{1}{1:07:28} \ts{16}{1}{1:07:49} \ts{16}{1}{1:08:10} \ts{16}{1}{1:08:31} \ts{16}{1}{1:08:52} \ts{16}{1}{1:09:12} \ts{16}{1}{1:09:33} \ts{16}{1}{1:09:54} \ts{16}{1}{1:10:14} \ts{16}{1}{1:10:34}. Which one? The off-diagonal Cartan matrix entries are negative: $C_{12}=2K^*(\pi_1,\pi_2)/K^*(\pi_1,\pi_1)<0$ while the denominator is positive, so $K^*(\pi_1,\pi_2)<0$: the projection of one root on the other is negative, so the angle is greater than $90^\circ$:
\[
  \theta_{12}=120^\circ .
\]
This is a general rule: from a Dynkin diagram the angle between two connected fundamental roots is always the obtuse one \ts{16}{1}{1:10:55} \ts{16}{1}{1:11:15} \ts{16}{1}{1:11:36} \ts{16}{1}{1:11:57}.

One can therefore draw $H^*_{\R}$, a two-dimensional real vector space (two fundamental roots), with $\pi_1=(1,0)$ of length $1$ (the overall scale of $K^*$ is a convention; for a single bond the two roots have the same length) and $\pi_2=(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$ at $120^\circ$: indeed $\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$ \ts{16}{1}{1:12:18} \ts{16}{1}{1:12:39} \ts{16}{1}{1:12:59} \ts{16}{1}{1:13:20}.

\textbf{Step 2: the root set by the Weyl group.} By repeated action of the Weyl group on $\Pi$ all the roots are recovered \ts{16}{1}{1:13:20}.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $s_{\pi_1}(\pi_1)=-\pi_1$, so $-\pi_1=(-1,0)$ is a root \ts{16}{1}{1:13:40}.
  \item $s_{\pi_1}(\pi_2)=\pi_2-2\frac{K^*(\pi_1,\pi_2)}{K^*(\pi_1,\pi_1)}\pi_1=\pi_2-2\cdot(-\tfrac12)\pi_1=\pi_1+\pi_2=(\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$ (``I forgot the 2 in front'', corrected on the board) \ts{16}{1}{1:14:01} \ts{16}{1}{1:14:23} \ts{16}{1}{1:14:44} \ts{16}{1}{1:15:05} \ts{16}{1}{1:15:26}.
  \item $s_{\pi_1}(\pi_1+\pi_2)=\pi_1+\pi_2-2\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)\pi_1=\pi_2$: one goes back \ts{16}{1}{1:15:46} \ts{16}{1}{1:16:08} \ts{16}{1}{1:16:28}.
  \item Acting with the other generator $s_{\pi_2}$ gives the remaining two roots $-\pi_2$ and $-\pi_1-\pi_2$ (the lecture does not carry this out: ``you get two more roots''), and any further action only permutes the six roots among themselves \ts{16}{1}{1:16:49} \ts{16}{1}{1:17:09}.
\end{enumerate}
So the root set has six elements: $\Phi=\{\pm\pi_1,\pm\pi_2,\pm(\pi_1+\pi_2)\}$, in the coordinates of the figure
\[
  \Phi=\Bigl\{(1,0),\ (-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2),\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}2),\ (\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2),\ (-\tfrac12,-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2),\ (-1,0)\Bigr\},
\]
the vertices of a regular hexagon. (The orbit computation is the content of \cref{ex:cl-full}(c).)
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,scale=1.2]
\draw[->,gray] (-2.4,0)--(2.4,0);\draw[->,gray] (0,-2)--(0,2);
\foreach \ang/\nm/\pos in {0/{\pi_1}/right,120/{\pi_2}/above left,60/{\pi_1+\pi_2}/above right,180/{-\pi_1}/left,300/{-\pi_2}/below right,240/{-\pi_1-\pi_2}/below left}{
  \draw[->,thick,defcol] (0,0)--(\ang:1.7) node[\pos,font=\scriptsize,black]{$\nm$};
}
\foreach \ang in {0,60,...,300}{\fill (\ang:1.7) circle (0.045);}
\draw[thmcol,thin] (0:1.7)--(60:1.7)--(120:1.7)--(180:1.7)--(240:1.7)--(300:1.7)--cycle;
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\textbf{Step 3: the dimension.} $\dim_{\C}H=\dim_{\C}H^*$ (finite-dimensional space and its dual) $=\dim_{\R}H^*_{\R}$ (a real basis of $H^*_{\R}$ is a complex basis of $H^*$) $=|\Pi|=2$. Since $L$ decomposes into the Cartan subalgebra and one eigenvector per root, $\dim L=\dim H+|\Phi|=2+6=8$ \ts{16}{1}{1:17:30} \ts{16}{1}{1:17:50} \ts{16}{1}{1:18:10} \ts{16}{1}{1:18:36}: whichever Lie algebra is represented by this diagram has dimension $8$, and the diagram is $A_2$ of the classification \ts{16}{1}{1:18:57}.
\begin{coursenote}[A slip in the count]
The lecture says ``plus the size of the root space, which in this case is eight'' while the root set has six elements and the dimension, eight, is $2+6$. \ts{16}{1}{1:18:10}
\end{coursenote}
\begin{aside}[$A_2=\mathfrak{sl}(3,\C)$]
Not said in class: $A_2$ is $\mathfrak{sl}(3,\C)$, of dimension $3^2-1=8$ (\cref{ch:l14}, aside on the classical series). \ts{16}{1}{1:18:57}
\end{aside}
So the lecture has reconstructed the root space: a two-dimensional real space, with the two fundamental roots $(1,0)$ and $(-\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2)$, and by repeated Weyl action also $(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}2)$, $(\frac12,-\frac{\sqrt3}2)$, $(-\frac12,-\frac{\sqrt3}2)$ and $(-1,0)$ \ts{16}{1}{1:19:17} \ts{16}{1}{1:19:38}.

\section{Recovering the bracket relations from the root diagram}\label{sec:sa-reconstruct}

The next step: from the root set to the \emph{full algebra}, i.e.\ its bracket relations \ts{16}{1}{1:19:59} \ts{16}{1}{1:20:20}. (The lecture corrects itself: ``commutation relation'' is wrong, it is the abstract Lie bracket; only in a representation does it become a commutator.) If one chooses a basis $X_i$, there are structure constants $C^k{}_{ij}$ with $i,j,k$ running from $1$ to $8$ \ts{16}{1}{1:20:41}.

\textbf{What is known by construction.} $L$ is simple. The Cartan subalgebra $H=\mathrm{span}_{\C}\{H_1,H_2\}$ is two-dimensional and there are six eigenvectors $E_1,\dots,E_6$, one for each root $\lambda_\alpha$:
\[
  [\![H_a,E_\alpha]\!]=\lambda_\alpha(H_a)\,E_\alpha,\qquad a=1,2,\ \alpha=1,\dots,6 ,
\]
where $\lambda_\alpha(H_a)$ are concrete numbers, those which appear in the root diagram (the first member of the pair is the first root evaluated on the first basis element of the Cartan subalgebra, the second one is the same root evaluated on the second basis element) \ts{16}{1}{1:21:01} \ts{16}{1}{1:21:22} \ts{16}{1}{1:21:44} \ts{16}{1}{1:22:04} \ts{16}{1}{1:22:25} \ts{16}{1}{1:22:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:23:09} \ts{16}{1}{1:23:29}. Reading this off as structure constants: $C^{\alpha}{}_{a\alpha}=\lambda_\alpha(H_a)$ (the bracket $[\![H_a,E_\alpha]\!]$ has only the $E_\alpha$-component); and $[\![H_a,H_b]\!]=0$ since the Cartan subalgebra is abelian \ts{16}{1}{1:23:52} \ts{16}{1}{1:24:13} \ts{16}{1}{1:24:35}.
\begin{coursenote}[Index conventions and the count of constants]
The lecture warns that the index $1$ of $H_1$ and the index $1$ of $E_1$ (for $\alpha$) could be confused (``you could think that $1\,1$ is $0$''), so it writes $\bar1,\bar2$ for the Cartan indices: $\bar1\neq1$ labels another basis element. The non-zero constants involving a Cartan element are the $12$ numbers $C^{\alpha}{}_{\bar a\alpha}$ ($\bar a=\bar1,\bar2$, $\alpha=1,\dots,6$), plus the $12$ with the two lower indices swapped by antisymmetry: $24$ in all. The lecture's further count (``another 8, 16 in total'') is muddled and not reproduced [?]. \ts{16}{1}{1:24:57} \ts{16}{1}{1:25:17} \ts{16}{1}{1:25:38} \ts{16}{1}{1:25:59} \ts{16}{1}{1:26:20} \ts{16}{1}{1:26:41} \ts{16}{1}{1:27:02}
\end{coursenote}
This covers every bracket with at least one element of the Cartan subalgebra \ts{16}{1}{1:27:22}. What remains to be clarified is how the $E_\alpha$ work among each other \ts{16}{1}{1:27:43} \ts{16}{1}{1:28:03}. From the Cartan construction alone one gets no information on this; but one has the \textbf{Jacobi identity} \ts{16}{1}{1:28:25}.
\begin{proposition}[$[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]$ is a root vector, or $0$, or in $H$]\label{prop:sa-jacobi}
For every $H_a$ (and then every $h\in H$),
\[
  \mathrm{ad}_{h}\,[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]=\bigl(\lambda_\alpha(h)+\lambda_\beta(h)\bigr)\,[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!].
\]
So $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]$ is an eigenvector of all $\mathrm{ad}_h$ with eigenvalue the functional $\lambda_\alpha+\lambda_\beta\in H^*$. Hence:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] if $\lambda_\alpha+\lambda_\beta=\lambda_\gamma$ is a root, then $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]$ is a multiple of $E_\gamma$;
  \item[(ii)] if $\lambda_\alpha+\lambda_\beta\neq0$ is \emph{not} a root, then $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]=0$;
  \item[(iii)] if $\lambda_\alpha+\lambda_\beta=0$, then $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]\in H$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Jacobi identity, with $h=H_a$,
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  [\![h,[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]]\!]&=[\![[\![h,E_\alpha]\!],E_\beta]\!]+[\![E_\alpha,[\![h,E_\beta]\!]]\!]\\
  &=\lambda_\alpha(h)[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]+\lambda_\beta(h)[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!],
  \end{aligned}
\]
using $[\![h,E_\alpha]\!]=\lambda_\alpha(h)E_\alpha$ and bilinearity (the lecture writes the cyclic form of the identity, $[h,[E_\alpha,E_\beta]]-[E_\alpha,[E_\beta,h]]-\dots$, and gets the same: ``this is just a number once evaluated, one pulls it out by bilinearity'') \ts{16}{1}{1:28:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:29:08} \ts{16}{1}{1:29:29} \ts{16}{1}{1:29:52} \ts{16}{1}{1:30:12} \ts{16}{1}{1:30:33} \ts{16}{1}{1:30:55} \ts{16}{1}{1:31:15} \ts{16}{1}{1:31:39}. So $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]$ lies in the joint eigenspace of the functional $\lambda_\alpha+\lambda_\beta$ (the sum is taken in $H^*$ first, and only afterwards evaluated on $h$): if this functional is a root $\lambda_\gamma$ that eigenspace is spanned by $E_\gamma$ (i); if it is $0$ the eigenspace is $H$ itself, the vectors with eigenvalue $0$ (iii); if it is neither, the eigenspace is $\{0\}$, which gives (ii) \ts{16}{1}{1:31:59} \ts{16}{1}{1:32:23}. For (iii) one does not immediately know which element of $H$ appears; ``by repeated application of what you know using the brackets you will find out'' \ts{16}{1}{1:36:02} \ts{16}{1}{1:37:45}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[The constants in (i), and why the eigenspace is one-dimensional]
The lecture writes the result of case (i) as $[E_1,E_3]=E_2$; in general it is only proportional, $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]=N_{\alpha\beta}E_\gamma$ with a constant $N_{\alpha\beta}$ which is non-zero (a theorem, not shown) and depends on the normalisation of the $E$'s; these constants are then fixed by the Jacobi identity and the normalisation. That each root eigenspace is one-dimensional (so that there is exactly one $E_\gamma$ per root and the count $\dim L=\dim H+|\Phi|$ holds) is also part of the general theory, not proved. Not said in class. \ts{16}{1}{1:35:20} \ts{16}{1}{1:36:02}
\end{coursenote}
\textbf{The three cases in the example $A_2$.} The lecture draws the root diagram with colours (``it is important not to get confused''): a root, say $\lambda_1$, with its eigenvector $E_1$, and another one, $\lambda_3$, with $E_3$. Their sum $\lambda_1+\lambda_3$ lies on the diagram and is a root (the one called $\lambda_2$ here), so $[\![E_1,E_3]\!]\propto E_2$ for the eigenvector $E_2$ of this root: that is case (i) \ts{16}{1}{1:32:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:33:08} \ts{16}{1}{1:33:29} \ts{16}{1}{1:33:55} \ts{16}{1}{1:34:16} \ts{16}{1}{1:34:37} \ts{16}{1}{1:34:58} \ts{16}{1}{1:35:20} \ts{16}{1}{1:35:40}. In the figure's own names: $[\![E_{\pi_1},E_{\pi_2}]\!]\propto E_{\pi_1+\pi_2}$. Case (ii): two roots whose sum would lie outside the diagram, for instance $\pi_1$ and $\pi_1+\pi_2$ (sum $2\pi_1+\pi_2$, not one of the six vertices): then the bracket is $0$ \ts{16}{1}{1:36:22} \ts{16}{1}{1:36:43} \ts{16}{1}{1:37:04}. Case (iii): opposite roots, for instance $\pi_1$ and $-\pi_1$: the eigenvectors $E_{\pi_1}$ and $E_{-\pi_1}$ are \emph{not} linearly dependent (the roots are, the eigenvectors are not, ``one, two, three, four: $E_1$ and $E_4$''), and their bracket is an element of the Cartan subalgebra \ts{16}{1}{1:37:24} \ts{16}{1}{1:37:45} \ts{16}{1}{1:38:07}. The earlier example is exactly this: in $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ the roots $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3=-\lambda_2$ have the eigenvectors $X_2,X_3$ and $[\![X_2,X_3]\!]=X_1$ lies in the Cartan subalgebra \ts{16}{1}{1:38:27} \ts{16}{1}{1:38:47} \ts{16}{1}{1:39:08}.
\begin{coursenote}[Slips in the labels]
The lecture says ``this is the case where $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$ yielded another root'' right after treating $\lambda_1+\lambda_3$; the labels refer to the colour-coded blackboard figure which the transcript does not reproduce, so only the structure of the three cases is certain. The names used above are those of the figure of this chapter. \ts{16}{1}{1:35:40} \ts{16}{1}{1:36:22}
\end{coursenote}
Conclusion of the lecture: first one evaluates what happens on the Cartan subalgebra by definition, then one uses the Jacobi identity and the case distinction, and \emph{one recovers the entire algebra}, i.e.\ its Lie bracket relations (which appear as commutation relations of, say, matrices only in a representation) from the root diagram; and the root diagram comes directly from the Dynkin diagram. So the Dynkin diagram \textbf{contains all the information about a simple Lie algebra} \ts{16}{1}{1:39:29} \ts{16}{1}{1:39:49} \ts{16}{1}{1:40:11}.

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How does one compute the bracket on $T_{\mathbb 1}G$ from left-invariant fields? Why do the second derivatives cancel?
  \item State the three brackets of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ and the non-zero structure constants.
  \item Write $K_{ij}$ in terms of the structure constants and compute $K_{11}$. How is semisimplicity decided?
  \item Why is $K$ not negative definite on $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$? What does negative definiteness say about the group?
  \item Prove that $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ is simple.
  \item Give a Cartan subalgebra, the roots and a set of fundamental roots of $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$; what is its Dynkin diagram?
  \item From the diagram with two nodes and one bond: find the angle and sketch the root set. How big is the algebra?
  \item Which three cases arise for $[\![E_\alpha,E_\beta]\!]$, and why (Jacobi)?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\C)$ and its structure constants (\cref{thm:sa-bracket,sec:sa-structure}); Killing form and semisimplicity (\cref{prop:sa-killing}).
  \item Simplicity (\cref{thm:sa-simple}); Cartan subalgebra, roots, Weyl group and the diagram $A_1$ (\cref{prop:sa-cartan,sec:sa-roots}).
  \item From the diagram to the algebra: angle, root set, dimension, bracket relations (\cref{sec:sa-a2,sec:sa-reconstruct,prop:sa-jacobi}); the classification \cref{thm:cl-classification}.
\end{itemize}
\end{keyconcepts}
