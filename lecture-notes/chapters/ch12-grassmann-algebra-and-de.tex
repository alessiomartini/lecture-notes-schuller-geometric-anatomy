\section{Differential forms}\label{sec:gr-forms}

Today differential forms, the last section of the chapter on differentiable manifolds. They are very important in physics (the electromagnetic field strength is a differential form, and whether one can find an electromagnetic potential is a question about them). More generally forms give rise to the \textbf{de Rham cohomology} of a smooth manifold; the big surprise is that although its definition uses the
\emph{differentiable} structure, it only tests the underlying \emph{topology}: it is a tool to construct topological invariants through the differentiable structure, and so more easily computable than touching the topology first hand \ts{12}{1}{0:08} \ts{12}{1}{0:29} \ts{12}{1}{0:49} \ts{12}{1}{1:10} \ts{12}{1}{1:32}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $n$-form]\label{def:gr-form}
Let $M$ be a smooth manifold. A (differential) \textbf{$n$-form} on $M$ is a $(0,n)$ tensor field $\omega$ (\cref{def:rm-tensorfield}) which is \textbf{totally antisymmetric}: for vector fields $X_1,\dots,X_n\in\Gamma(TM)$ and every permutation $\pi$ of the first $n$ numbers
\[
  \omega\bigl(X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(n)}\bigr)=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,\omega\bigl(X_1,\dots,X_n\bigr),
\]
that is, a minus sign when two entries are exchanged; $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=\pm1$ is the signature, $+1$ for even and $-1$ for odd permutations \ts{12}{1}{1:53} \ts{12}{1}{2:15} \ts{12}{1}{2:36} \ts{12}{1}{2:57} \ts{12}{1}{3:18} \ts{12}{1}{3:40} \ts{12}{1}{4:01}.
\end{definition}
One has $0\le n\le\dim M$: with more vectors than the dimension, antisymmetry would force the form to vanish (one could write down a $(\dim M+1)$-form, but it is necessarily zero). For $n=1$ the antisymmetry condition is void (the $1$-forms are the covector fields), and for $n=0$ too: the $0$-forms are the $(0,0)$ tensor fields, the scalar fields, i.e.\ the
smooth functions \ts{12}{1}{5:04} \ts{12}{1}{5:24} \ts{12}{1}{5:44}.

\begin{example}[Examples of forms: volume, Faraday tensor, symplectic form]\label{ex:gr-examples}
(a) A smooth manifold $M$ is \emph{orientable} if one can restrict the atlas such that the transition maps behave in a certain way (a problem sheet); then there exists a nowhere vanishing \textbf{top form}, $n=\dim M$ (``maximal $n$''), which provides a volume in every tangent space (\cref{def:te-top,def:te-vol}).
(b) The electromagnetic field strength $F$, the Faraday tensor, is a $2$-form.
(c) In classical mechanics, if the smooth manifold $Q$ describes the configurations of a system, the phase space is the cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ (to every point all the momenta, the covectors, are attached); on $T^*Q$ there is a canonically defined $2$-form $\omega$ (no extra structure needed), the \textbf{symplectic form}. Not every $2$-form is one: it needs further properties, written down later. But being a $2$-form means that
the two entries can be exchanged at the price of a minus sign, and that is the minus sign in the Hamilton equations $\dot q=\partial H/\partial p$, $\dot p=-\partial H/\partial q$: ``very mysterious remarks, very simple'', to show the immediate implications for physics
\ts{12}{1}{4:22} \ts{12}{1}{6:05} \ts{12}{1}{6:26} \ts{12}{1}{6:48} \ts{12}{1}{7:09} \ts{12}{1}{7:29} \ts{12}{1}{7:50} \ts{12}{1}{8:10} \ts{12}{1}{8:32}.
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: the module $\Omega^n(M)$]\label{def:gr-omega-n}
The set of all $n$-forms on $M$ is denoted $\Omega^n(M)$. It inherits the structure of the module of tensor fields: the sum of two $n$-forms is an $n$-form, and so is a $C^\infty(M)$-multiple of an $n$-form; so $\Omega^n(M)$ is a $C^\infty(M)$-\emph{submodule} of the $C^\infty(M)$-module of $(0,n)$ tensor fields \ts{12}{1}{8:53} \ts{12}{1}{9:16} \ts{12}{1}{9:36} \ts{12}{1}{9:57} \ts{12}{1}{10:18}.
\end{definition}
It is, however, not closed under the tensor product: even worse than not being closed, the tensor product of two forms is generically not a form (unless one of them is a $0$-form). But one would like a product that again yields a form \ts{12}{1}{10:39} \ts{12}{1}{11:01} \ts{12}{1}{11:27}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: wedge product (exterior product)]\label{def:gr-wedge}
The \textbf{wedge product} is the map $\wedge\colon\Omega^n(M)\times\Omega^m(M)\to\Omega^{n+m}(M)$ defined, for $\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$, $\sigma\in\Omega^m(M)$ and vector fields $X_1,\dots,X_{n+m}$, by
\[
  (\omega\wedge\sigma)(X_1,\dots,X_{n+m}):=\frac1{n!\,m!}\sum_{\pi}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,(\omega\otimes\sigma)\bigl(X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(n+m)}\bigr),
\]
the sum running over all permutations $\pi$ of the first $n+m$ numbers. Recall that $\omega\otimes\sigma$ feeds the first $n$ entries to $\omega$, the remaining $m$ to $\sigma$ and multiplies the two numbers. The sign makes the result antisymmetric; the factors $1/n!\,m!$ compensate for the fact that $\omega$ and $\sigma$
are already antisymmetric (``one does not want to pick up ever more factors''). The map is $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear (a function multiplying $\omega$ or $\sigma$ just pulls out, and it is additive anyway): as one works with modules, linearity is automatically $C^\infty(M)$-linearity. It is also called the \textbf{exterior product} \ts{12}{1}{11:48} \ts{12}{1}{12:09} \ts{12}{1}{12:29} \ts{12}{1}{12:51} \ts{12}{1}{13:12} \ts{12}{1}{13:32} \ts{12}{1}{13:52} \ts{12}{1}{14:55} \ts{12}{1}{15:15} \ts{12}{1}{15:36} \ts{12}{1}{15:58}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[A slip in the weights]
The lecture says ``one over $m$ factorial, one over $m$ factorial''; what is meant (and consistent with the example) is $1/(n!\,m!)$, as above. The simplest example is the one in the lecture: for two $1$-forms $\omega\wedge\sigma=\omega\otimes\sigma-\sigma\otimes\omega$ (the factors are $1/(1!\,1!)=1$). The lecture stresses that this is the convention chosen \emph{here}, that of
no extra weight; other conventions put a weight in the definition. Not said in class beyond the remark. \ts{12}{1}{14:13} \ts{12}{1}{14:34}
\end{coursenote}

\section{Pull-back of forms}\label{sec:gr-pullback}

Questions so far? None \ts{12}{1}{16:18}. Recall (\cref{sec:tb-pullback}) that a smooth map $h\colon M\to N$ induces the pull-back maps $h^*\colon T^*_{h(p)}N\to T^*_pM$ at points $p$, going the \emph{other} direction. Freeing oneself of the point, one can pull back an entire covector field, i.e.\ a $1$-form on $N$, to a $1$-form on $M$, by doing it at every point; this is a linear map
$\Omega^1(N)\to\Omega^1(M)$ \ts{12}{1}{16:18} \ts{12}{1}{16:39} \ts{12}{1}{16:59} \ts{12}{1}{17:21} \ts{12}{1}{17:43} \ts{12}{1}{18:03} \ts{12}{1}{18:24}.
\begin{recall}[Pull-back and push-forward]
The push-forward $h_*\colon T_pM\to T_{h(p)}N$ and the pull-back of covectors were defined in \cref{def:tb-push,def:tb-pull}; the motto ``vectors are pushed forward, covectors are pulled back'' is \cref{int:tb-motto}.
\end{recall}
Does this work with $n$-forms? In fact it does.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: pull-back of an $n$-form]\label{def:gr-pullback}
Let $h\colon M\to N$ be smooth and $\omega\in\Omega^n(N)$. The pull-back $h^*\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$ is defined by
\[
  (h^*\omega)(X_1,\dots,X_n):=\omega\bigl(h_*X_1,\dots,h_*X_n\bigr),
\]
using in every argument the push-forward, \emph{at every point}: $(h^*\omega)_p(v_1,\dots,v_n)=\omega_{h(p)}(h_*v_1,\dots,h_*v_n)$ for $v_i\in T_pM$ \ts{12}{1}{18:47} \ts{12}{1}{19:07} \ts{12}{1}{19:28} \ts{12}{1}{19:49} \ts{12}{1}{20:09} \ts{12}{1}{20:30} \ts{12}{1}{20:50}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Push-forward of vector fields]
The lecture writes the push-forward of the vector field and says that it is ``the same construction'' as for vectors, done at every point. Strictly, the push-forward of a \emph{field} $X$ by $h$ is not defined unless $h$ is a diffeomorphism (two points of $M$ can have the same image); the pull-back is defined pointwise as above, which is what is meant. Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}
One has to check that $h^*\omega$ is $C^\infty(M)$-linear (functions multiplying an $X_i$ jump out, since the push-forward is linear at every point) and that it remains antisymmetric (only the individual entries are touched and $\omega$ is an $n$-form), so it is an $n$-form \ts{12}{1}{20:50} \ts{12}{1}{21:11} \ts{12}{1}{21:32}.
The mantra extends: \textbf{forms are pulled back}, not pushed forward \ts{12}{1}{21:32} \ts{12}{1}{21:52} \ts{12}{1}{22:14}.

\begin{proposition}[Pull-back distributes over the wedge]\label{prop:gr-pullback-wedge}
For $\omega\in\Omega^n(N)$ and $\sigma\in\Omega^m(N)$ one has $h^*(\omega\wedge\sigma)=h^*\omega\wedge h^*\sigma$ \ts{12}{1}{22:35} \ts{12}{1}{22:57} \ts{12}{1}{23:18} \ts{12}{1}{23:38}.
\end{proposition}
It can be shown in a few lines from the definitions; it is on the problem sheet, ``to get some facility in manipulating these expressions'' (\cref{exam:ch12}) \ts{12}{1}{24:00}.

\section{The Grassmann algebra}\label{sec:gr-grassmann}

One kept saying that the wedge acts on a certain space, but it takes $\Omega^n(M)\times\Omega^m(M)\to\Omega^{n+m}(M)$: one would like one space that is \emph{closed} under the wedge \ts{12}{1}{24:21} \ts{12}{1}{24:41}. There is one.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: the Grassmann algebra $\Omega(M)$]\label{def:gr-grassmann}
The \textbf{Grassmann algebra} of $M$ (other names are in use) is
\[
  \Omega(M):=\Omega^0(M)\oplus\Omega^1(M)\oplus\dots\oplus\Omega^{\dim M}(M),
\]
a finite direct sum, since there are no forms beyond the top forms (without mention of the degree, which is the $n$ of an $n$-form, or its rank as a tensor). As a set the direct sum is the Cartesian product, with the componentwise addition (\cref{sec:rm-projective}), so $\Omega(M)$ is a $C^\infty(M)$-module. Here $\Omega^0(M)=C^\infty(M)$ are the
smooth functions and $\Omega^1(M)$ the covector fields, special cases met before; then the higher forms, up to the top forms. The wedge is extended to $\Omega(M)$ by \textbf{linear continuation}: it is $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear on $\Omega(M)$, and on the homogeneous pieces it is the wedge of \cref{def:gr-wedge}. A module with such a bilinear map is an \textbf{algebra} (\cref{def:tv-algebra},
here over the ring $C^\infty(M)$), the \emph{Grassmann algebra} (also: algebra of forms), written $\bigl(\Omega(M),+,\cdot,\wedge\bigr)$ \ts{12}{1}{24:41} \ts{12}{1}{25:02} \ts{12}{1}{25:25} \ts{12}{1}{25:46} \ts{12}{1}{26:07} \ts{12}{1}{26:28} \ts{12}{1}{26:48} \ts{12}{1}{27:09} \ts{12}{1}{27:29} \ts{12}{1}{27:50} \ts{12}{1}{28:10} \ts{12}{1}{28:31} \ts{12}{1}{28:51} \ts{12}{1}{29:13}.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}[Linear continuation]\label{ex:gr-linear-cont}
Let $\psi=\omega+\sigma$ with $\omega\in\Omega^1(M)$ and $\sigma\in\Omega^3(M)$, and $\varphi\in\Omega^n(M)$. How can one add a $1$-form to a $3$-form? Not with the addition of tensors of a certain rank, but with the addition of $\Omega(M)$, coming from the direct sum: both are parts of the big space. Then
\[
  \psi\wedge\varphi=\omega\wedge\varphi+\sigma\wedge\varphi,
\]
where $\omega\wedge\varphi\in\Omega^{n+1}(M)$, $\sigma\wedge\varphi\in\Omega^{n+3}(M)$, and the plus on the right is again that of $\Omega(M)$. This is the principle of linear continuation: distributivity because the wedge is bilinear on $\Omega(M)$, reduced to the wedge on the specific subspaces. When one calculates one hardly sees it
\ts{12}{1}{29:35} \ts{12}{1}{29:56} \ts{12}{1}{30:16} \ts{12}{1}{30:37} \ts{12}{1}{30:58} \ts{12}{1}{31:19} \ts{12}{1}{31:39} \ts{12}{1}{32:01} \ts{12}{1}{32:22} \ts{12}{1}{32:43} \ts{12}{1}{33:04}.
\end{example}
\begin{aside}[Grassmann numbers]
In supersymmetry one speaks of Grassmann numbers, ``numbers that do not commute but anti-commute''. But it is not the numbers that commute or not: how could objects commute? It is an \emph{operation} that is commutative or not (the addition is commutative; the Lie bracket, a bilinear map, is antisymmetric). That is the first misnomer. The whole secret of Grassmann numbers is that they can be understood by reducing them to such a Grassmann algebra (hence the name). Worth emphasising
\ts{12}{1}{33:25} \ts{12}{1}{33:51} \ts{12}{1}{34:11} \ts{12}{1}{34:32} \ts{12}{1}{34:52}.
\end{aside}
\begin{history}[Grassmann]
\emph{Not said in class.} Hermann Grassmann (1809--1877) introduced the algebra of the ``extension'' of geometric quantities in \emph{Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre} (Leipzig, 1844); the exterior algebra is named after him.\par
\smallskip
\emph{References.} H.~Grassmann, \emph{Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre}, Leipzig 1844.
\end{history}

\begin{theorem}[Commutation of the wedge on homogeneous forms]\label{thm:gr-commute}
Let $\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$ and $\sigma\in\Omega^m(M)$. Then
\[
  \omega\wedge\sigma=(-1)^{nm}\,\sigma\wedge\omega.
\]
\ts{12}{1}{35:14} \ts{12}{1}{35:35} \ts{12}{1}{35:56}
\end{theorem}
It is easy to prove. But it is true only for \emph{specific} $n$- and $m$-forms, not for arbitrary elements of $\Omega(M)$, which can be a superposition of an $n$-form and an $(n+2)$-form. For general $\psi,\varphi\in\Omega(M)$ there is no relation between $\psi\wedge\varphi$ and $\varphi\wedge\psi$: the different parts have different commutation behaviour, so one must break them up
by linear continuation into the summands of the direct sum and apply the rule on each. The wedge neither commutes nor anti-commutes on $\Omega(M)$: ``this just as a warning'' \ts{12}{1}{36:17} \ts{12}{1}{36:38} \ts{12}{1}{36:59} \ts{12}{1}{37:20} \ts{12}{1}{37:41} \ts{12}{1}{38:03}.
\begin{exercise}[The sign of the wedge]\label{ex:gr-sign}
Show $\omega\wedge\sigma=(-1)^{nm}\sigma\wedge\omega$ from \cref{def:gr-wedge}, by counting the transpositions that bring the block $(n+1,\dots,n+m,1,\dots,n)$ back to $(1,\dots,n+m)$ (one needs $nm$ of them). In particular $\omega\wedge\omega=0$ for odd $n$. \emph{Not said in class; the lecture says ``very simple to prove''.}
\end{exercise}

\section{The commutator of vector fields}\label{sec:gr-commutator}

The question now is differentiation. Functions were differentiated through the gradient, maps through the push-forward (their differential); what is the derivative of a form? First a notion needed in the definition, an aside of its own, ``extremely important also later on when we discuss Lie groups and Lie algebras'' \ts{12}{1}{38:03} \ts{12}{1}{38:24} \ts{12}{1}{46:46} \ts{12}{1}{47:08} \ts{12}{1}{47:28}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: commutator of two vector fields]\label{def:gr-commutator}
For $X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)$ the \textbf{commutator} $[X,Y]$ is defined by its action on smooth functions $f$,
\[
  [X,Y](f):=X\bigl(Y(f)\bigr)-Y\bigl(X(f)\bigr).
\]
It is anti-symmetric, $[X,Y]=-[Y,X]$, and, as can be shown (problem sheet), \emph{only this combination} (not $X$ after $Y$, nor $Y$ after $X$) is again a vector field: it is $\R$-linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule, and it is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear in the sense that makes it a vector field. The commutator thus constructs a new vector field out of two
\ts{12}{1}{47:50} \ts{12}{1}{48:11} \ts{12}{1}{48:32} \ts{12}{1}{48:52}.
\end{definition}
\begin{exercise}[The commutator is a vector field]\label{ex:gr-comm}
Show that $[X,Y]$ is a derivation of $C^\infty(M)$ (\cref{def:tv-derivation}): the second-order terms cancel and $[X,Y](fg)=[X,Y](f)\,g+f\,[X,Y](g)$; and that $[X,fY]=X(f)\,Y+f[X,Y]$. \emph{Not said in class: left to the problem sheet.}
\end{exercise}

\section{The exterior derivative}\label{sec:gr-d}

Recall the gradient (\cref{def:tb-gradient}): to a smooth function $f$ it assigns the $1$-form $\mathrm df$, defined at each point $p$ by $\mathrm d_pf$ and now taken at every point. So $\mathrm d$ takes $0$-forms to $1$-forms. The aim is to extend it so that it takes $n$-forms to $(n+1)$-forms \ts{12}{1}{38:24} \ts{12}{1}{38:47} \ts{12}{1}{39:07} \ts{12}{1}{39:29} \ts{12}{1}{39:49} \ts{12}{1}{40:09}.

Why not extend it to arbitrary tensors? Because on arbitrary tensors one cannot write down a derivative without \emph{adding further structure} to the manifold (a covariant derivative, for later). On the totally antisymmetric tensors, the forms, there is a notion of derivative that can be defined \emph{purely in terms of the differentiable structure}: the exterior derivative. In keeping with
the philosophy of always keeping track of which structure is needed for which definition, an important point \ts{12}{1}{40:09} \ts{12}{1}{40:30} \ts{12}{1}{40:50} \ts{12}{1}{41:11}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: exterior derivative]\label{def:gr-d}
The \textbf{exterior derivative} is the map $\mathrm d\colon\Omega^n(M)\to\Omega^{n+1}(M)$ defined, for $\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$ and vector fields $X_1,\dots,X_{n+1}$, by
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathrm d\omega(X_1,\dots,X_{n+1}):={}&\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{i+1}\,X_i\bigl(\omega(X_1,\dots,\widehat{X_i},\dots,X_{n+1})\bigr)\\
  &+\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\,\omega\bigl([X_i,X_j],X_1,\dots,\widehat{X_i},\dots,\widehat{X_j},\dots,X_{n+1}\bigr),
  \end{aligned}
\]
where a hat means that the entry is left out. In the first sum the $i$-th vector acts, as a directional derivative, on the \emph{function} $\omega(\dots)$ obtained by feeding $\omega$ with all the other $n$ vectors; ``democratically for every entry''. The sign $(-1)^{i+1}$ takes care of antisymmetry. In the second sum, over the ordered pairs $i<j$, two entries are taken out and
replaced by their commutator in the first slot: $n$ entries remain, as needed. It is the coordinate-free definition. For $n=0$ it is the gradient: $\mathrm df(X)=X(f)$ \ts{12}{1}{41:11} \ts{12}{1}{41:33} \ts{12}{1}{41:54} \ts{12}{1}{42:15} \ts{12}{1}{42:36} \ts{12}{1}{42:57} \ts{12}{1}{43:17} \ts{12}{1}{43:38} \ts{12}{1}{43:59} \ts{12}{1}{44:20} \ts{12}{1}{44:40} \ts{12}{1}{45:01} \ts{12}{1}{45:22}.
\end{definition}
\begin{coursenote}[Slips in the definition]
The lecture says at first ``takes an $n$-form to an $m$-form'' and corrects it to $n+1$. It also first claims that antisymmetry in the second sum is taken care of by the commutator alone, and later, in the example, finds that the additional sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ is needed; the formula above has it. The first sum starts with the sign written as $(-1)^{i+1}$.
\ts{12}{1}{44:20} \ts{12}{1}{50:16}
\end{coursenote}
One has to prove that $\mathrm d\omega$ is indeed an $(n+1)$-form. Total antisymmetry is clear from the definition (exchange two entries and pick up an overall minus sign). What needs checking is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity: additivity in each slot is quick, and the crucial question is whether a function multiplying any of the entries pulls out. Intuitively one wants that; the second term (with the
commutator) is exactly what is needed so that $\mathrm d\omega$ is really a tensor field \ts{12}{1}{45:43} \ts{12}{1}{46:05} \ts{12}{1}{46:26}.

\begin{example}[The case $n=1$, checked without components]\label{ex:gr-d1}
For a $1$-form $\omega$ the definition gives, with vector fields $X,Y$ (two entries; the only ordered pair is $(1,2)$ with sign $(-1)^{1+2}=-1$),
\[
  \mathrm d\omega(X,Y)=X\bigl(\omega(Y)\bigr)-Y\bigl(\omega(X)\bigr)-\omega\bigl([X,Y]\bigr).
\]
Additivity is obvious, and by antisymmetry it suffices to check the scaling in one entry: $Y\to fY$ with $f\in C^\infty(M)$. The claim $\mathrm d\omega(X,fY)=f\,\mathrm d\omega(X,Y)$ uses the last term:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathrm d\omega(X,fY)&=X\bigl(f\omega(Y)\bigr)-fY\bigl(\omega(X)\bigr)\\
  &\quad-\omega\bigl([X,fY]\bigr),\\
  X\bigl(f\omega(Y)\bigr)&=X(f)\,\omega(Y)+f\,X\bigl(\omega(Y)\bigr),\\
  [X,fY]&=X(f)\,Y+f\,[X,Y].
  \end{aligned}
\]
The first line uses that $\omega$ is a covector field (the $f$ jumps in front), the second that the vector field $X$ is a derivation (Leibniz rule), the third \cref{ex:gr-comm}. Putting together, the terms $X(f)\,\omega(Y)$ cancel:
\[
  \mathrm d\omega(X,fY)=f\,\bigl(X(\omega(Y))-Y(\omega(X))-\omega([X,Y])\bigr).
\]
Without the last term one would get only $\R$-linearity, not $C^\infty(M)$-linearity, and $\mathrm d\omega$ would not be a tensor field. ``All of this without choosing any components, any basis, because we do not even know whether we have a global basis'' (\cref{int:rm-nocomp})
\ts{12}{1}{49:14} \ts{12}{1}{49:35} \ts{12}{1}{49:56} \ts{12}{1}{50:37} \ts{12}{1}{50:58} \ts{12}{1}{51:18} \ts{12}{1}{51:39} \ts{12}{1}{52:00} \ts{12}{1}{52:20} \ts{12}{1}{52:42} \ts{12}{1}{53:03} \ts{12}{1}{53:24} \ts{12}{1}{53:46} \ts{12}{1}{54:07} \ts{12}{1}{54:28} \ts{12}{1}{54:49} \ts{12}{1}{55:09} \ts{12}{1}{55:30} \ts{12}{1}{55:50} \ts{12}{1}{56:11}.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Graded Leibniz rule]\label{thm:gr-leibniz}
For $\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$ and $\varphi\in\Omega^m(M)$
\[
  \mathrm d(\omega\wedge\varphi)=\mathrm d\omega\wedge\varphi+(-1)^n\,\omega\wedge\mathrm d\varphi.
\]
\ts{12}{1}{56:39} \ts{12}{1}{56:59} \ts{12}{1}{57:20} \ts{12}{1}{57:41} \ts{12}{1}{58:01}
\end{theorem}
One expects a Leibniz rule (product rule): a kind of differentiation and a kind of product. But it is not the ordinary one, since neither is $\mathrm d$ an ordinary differentiation nor $\wedge$ an ordinary product: there is an extra sign, in which only the degree of $\omega$ counts (the degree of $\varphi$ is irrelevant). One calls this a \emph{graded} action, and $\Omega(M)$ a \emph{graded algebra},
since commuting or anti-commuting depends on the degree. The formula is not in the transcript; it is the standard one with the conventions chosen here [?]. This can be proven; it is to be memorised (it might be on the problem sheet) \ts{12}{1}{58:01} \ts{12}{1}{58:21} \ts{12}{1}{58:42}.

\begin{theorem}[The exterior derivative commutes with the pull-back]\label{thm:gr-pullback-d}
For a smooth map $h\colon M\to N$ and $\omega\in\Omega^n(N)$
\[
  h^*(\mathrm d\omega)=\mathrm d(h^*\omega).
\]
\ts{12}{1}{58:42} \ts{12}{1}{59:03} \ts{12}{1}{59:24} \ts{12}{1}{59:47}
\end{theorem}
``Wildly, really wildly important'': it deserves a box, ``it comes as a gift''. The two $\mathrm d$'s are not literally the same operator: the left one acts on an $n$-form on $N$ (it makes an $(n+1)$-form on $N$, which is then pulled back), the right one on the $n$-form $h^*\omega$ on $M$, making it an $(n+1)$-form on $M$. A student points out that they are on different manifolds; the lecturer agrees
(``that was not the point I wanted to make, but it is true''): symbolically it commutes, and one can remember it that way, knowing what one is doing \ts{12}{1}{59:47} \ts{12}{1}{1:00:07} \ts{12}{1}{1:00:33} \ts{12}{1}{1:00:53} \ts{12}{1}{1:01:14} \ts{12}{1}{1:01:34}.
Similarly $\mathrm d$ extends, by linear continuation (or at least additivity), to the whole Grassmann algebra $\Omega(M)\to\Omega(M)$: a sum of a $2$-form and a $3$-form goes to the sum of a $3$-form and a $4$-form \ts{12}{1}{1:01:58} \ts{12}{1}{1:02:20} \ts{12}{1}{1:02:41}.

\section{Two physical examples}\label{sec:gr-examples}

Two brief examples, since there is not yet enough technology to embellish them \ts{12}{1}{1:02:41} \ts{12}{1}{1:03:01}.

\begin{example}[Electrodynamics: the homogeneous Maxwell equations]\label{ex:gr-maxwell}
Books such as Jackson give four equations and say that is Maxwell electrodynamics. But are there other electrodynamics, and which equations can be changed without leaving the domain of electrodynamics? Any electrodynamics talks about the field strength $F$, a $2$-form (in components on a four-manifold, containing the $E$ and the $B$ field), and one requires that the $3$-form $\mathrm dF$ vanish:
\[
  \mathrm dF=0\quad\text{in }\Omega^3(M),
\]
the \textbf{homogeneous Maxwell equations}. It looks like one tensorial equation instead of two Maxwell equations, but it covers both. In standard electrodynamics one plays with curls and divergences and finds that $F$ can be expressed as the exterior derivative of a $1$-form, $F=\mathrm dA$; $A$ is the \textbf{gauge potential} (or potential), $F$ the field strength. Is the implication
$\mathrm dF=0\Rightarrow\exists A\colon F=\mathrm dA$ really true? Yes, if space-time is $\R^4$; it fails as soon as one removes, e.g., entire lines from $\R^4$: that is the topic of the end of this lecture \ts{12}{1}{1:03:23} \ts{12}{1}{1:03:43} \ts{12}{1}{1:04:04} \ts{12}{1}{1:04:24} \ts{12}{1}{1:04:48} \ts{12}{1}{1:05:09} \ts{12}{1}{1:05:29} \ts{12}{1}{1:05:51} \ts{12}{1}{1:06:12} \ts{12}{1}{1:06:32}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Mechanics: the symplectic form is closed]\label{ex:gr-symplectic}
On the phase space $T^*Q$ (\cref{ex:gr-examples}) the symplectic form $\omega$ is a $2$-form (the omega ``does not live on $M$, it lives on the cotangent bundle of the configuration manifold''; the generalisation is a \emph{symplectic manifold}), with a second condition: it must be \emph{non-degenerate} (later). One further requires that it be \textbf{closed}, $\mathrm d\omega=0$, as in the electrodynamic case.
On $T^*Q$ this is automatic: one can always write down a $1$-form $\theta=p_i\,\mathrm dq^i$ (in the lecture ``$P_I\,\mathrm dQ_I$'', with $q^i$, $p_i$ the coordinates of a point of the cotangent bundle, $i=1,\dots,\dim Q$, constructed much like the coordinates of the tangent bundle), the \emph{symplectic potential} (``an analogy''), and defines $\omega$ through $\mathrm d\theta$ (up to a sign convention): then $\mathrm d\omega=\mathrm d\,\mathrm d\theta=0$,
see $\mathrm d^2=0$ below. If instead one is given an even dimensional manifold $M$ with a $2$-form $\omega$, one requires $\mathrm d\omega=0$ by force and non-degeneracy, and calls $(M,\omega)$ a \textbf{symplectic manifold}: ``that is essentially the whole business of Hamiltonian mechanics''. Coming from the Lagrangian formalism one goes to the cotangent bundle, but canonical transformations violate the bundle structure, so one must think more generally in terms of symplectic
manifolds. If this seems mysterious, take it as an example where in electrodynamics and in classical mechanics the exterior derivative plays an important structural role. The question under which circumstances the vanishing of an exterior derivative implies that the form is itself an exterior derivative gives rise to cohomology \ts{12}{1}{1:06:53} \ts{12}{1}{1:07:13} \ts{12}{1}{1:07:33} \ts{12}{1}{1:07:53} \ts{12}{1}{1:08:14} \ts{12}{1}{1:08:36} \ts{12}{1}{1:08:56} \ts{12}{1}{1:09:17} \ts{12}{1}{1:09:37} \ts{12}{1}{1:09:58} \ts{12}{1}{1:10:18} \ts{12}{1}{1:10:38}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[The symplectic potential]
The lecture says that $p_i$ and $\mathrm dq_i$ ``are the components of a point of the cotangent bundle'' and that one can ``trivially find one such thing, at least in some local patch'', but that in fact it always exists: the coordinates $(q^i,p_i)$ are coordinates on $T^*Q$ induced by a chart of $Q$, so $p_i\,\mathrm dq^i$ is defined in the chart; that it is a globally defined $1$-form (the canonical one) is the content of the ``canonically defined'' remark, not proven in class. Not said in class.
\ts{12}{1}{1:09:37} \ts{12}{1}{1:09:58}

\section{$\mathrm d^2=0$}\label{sec:gr-dd}

The basic theorem on which the whole business of cohomology is built \ts{12}{1}{1:10:59} \ts{12}{1}{1:11:27}.
\begin{theorem}[$\mathrm d^2=0$]\label{thm:gr-dd}
$\mathrm d\circ\mathrm d=0$: for every $n$-form $\omega$ the $(n+2)$-form $\mathrm d(\mathrm d\omega)$ vanishes \ts{12}{1}{1:11:27} \ts{12}{1}{1:11:47} \ts{12}{1}{1:12:10}.
\end{theorem}
One could show this directly from the definition, ``a little involved''; it is immediate in local coordinates. First some notation.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: antisymmetrisation and symmetrisation brackets]\label{def:gr-brackets}
For an object $O_{m_1\dots m_f}$ with $f$ indices (``it does not mean what it is'': it may be a family of numbers or of forms) one defines
\[
  O_{[m_1\dots m_f]}:=\frac1{f!}\sum_\pi\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,O_{m_{\pi(1)}\dots m_{\pi(f)}},
\]
the sum over all permutations of the $f$ numbers; the \emph{symmetrisation} bracket $O_{(m_1\dots m_f)}$ with round brackets is the same without the sign of the permutation \ts{12}{1}{1:15:17} \ts{12}{1}{1:15:38} \ts{12}{1}{1:16:00} \ts{12}{1}{1:16:21} \ts{12}{1}{1:16:41}.
\end{definition}
Two small results (``try to show it from the definition, a two-liner''), valid for more indices too \ts{12}{1}{1:16:41} \ts{12}{1}{1:17:02}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $A^{ab}$ is antisymmetric, then $A^{ab}B_{ab}=A^{ab}B_{[ab]}$: the brackets may be inserted when contracting with something antisymmetric (``a full contraction with something totally antisymmetric induces an antisymmetry'').
  \item A contraction of something symmetric with something antisymmetric in the contracted indices is zero: $S^{ab}A_{ab}=0$ if $S^{ab}=S^{ba}$ and $A_{ab}=-A_{ba}$ \ts{12}{1}{1:17:23} \ts{12}{1}{1:17:44} \ts{12}{1}{1:18:05} \ts{12}{1}{1:18:26} \ts{12}{1}{1:18:46} \ts{12}{1}{1:19:06}.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:gr-dd}]
In a chart, with respect to the coordinate-induced basis, an $n$-form has component functions $\omega_{a_1\dots a_n}$ (functions, since $\omega$ is a field) and
\[
  \mathrm d\omega=\frac1{n!}\,\partial_b\omega_{a_1\dots a_n}\,\mathrm dx^b\wedge\mathrm dx^{a_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm dx^{a_n}
\]
(the lecture writes it without the factor, ``to be done in detail on the problem sheet to get the factors right''). Apply $\mathrm d$ once more:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathrm d^2\omega=\frac1{n!}\,\partial_c\partial_b\omega_{a_1\dots a_n}\ &\mathrm dx^c\wedge\mathrm dx^b\\
  &\wedge\mathrm dx^{a_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm dx^{a_n}.
  \end{aligned}
\]
The product of $1$-forms is antisymmetric in all indices $c,b,a_1,\dots,a_n$ (an exchange of any two picks up a minus sign), so antisymmetrisation brackets may be put on the indices of the coefficient (first result). In particular $\partial_{[c}\partial_{b]}\omega_{\dots}$ appears. But $\partial_c\partial_b\omega=\partial_b\partial_c\omega$ is symmetric in $c,b$ if the coefficients are at least $C^2$ (Schwarz), and the contraction of symmetric with antisymmetric
indices is zero (second result). Hence $\mathrm d^2\omega=0$. The zero lives in the space of $(n+2)$-forms \ts{12}{1}{1:12:30} \ts{12}{1}{1:12:50} \ts{12}{1}{1:13:11} \ts{12}{1}{1:13:32} \ts{12}{1}{1:13:53} \ts{12}{1}{1:14:14} \ts{12}{1}{1:14:34} \ts{12}{1}{1:14:55} \ts{12}{1}{1:19:26} \ts{12}{1}{1:19:47} \ts{12}{1}{1:20:08}.
\end{proof}
\begin{coursenote}[Smoothness and locality]
The proof is done in a chart: $\mathrm d$ has been defined without coordinates, and the identity is local (one may check it on a chart around any point), so it holds on $M$; the components are smooth, hence $C^2$. In the lecture the board has a few index misprints (``$\mathrm dx^c\wedge\mathrm dx^d\wedge\mathrm dx^{a\,b}$''). Not said in class.
\ts{12}{1}{1:14:14}
\end{coursenote}
Why important? Lots follows from $\mathrm d^2=0$ \ts{12}{1}{1:20:08} \ts{12}{1}{1:20:40}.

\section{Exact and closed forms}\label{sec:gr-exact}

\paragraph{The sequence.} One has a chain of maps: from the $0$-forms (the smooth functions) $\mathrm d$ goes to the $1$-forms, then to $2$-forms, and so on up to the top forms $\Omega^{\dim M}(M)$; applying $\mathrm d$ to a top form gives a $(\dim M+1)$-form, which is always zero. In front one puts a trivial vector space containing only the zero element, $\{0\}$ (there is none smaller), and defines a map $\{0\}\to\Omega^0(M)$ sending
the zero to the zero function; at the end the target $\{0\}$ again:
\[
  \{0\}\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm d\ }\Omega^0(M)\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm d\ }\Omega^1(M)\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm d\ }\cdots\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm d\ }\Omega^{\dim M}(M)\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm d\ }\{0\}.
\]
All the $\mathrm d$'s are different maps (from $\Omega^n$ to $\Omega^{n+1}$), but they are all called $\mathrm d$; one must keep in mind from where to where it maps \ts{12}{1}{1:20:40} \ts{12}{1}{1:21:01} \ts{12}{1}{1:21:21} \ts{12}{1}{1:21:41} \ts{12}{1}{1:22:02} \ts{12}{1}{1:22:22} \ts{12}{1}{1:22:44} \ts{12}{1}{1:23:05} \ts{12}{1}{1:25:09}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: image and kernel]\label{def:gr-imker}
For a linear map $\phi\colon P\to Q$ between modules (vector spaces) the \textbf{image} is $\operatorname{im}\phi:=\{\phi(p)\mid p\in P\}\subseteq Q$ and the \textbf{kernel} is $\ker\phi:=\{p\in P\mid\phi(p)=0\}\subseteq P$, a submodule (sub-vector space) of the domain \ts{12}{1}{1:23:25} \ts{12}{1}{1:23:46} \ts{12}{1}{1:24:07} \ts{12}{1}{1:24:27} \ts{12}{1}{1:24:48}.
\end{definition}
Look at $\Omega^{n-1}(M)\to\Omega^n(M)\to\Omega^{n+1}(M)$: the image of the first $\mathrm d$ and the kernel of the second both are subsets of the same space $\Omega^n(M)$. The statement $\mathrm d\circ\mathrm d=0$ says: the first $\mathrm d$ takes one into $\Omega^n$, the second takes the result to zero; that is, the image of the first lies in the kernel of the second:
\[
  \operatorname{im}\bigl(\mathrm d\colon\Omega^{n-1}\to\Omega^n\bigr)\ \subseteq\ \ker\bigl(\mathrm d\colon\Omega^n\to\Omega^{n+1}\bigr).
\]
\ts{12}{1}{1:25:29} \ts{12}{1}{1:25:49} \ts{12}{1}{1:26:10} \ts{12}{1}{1:26:30} \ts{12}{1}{1:26:51} \ts{12}{1}{1:27:11} \ts{12}{1}{1:27:45}
\begin{coursenote}[Vector space, not module]
The lecture calls the kernel a sub-\emph{module}. For the module maps of \cref{ch:l11} that is right, but $\mathrm d$ is \emph{not} $C^\infty(M)$-linear: $\mathrm d(f\omega)=\mathrm df\wedge\omega+f\,\mathrm d\omega\ne f\,\mathrm d\omega$ in general (graded Leibniz rule). It is only $\R$-linear, so the kernel and the image of $\mathrm d$ are real vector subspaces, not $C^\infty(M)$-submodules, and the cohomology groups below are real vector spaces. Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: exact and closed forms]\label{def:gr-exact}
An $n$-form $\omega\in\Omega^n(M)$ is called \textbf{exact} if it lies in the image of $\mathrm d\colon\Omega^{n-1}\to\Omega^n$, i.e.\ if there is an $(n-1)$-form $\alpha$ with $\omega=\mathrm d\alpha$. It is called \textbf{closed} if it lies in the kernel of $\mathrm d\colon\Omega^n\to\Omega^{n+1}$ (``not of the same $\mathrm d$ but of the next one''), i.e.\ if $\mathrm d\omega=0$. A general form is neither \ts{12}{1}{1:28:22} \ts{12}{1}{1:28:42} \ts{12}{1}{1:29:02} \ts{12}{1}{1:29:24} \ts{12}{1}{1:29:44} \ts{12}{1}{1:30:05} \ts{12}{1}{1:30:46} \ts{12}{1}{1:31:08}.
\end{definition}
From $\mathrm d^2=0$: \textbf{every exact form is closed} \ts{12}{1}{1:30:25} \ts{12}{1}{1:31:28}. One must be able to switch between the formulations ``exists $\alpha$ with $\omega=\mathrm d\alpha$'' and ``in the image''.
Back to electrodynamics: if one has a $1$-form $A$ (a potential) and defines $F:=\mathrm dA$, then $\mathrm dF=\mathrm d^2A=0$: a field strength with a gauge potential satisfies the homogeneous Maxwell equations (``that is why one chooses that electrodynamics if one can''). What the homogeneous Maxwell equations say is that $F$ is \emph{closed}. The question is whether the implication can be turned around: is the closed $F$ also exact?
That is: under what circumstances are the inclusions at each place of the sequence actually equalities? It is of immediate importance in electrodynamics and in many other fields \ts{12}{1}{1:31:28} \ts{12}{1}{1:31:50} \ts{12}{1}{1:32:11} \ts{12}{1}{1:32:31} \ts{12}{1}{1:32:52} \ts{12}{1}{1:33:13} \ts{12}{1}{1:33:34}.

The traditional (``needless'') notation: $B^n(M)$ for the exact $n$-forms (the image of $\mathrm d$ into $\Omega^n$) and $Z^n(M)$ for the closed $n$-forms (the kernel of $\mathrm d$ on $\Omega^n$); the inclusion is $B^n(M)\subseteq Z^n(M)$. The notation does save writing which $\mathrm d$ one means \ts{12}{1}{1:33:34} \ts{12}{1}{1:34:03} \ts{12}{1}{1:34:23} \ts{12}{1}{1:34:44} \ts{12}{1}{1:35:05} \ts{12}{1}{1:35:26}.

\begin{theorem}[Poincar\'e lemma]\label{thm:gr-poincare}
On $M=\R^m$ one has $B^n(\R^m)=Z^n(\R^m)$ for all $n>0$: every closed form of positive degree is exact \ts{12}{1}{1:35:48} \ts{12}{1}{1:36:08} \ts{12}{1}{1:36:29}.
\end{theorem}
The case $n=0$ is looked at below: there the equality fails (the closed $0$-forms are the constant functions, the exact ones only the zero function, since there are no $(-1)$-forms). So are the two always equal? \emph{No}; but on $\R^m$ they are. In electrodynamics on $\R^4$, an infinitely extended space-time, one can go back and forth between the homogeneous Maxwell equations and the gauge potential; that is only on $\R^4$. Already if one removes a piece of space (say a long coil of an electromagnet)
one starts disturbing this; that plays a role in the \emph{Aharonov--Bohm effect} in quantum mechanics, and it is not esoteric: experimental setups that produce this effect are easily built \ts{12}{1}{1:36:29} \ts{12}{1}{1:36:50} \ts{12}{1}{1:37:11} \ts{12}{1}{1:37:32}.
\begin{history}[Aharonov--Bohm]
\emph{Not said in class.} The effect is named after Y.~Aharonov and D.~Bohm, who predicted in 1959 that a charged particle is affected by the electromagnetic potential even in a region where the field strength vanishes.\par
\smallskip
\emph{References.} Y.~Aharonov, D.~Bohm, \emph{Phys.\ Rev.} 115 (1959) 485.
\end{history}

\section{de Rham cohomology}\label{sec:gr-derham}

Question two: what if they are not equal? One would like to \emph{quantify} by how much the closed forms fail to be exact: it is one thing to know that they differ, another to know how much \ts{12}{1}{1:37:52} \ts{12}{1}{1:38:14} \ts{12}{1}{1:38:34}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: de Rham cohomology group]\label{def:gr-derham}
The \textbf{$n$-th de Rham cohomology group} of $M$ is the quotient vector space
\[
  H^n(M):=Z^n(M)\big/B^n(M).
\]
(``H like homology'': for a homology group the index would be down; this is a cohomology group, with the index up.) The quotient is short notation for the set of equivalence classes under the equivalence relation $\sim$ on $Z^n(M)$ given, for closed forms $\omega,\sigma$, by
\[
  \omega\sim\sigma\ :\Longleftrightarrow\ \omega-\sigma\ \text{is exact};
\]
the tilde depends on the exact forms, and people forget it and write only $B^n$. Nothing new: the old game of equivalence relations and of the quotient set of classes (\cref{ch:l03}), here a quotient vector space \ts{12}{1}{1:38:34} \ts{12}{1}{1:38:55} \ts{12}{1}{1:39:18} \ts{12}{1}{1:39:39} \ts{12}{1}{1:40:00} \ts{12}{1}{1:40:22} \ts{12}{1}{1:40:42}.
\end{definition}
(The lecture speaks of ``groups''; they are vector spaces over $\R$ since $\mathrm d$ is $\R$-linear.)

\begin{theorem}[de Rham, conceptual form]\label{thm:gr-derham}
The cohomology $H^n(M)$ depends only on the \emph{topology} of $M$. \ts{12}{1}{1:41:03} \ts{12}{1}{1:41:24}
\end{theorem}
Quoted only in its conceptual implication, not in its technical detail. This is remarkable: everything in the definition is expressed through exterior derivatives, which have to do with the local differentiable structure, yet the result depends on the global topology; two differentiable manifolds built on the same topological manifold have the same de Rham cohomology groups. Like the fundamental group (\cref{ch:l05})
these groups are topological invariants (they have a certain relation to the fundamental group, but not a simple one), and it is much easier to compute with them than to deal with open sets directly \ts{12}{1}{1:41:24} \ts{12}{1}{1:41:45} \ts{12}{1}{1:45:12} \ts{12}{1}{1:45:33} \ts{12}{1}{1:45:53} \ts{12}{1}{1:46:15}.
\begin{history}[de Rham]
\emph{Not said in class.} The theorem is due to Georges de Rham (1931), who proved that the cohomology of a smooth manifold computed by differential forms is the same as the topological cohomology with real coefficients.\par
\smallskip
\emph{References.} G.~de Rham, \emph{Sur l'analysis situs des vari\'et\'es \`a $n$ dimensions}, J.\ Math.\ Pures Appl.\ 10 (1931) 115--200.
\end{history}

\begin{example}[$H^0$ counts the connected components]\label{ex:gr-h0}
For a smooth real manifold $H^0(M)\cong\R^{\#}$, with $\#$ the number of connected pieces of $M$. The lecture leaves the proof to the problem sheet (time), with the idea: a $0$-form is closed if its exterior derivative, the gradient, vanishes everywhere, so it is a constant function; but on a space with several disconnected pieces the function can be constant with one value on one piece and
another value on another. There are no exact $0$-forms (other than $0$), so $H^0=Z^0$ is spanned by the locally constant functions \ts{12}{1}{1:42:05} \ts{12}{1}{1:42:26} \ts{12}{1}{1:42:47} \ts{12}{1}{1:43:08} \ts{12}{1}{1:43:28} \ts{12}{1}{1:43:49}.
It follows that $H^0(\R)\cong H^0(S^1)\cong\R$ (both connected). For $n\ge1$ one has $H^n(\R)=\{0\}$ (the vector space with just the zero element), but showing that already requires integration on manifolds, ``not in a heavy way'', which comes later (the lecture wants to state the theorem before the practical calculation). More generally, by the Poincar\'e lemma: $H^n(\R^m)=\{0\}$ for $n\ge1$, and $H^0(\R^m)=\R$
\ts{12}{1}{1:44:10} \ts{12}{1}{1:44:30} \ts{12}{1}{1:44:51} \ts{12}{1}{1:46:35}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[Closing remark]
The lecture ends with ``you can also show the other identity that I wrote down'' and continues next time; the identity is not specified. The summary at the end: $\mathrm d^2=0$ gives the sequence with inclusion of images in kernels; modding out the exact forms from the closed ones quantifies the failure; although everything depends on the differentiable structure the result is purely topological information. \ts{12}{1}{1:44:51} \ts{12}{1}{1:46:56}
\end{coursenote}
