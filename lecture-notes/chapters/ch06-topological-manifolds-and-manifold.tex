\section{Topological manifolds}\label{sec:mf-def}

A new chapter: topological manifolds and bundles \ts{6}{1}{0:08}. So far the lectures revisited material one has very likely seen in one form or another; from the bundles on there will be some new
results and a new type of thinking \ts{6}{1}{0:08}\ts{6}{1}{0:29}. Topological manifolds are still topological spaces, but very special ones, so special that they deserve an extra term. All the later manifolds,
differentiable manifolds and so on, will still be topological manifolds \ts{6}{1}{0:29}\ts{6}{1}{0:49}\ts{6}{1}{1:10}. \emph{Bundles} are a way to think about topological manifolds that have a certain structure,
which underlies much of modern physics, for instance the standard model of particle physics, in particular non-Abelian gauge theories; one really understands their structure only through bundles
\ts{6}{1}{1:10}\ts{6}{1}{1:30}.

Very roughly, a topological manifold is a topological space that \emph{locally} (the keyword) looks like some $\R^D$ for a fixed number $D$ \ts{6}{1}{1:30}\ts{6}{1}{1:51}\ts{6}{1}{2:13}. Examples are easy to draw: the surface of a
doughnut, the torus $T^2$, the surface of the sphere $S^2$, both looking locally like $\R^2$, and the pretzel; of course also for higher $D$, though $D=2$ is easiest to draw \ts{6}{1}{2:13}\ts{6}{1}{2:33}\ts{6}{1}{2:59}\ts{6}{1}{3:21}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: topological manifold]\label{def:mf-manifold}
A paracompact Hausdorff topological space $(M,\mathcal O)$ (so paracompactness and the Hausdorff property are raised to conditions here: \cref{def:iv-paracompact,def:iv-T2}) is called a \textbf{$D$-dimensional
topological manifold} if for every point $p\in M$ there exist an open neighbourhood $U\in\mathcal O$ with $p\in U$ and a homeomorphism
\[
  x\colon U\to x(U)\subseteq\R^D,
\]
with the \emph{same} $D$ for every point \ts{6}{1}{3:21}\ts{6}{1}{3:44}\ts{6}{1}{4:13}\ts{6}{1}{4:37}\ts{6}{1}{4:58}\ts{6}{1}{5:21}\ts{6}{1}{5:43}.
Here $\R^D$ carries the standard topology and $x(U)$ the induced topology; since $U$ is already open in $M$, the induced topology on $U$ is what is needed \ts{6}{1}{5:43}\ts{6}{1}{6:04}.
\end{definition}
Usually one says just ``manifold''; ``topological'' is used as a qualifier to distinguish it from manifolds with more structure, to come \ts{6}{1}{3:44}\ts{6}{1}{4:13}. This is the locality: for every point there is an open set
(without boundary, in the standard-topology sense) that is mapped by a homeomorphism into some subset of $\R^D$; seen through topological glasses it can be deformed into a piece of $\R^D$ \ts{6}{1}{6:04}\ts{6}{1}{6:26}\ts{6}{1}{6:48}\ts{6}{1}{7:08}.
\begin{recall}[Why the two conditions]
Paracompactness and the Hausdorff property are the two assumptions announced in \cref{sec:iv-compact}; together they give partitions of unity (\cref{thm:iv-pou}), used for integration later.
\end{recall}

\begin{example}[Seen through topological glasses]\label{ex:mf-glasses}
Looking at things through topological glasses means identifying all homeomorphic manifolds \ts{6}{1}{7:08}\ts{6}{1}{7:33}\ts{6}{1}{7:57}\ts{6}{1}{8:20}. The circle embedded in $\R^2$ (called ``$S^2$'' in the lecture as heard; it must be $S^1$ [?]) with the topology inherited from $\R^2$ is topologically the same as a square drawn instead: the square can be continuously deformed into the circle. Topology has no notion of shape. The same holds for $S^2$ embedded in $\R^3$;
and the real line is topologically equivalent to an open piece of a curve, whether drawn straight or not \ts{6}{1}{8:20}\ts{6}{1}{8:40}\ts{6}{1}{9:01}\ts{6}{1}{9:22}.
\end{example}
For topological \emph{spaces} it was very difficult to get an intuition for their weirdness, a very general notion; topological manifolds are very special spaces, but they still cover a wide range of the cases wanted in physics \ts{6}{1}{9:44}\ts{6}{1}{10:05}.

\begin{intuition}[Local versus global]\label{int:mf-local}
Compare $S^2$ with the real line: locally both look like a piece of $\R^D$, but globally, in contrast to locally, they are different manifolds. $S^2$ with the topology induced from the ambient space is compact, the real line is non-compact; compactness is a topological property, so the two
spaces cannot be homeomorphic \ts{6}{1}{10:05}\ts{6}{1}{10:25}\ts{6}{1}{10:47}\ts{6}{1}{11:07}.
\end{intuition}
\begin{coursenote}[Which $D$]
Here the comparison is between manifolds of the same dimension: the real line is one-dimensional, $S^2$ two-dimensional; the lecture's point is the local picture ``a piece of the real line'' versus the global compactness; the
comparison with $S^1$ would have been the cleaner one. Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}

\subsection{Submanifolds}

As always one wants to construct new manifolds from old ones \ts{6}{1}{11:07}\ts{6}{1}{11:29}. Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a topological manifold (one can give the dimension: part of what a manifold is; for generic topological spaces there is no notion
of dimension, for manifolds there is) \ts{6}{1}{11:29}\ts{6}{1}{11:49}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: submanifold]\label{def:mf-sub}
A subset $N\subseteq M$ with the induced topology (\cref{def:to-induced}) is called a \textbf{submanifold} of $(M,\mathcal O)$ if it is a manifold in its own right: one looks at the induced topology on the subset and checks that it is still a topological manifold
\ts{6}{1}{11:49}\ts{6}{1}{12:10}\ts{6}{1}{12:33}.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}[Submanifolds of $\R^2$]\label{ex:mf-sub}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $M=\R^2$ with the standard topology (always the standard one), $N=\{(x,y)\mid x^2+y^2=1\}$, the circle: with the induced topology it is again a topological manifold, of lower dimension \ts{6}{1}{12:33}\ts{6}{1}{12:54}\ts{6}{1}{13:16}\ts{6}{1}{13:37}.
  \item The half space also gives a topological manifold \ts{6}{1}{13:37}. (As heard; it must be the \emph{open} half space: with its boundary line included, the points on the boundary have no neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of $\R^2$ [?: not said in class].)
  \item \emph{Counterexample:} two circles touching in one point \ts{6}{1}{14:00}\ts{6}{1}{14:20}. Around every point it looks locally like a piece of $\R^1$, but at the touching point it looks like two copies of $\R^1$ with one common point; what is the dimension
        there? It is not locally $\R^D$, so this is not a submanifold of $\R^2$ \ts{6}{1}{14:20}\ts{6}{1}{14:41}.
  \item Neither is the \emph{light cone} of relativistic physics a submanifold of $\R^3$, since at the tip it fails to be a manifold \ts{6}{1}{14:41}\ts{6}{1}{15:02}. As on the last problem sheet, it is an algebraic variety, but not a submanifold \ts{6}{1}{15:02}\ts{6}{1}{15:23}.
\end{itemize}
This works in higher dimensions as well \ts{6}{1}{15:23}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Product manifolds}

A slightly more interesting case \ts{6}{1}{15:23}\ts{6}{1}{15:48}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: product manifold]\label{def:mf-product}
Let $(M,\mathcal O_M)$ and $(N,\mathcal O_N)$ be topological manifolds. Then $M\times N$ with the product topology (\cref{def:to-product}) is a topological manifold of dimension $\dim M+\dim N$, the \textbf{product manifold}
\ts{6}{1}{15:48}\ts{6}{1}{16:10}\ts{6}{1}{16:37}.
\end{definition}
(By $\dim M$ is meant the $D$ of \cref{def:mf-manifold}.) One does not need to check every time.
\begin{example}[Torus, $n$-torus, cylinder]\label{ex:mf-torus}
$T^2=S^1\times S^1$ was seen before only as a product of topological spaces; $S^1$ is a one-dimensional manifold, so $T^2$ is guaranteed to be a two-dimensional topological manifold, though it is pretty obvious \ts{6}{1}{16:37}\ts{6}{1}{16:58}\ts{6}{1}{17:18}.
The $n$-torus $T^n$, not so easy to imagine, is just $n$ copies of the circle in a Cartesian product, immediately an $n$-dimensional manifold \ts{6}{1}{17:18}\ts{6}{1}{17:39}\ts{6}{1}{17:59}.
The cylinder is $S^1\times\R$, as manifolds two-dimensional; one can go to higher cylinders; one always has to think at what level one is \ts{6}{1}{17:59}\ts{6}{1}{18:19}.
\end{example}
By taking products one builds interesting manifolds from old ones \ts{6}{1}{18:19}.

\subsection{The Möbius strip: a manifold that is not a product}

Products are nice, and in physics one often thinks of a manifold as a base space with the same copy of another space attached at every point, which is one way to think of a Cartesian product \ts{6}{1}{18:19}\ts{6}{1}{18:41}.
It turns out that taking the product is somewhat too restrictive, too special \ts{6}{1}{18:41}\ts{6}{1}{19:03}. The counterexample: the \textbf{Möbius strip} \ts{6}{1}{19:03}\ts{6}{1}{19:28}.

Take a rectangle as the space. Glue one side to the opposite one with the same orientation: one gets a cylinder (infinite if the rectangle is infinitely long); then glue the two other sides too, in the same direction: one gets the torus
\ts{6}{1}{19:28}\ts{6}{1}{19:48}\ts{6}{1}{20:09}. That is an example, not the counterexample. The counterexample to a product manifold arises by identifying two sides \emph{after twisting one end} \ts{6}{1}{20:09}\ts{6}{1}{20:29}\ts{6}{1}{21:11}.
(Schuller first glued the second pair as well, ``that's already the Klein bottle'', which is also an example, but he wanted the Möbius strip.) Take a strip, twist one side and glue it round; it intuitively has only one side, since from every point
one can get to ``the other side of the piece of paper'' \ts{6}{1}{20:29}\ts{6}{1}{20:50}\ts{6}{1}{21:11}\ts{6}{1}{21:31}. It cannot be written as a product manifold, although everywhere locally it looks like a product \ts{6}{1}{21:31}\ts{6}{1}{21:51}.
To formalise this idea one introduces a generalisation of the product: the bundle \ts{6}{1}{21:51}\ts{6}{1}{22:12}.
\begin{history}[The Möbius strip]
\emph{Not said in class.} \emph{Checked.} The one-sided strip was found independently, in 1858, by A.~F.~M\"obius and J.~B.~Listing; the Klein bottle is due to Felix Klein (1882).
\end{history}

\section{Bundles}\label{sec:mf-bundles}

(The section is numbered 3.2 on the board, after 3.1 ``definition and construction of topological manifolds'') \ts{6}{1}{22:12}\ts{6}{1}{22:37}. Again and again in the course there will be bundles, and every time structure is added to the manifold one has to revisit
the bundles and see what happens to the structure from their point of view; but the basic idea is already clear at the topological level \ts{6}{1}{23:02}\ts{6}{1}{23:22}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: bundle, total space, base space, projection, fibre]\label{def:mf-bundle}
A \textbf{bundle} (of topological manifolds) is a triple $(E,\pi,M)$ where
\begin{itemize}
  \item $E$ is a topological manifold, the \textbf{total space};
  \item $M$ is a topological manifold, the \textbf{base space};
  \item $\pi\colon E\to M$ is a surjective, continuous map, the \textbf{projection}; continuity is meaningful as there is a topology on both sides
\end{itemize}
\ts{6}{1}{23:22}\ts{6}{1}{23:44}\ts{6}{1}{24:05}\ts{6}{1}{24:26}\ts{6}{1}{24:47}. For $p\in M$ the pre-image $F_p:=\operatorname{preim}_\pi(\{p\})$ (``not $\pi^{-1}$'': that is not a good notation for the pre-image) is called the \textbf{fibre at $p$}
\ts{6}{1}{24:47}\ts{6}{1}{25:08}\ts{6}{1}{25:29}\ts{6}{1}{25:49}.
\end{definition}
``Nothing more and nothing less'' \ts{6}{1}{24:47}.

\begin{example}[The product bundle]\label{ex:mf-product-bundle}
Let $M$ and $F$ be topological manifolds, $E:=M\times F$ (a topological manifold as a product), and $\pi\colon M\times F\to M$, $(p,f)\mapsto p$ \ts{6}{1}{25:49}\ts{6}{1}{26:11}\ts{6}{1}{26:32}. With the product topology
(understood unless otherwise said) $\pi$ is continuous \ts{6}{1}{26:32}\ts{6}{1}{26:52}. So the notion of bundle contains the idea of the product \ts{6}{1}{27:14}\ts{6}{1}{27:35}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The Möbius strip as a bundle over the circle]\label{ex:mf-moebius}
Let $E$ be the Möbius strip and $M=S^1$ \ts{6}{1}{27:35}\ts{6}{1}{27:56}. Draw the strip as the rectangle before gluing, twisted. In the middle there is a circle: once the two ends are identified with the twist, the dotted middle line closes up to a circle, and one agrees to map every point of the strip
to the point of the middle line it lies over; that defines the projection $\pi$, which is continuous \ts{6}{1}{27:56}\ts{6}{1}{28:16}\ts{6}{1}{28:37}\ts{6}{1}{28:57}. The Möbius strip is not the product of $S^1$ with an interval; nevertheless the
pre-image of every point $p\in S^1$ is the interval $[-1,1]$, say: the same interval at every point \ts{6}{1}{28:57}\ts{6}{1}{29:17}\ts{6}{1}{29:39}. It is not a product: it would be, if one did not twist; as one twists the fibres are glued as they meet.
So the Möbius strip is a bundle, but not a product manifold \ts{6}{1}{29:39}\ts{6}{1}{30:00}.
\end{example}
\begin{intuition}[A weaker notion than ``something attached to the base'']\label{int:mf-weaker}
It is very intuitive to think of the total space as a base space with a fibre attached over every point, but that is the special case. In the definition there is no mention of the total space being built from the base space with something attached;
it is weaker: there is a total space and a projection map to the base space, with no previous assumption on how the total space is built. That is the generalisation, and it is ``wildly important''. In the product case one has the preconceived notion of a base and
an attached fibre; bundles generalise the idea of taking a product, in this subtle form \ts{6}{1}{30:00}\ts{6}{1}{30:20}\ts{6}{1}{30:42}\ts{6}{1}{31:02}.
\end{intuition}

\begin{example}[Fibres that are not homeomorphic]\label{ex:mf-varying}
Take the real line as the base space and attach at the point $P$ a circle, which looks like a cylinder; as topologically it does not matter how big the circle is, one can shrink it to a point at $Q$ and afterwards attach small intervals at $R$,
and so on \ts{6}{1}{31:24}\ts{6}{1}{31:44}\ts{6}{1}{32:05}. With the projection $\pi$ down to the real line, continuous, this is not a product: the fibre at $P$ is a circle, at $Q$ a single point, at $R$ an interval, and these are not homeomorphic. So in general one
can have a bundle whose fibre needs not be the same everywhere \ts{6}{1}{32:05}\ts{6}{1}{32:25}\ts{6}{1}{32:47}\ts{6}{1}{33:09}\ts{6}{1}{33:29}.
\end{example}
\begin{coursenote}[Is the total space a manifold?]
With the pointed tip at $Q$ and the thin tube at $R$ the total space of this picture is not locally homeomorphic to $\R^D$ at $Q$, so it would not satisfy \cref{def:mf-bundle} if $E$ must be a manifold; as a picture of bundles with varying fibre
it is fine. Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: fibre bundle, typical fibre]\label{def:mf-fibre-bundle}
A bundle $(E,\pi,M)$ (also written $E\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }M$, to emphasise that $\pi$ goes to $M$) such that for all $p\in M$ the fibre $\operatorname{preim}_\pi(\{p\})$ is homeomorphic to one and the same manifold $F$, is called a \textbf{fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$}
\ts{6}{1}{33:29}\ts{6}{1}{33:50}\ts{6}{1}{34:10}\ts{6}{1}{34:31}\ts{6}{1}{34:52}.
\end{definition}
A little less general than a bundle but much more general than a product \ts{6}{1}{34:52}. Examples: those given before; the \textbf{$\mathbb C$ line bundle} over a manifold $M$: ``line'' refers to $\mathbb C^1$; it is a fibre bundle $(E,\pi,M)$ with typical fibre $\mathbb C$
\ts{6}{1}{34:52}\ts{6}{1}{35:12}\ts{6}{1}{35:34}\ts{6}{1}{35:55}. For a fibre bundle one often draws a diagram, $F\to E\xrightarrow{\pi}M$, meaning that $F$ is the typical fibre; it is just notation, quicker than ``let this be the total space, this the base space, this the fibre''
\ts{6}{1}{35:55}\ts{6}{1}{36:16}\ts{6}{1}{36:37}. The $\mathbb C$ line bundle will play a very important role in quantum mechanics, through the following construction \ts{6}{1}{36:16}\ts{6}{1}{36:37}\ts{6}{1}{37:00}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: section]\label{def:mf-section}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle. A map $\sigma\colon M\to E$ (``in the other direction'') is called a \textbf{section} (cross-section) of the bundle if
\[
  \pi\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_M
\]
\ts{6}{1}{36:37}\ts{6}{1}{37:00}\ts{6}{1}{37:20}\ts{6}{1}{37:41}.
\end{definition}
(The projection has not been used up to now in the definition; it enters here.) Picture, for a fibre bundle: a base $M$, at each point the same manifold $F$ attached, the whole picture being the total space. A point of the total space is sent by $\pi$ to its base point, and every other point of that fibre
is sent to the same base point \ts{6}{1}{37:41}\ts{6}{1}{38:02}\ts{6}{1}{38:24}\ts{6}{1}{38:44}. A section takes a base point to some point of the total space; but if it takes it to a point of a \emph{different} fibre, then $\pi\circ\sigma\ne\mathrm{id}_M$ and it is not a section. Intuitively:
it is a section only if $\sigma$ always maps into the fibre over that point, so that $\pi$ takes one back to the base point, and does so for every point of the manifold \ts{6}{1}{38:44}\ts{6}{1}{39:04}\ts{6}{1}{39:25}\ts{6}{1}{39:46}.

\begin{proposition}[Sections of product bundles]\label{prop:mf-sections-product}
Let the bundle be a \textbf{product bundle}: $E=M\times F$ and $\pi$ the projection to the first factor (every product bundle is in particular a fibre bundle, as every fibre is the second factor). Then, and only then, a section is of the form
\[
  \sigma(p)=\bigl(p,s(p)\bigr)
\]
with $s\colon M\to F$ an arbitrary map, without further conditions \ts{6}{1}{39:46}\ts{6}{1}{40:06}\ts{6}{1}{40:27}\ts{6}{1}{40:47}\ts{6}{1}{41:09}\ts{6}{1}{41:29}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(Not written out in class.) $\pi(\sigma(p))=p$ forces the first component of $\sigma(p)$ to be $p$; the second is some $s(p)\in F$. Conversely every $s$ defines such a $\sigma$.
\end{proof}
So if one tells, for each point of the base space, the element of the fibre, which is a certain function one must give, what is the difference between a section and a function? From a function one can construct a section; yes, but \emph{only if the total space is a product}. Bundles are more
general than products, and in general a section is a different notion \ts{6}{1}{41:29}\ts{6}{1}{41:49}\ts{6}{1}{42:10}.

\begin{example}[The wave function is a section]\label{ex:mf-wave}
In quantum mechanics consider a $\mathbb C$ line bundle over $M$, physical space, for instance $\R^3$. A $\mathbb C$ line bundle need not be the product $M\times\mathbb C$: it is just a fibre bundle \ts{6}{1}{42:10}\ts{6}{1}{42:33}. What is usually called a \emph{wave function} is not a function at all:
it is a \emph{section} of the $\mathbb C$ line bundle over physical space, and one always pretends that it is a function from physical space to the complex numbers \ts{6}{1}{42:33}\ts{6}{1}{42:56}\ts{6}{1}{43:20}. If the bundle were always the product $M\times\mathbb C$, the name wave function would be
appropriate and making it a section would be cumbersome, unneeded formalism; but one cannot always do that, and one gets away with not doing it as long as one stays in Cartesian coordinates on physical space (coordinates are introduced later, \cref{sec:mf-atlas}); in fact a $\mathbb C$ line bundle is something more special than
attaching a complex line over every point \ts{6}{1}{43:20}\ts{6}{1}{43:42}\ts{6}{1}{44:02}.
\end{example}
\begin{intuition}[Product $\subset$ fibre bundle $\subset$ bundle]\label{int:mf-hierarchy}
Product manifolds are contained in the fibre bundles, which are contained in the bundles: this is the degree of specialisation. If the structure of quantum mechanics were that of a product, one could talk about wave functions; in fact there is only a fibre bundle, which is still much more general
than a product manifold. One should compare the definitions at home: it is just a lot of terminology at once. The physics example is only a hint at what is to come: these constructions are not mathematical extravagance, but intimately connected with questions and structures in physics
\ts{6}{1}{44:24}\ts{6}{1}{44:45}\ts{6}{1}{45:05}\ts{6}{1}{45:26}\ts{6}{1}{45:46}.
\end{intuition}

\section{Sub-bundles, restrictions, morphisms and isomorphisms}\label{sec:mf-morph}

Once there is a structure such as bundles, one needs to understand and classify it \ts{6}{1}{45:46}\ts{6}{1}{46:09}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: sub-bundle]\label{def:mf-subbundle}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle. A bundle $(E',\pi',M')$ is a \textbf{sub-bundle} if $E'\subseteq E$ and $M'\subseteq M$ (submanifolds: otherwise one could not speak of a bundle) and the restriction $\pi|_{E'}$ coincides with $\pi'$
\ts{6}{1}{46:09}\ts{6}{1}{46:30}\ts{6}{1}{46:51}\ts{6}{1}{47:12}\ts{6}{1}{47:33}\ts{6}{1}{47:56}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[New concepts: restricted bundle]\label{def:mf-restricted}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle and $N\subseteq M$ a submanifold. The \textbf{restricted bundle} (restriction to $N$) is
\[
  \bigl(\operatorname{preim}_\pi(N),\ \pi|_{\operatorname{preim}_\pi(N)},\ N\bigr)
\]
\ts{6}{1}{47:12}\ts{6}{1}{47:56}\ts{6}{1}{48:18}\ts{6}{1}{48:39}\ts{6}{1}{49:00}. One starts with a bundle and only looks at it over a subset of the original base space \ts{6}{1}{49:00}\ts{6}{1}{49:21}. A very simple definition, but very useful.
\end{definition}

It is also very important to see in which cases two bundles are to be considered the same \ts{6}{1}{49:21}\ts{6}{1}{49:41}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: bundle morphism]\label{def:mf-morphism}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ and $(E',\pi',M')$ be bundles (no prior relation between them), $u\colon E\to E'$ and $f\colon M\to M'$ maps. The pair $(u,f)$ is called a \textbf{bundle morphism} if the diagram
\[
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,node distance=2.4cm]
  \node (E) {$E$}; \node[right=of E] (E2) {$E'$};
  \node[below=of E] (M) {$M$}; \node[right=of M] (M2) {$M'$};
  \draw[->] (E) -- node[above]{$u$} (E2);
  \draw[->] (M) -- node[below]{$f$} (M2);
  \draw[->] (E) -- node[left]{$\pi$} (M);
  \draw[->] (E2) -- node[right]{$\pi'$} (M2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\]
commutes, i.e.\ $\pi'\circ u=f\circ\pi$ \ts{6}{1}{49:41}\ts{6}{1}{50:02}\ts{6}{1}{50:23}\ts{6}{1}{50:44}\ts{6}{1}{51:04}\ts{6}{1}{51:29}\ts{6}{1}{51:50}.
\end{definition}
(Commuting means that one can go either way, from $E$ to $M'$, and get the same result \ts{6}{1}{51:29}\ts{6}{1}{51:50}.) Is this already structure preserving? The structure of a bundle is the base space and the projection map: one preserves the fibres \ts{6}{1}{51:50}\ts{6}{1}{52:10}\ts{6}{1}{52:31}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: bundle isomorphism]\label{def:mf-iso}
Two bundles $(E,\pi,M)$ and $(E',\pi',M')$ are \textbf{isomorphic as bundles} if there exist bundle morphisms $(u,f)$ and $(u^{-1},f^{-1})$, i.e.\ $u$ and $f$ are bijective and in both directions morphisms; such a $(u,f)$ is called a
\textbf{bundle isomorphism} \ts{6}{1}{52:31}\ts{6}{1}{52:52}\ts{6}{1}{53:13}\ts{6}{1}{53:37}\ts{6}{1}{53:57}\ts{6}{1}{54:18}\ts{6}{1}{54:39}.
\end{definition}
It is easy to see that the fibre structure of the two bundles is then the same; bundle isomorphisms are clearly the relevant structure preserving maps for bundles \ts{6}{1}{54:18}\ts{6}{1}{54:39}\ts{6}{1}{55:00}. Remember the recurrent theme (\cref{int:cl-theme}): one studies a structure by studying
its structure preserving maps \ts{6}{1}{55:00}\ts{6}{1}{55:21}.

\begin{intuition}[Isomorphic below, not isomorphic as bundles]\label{int:mf-iso-lower}
One can have two total spaces $E$ and $E'$ that are homeomorphic as topological manifolds, with homeomorphic base spaces $M$, $M'$, but not isomorphic as bundles, because the projection is defined differently. Everything can be isomorphic at a lower structural level, but as soon as the projection map comes into play
\ts{6}{1}{55:21}\ts{6}{1}{55:42}\ts{6}{1}{56:02}. Schuller's example: the cylinder against the Möbius strip, both with fibre $[-1,1]$ and base $S^1$; the twisting is encoded in the projection map, which must be continuous: one cannot take the projection of the cylinder, twist and join, since at the point
where one joins, a point up here would have to go down there, and the next point would not follow continuously \ts{6}{1}{56:02}\ts{6}{1}{56:24}\ts{6}{1}{56:45}\ts{6}{1}{57:05}.
\end{intuition}
\begin{coursenote}[A correction to the example]
As total spaces the cylinder $S^1\times[-1,1]$ and the Möbius strip are \emph{not} homeomorphic, contrary to what is said at \ts{6}{1}{57:05}\ts{6}{1}{57:26}: the cylinder has two boundary circles, the Möbius strip only one. (With open intervals as fibres they are
still not homeomorphic: one is orientable, the other not.) The point of the passage, that isomorphism as bundles is finer than homeomorphism of the total spaces, stands, but needs another example: two different projections on the same total space. Not said in class.
\end{coursenote}
Hence the need for a notion of isomorphism of bundles \ts{6}{1}{57:05}\ts{6}{1}{57:26}.

\section{Local isomorphism, trivial and locally trivial bundles}\label{sec:mf-local}

A topological manifold is a topological space \emph{locally} homeomorphic to a part of $\R^D$, not homeomorphic: that would be too strong, as all spaces homeomorphic to $\R^D$ are, through topological glasses, $\R^D$. Similarly, isomorphism of bundles is strong and can be weakened to
\emph{local} isomorphism \ts{6}{1}{57:26}\ts{6}{1}{57:47}\ts{6}{1}{58:08}. ``There is a lot of definitions, because we are developing the language; trust me that one needs to know all of them, think about them and memorise them'' \ts{6}{1}{58:08}\ts{6}{1}{58:28}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: locally isomorphic bundles]\label{def:mf-local-iso}
A bundle $(E,\pi,M)$ is \textbf{locally isomorphic as a bundle} to a bundle $(E',\pi',M')$ if for every $p\in M$ there is an open set $U\in\mathcal O_M$ with $p\in U$ such that the restricted bundle
$\bigl(\operatorname{preim}_\pi(U),\pi|_{\operatorname{preim}_\pi(U)},U\bigr)$ (\cref{def:mf-restricted}) is isomorphic to $(E',\pi',M')$ \ts{6}{1}{58:28}\ts{6}{1}{58:49}\ts{6}{1}{59:11}\ts{6}{1}{59:34}\ts{6}{1}{59:56}\ts{6}{1}{1:00:18}.
\end{definition}
These notions are developed to elucidate and quantify the relation between bundles, product bundles, fibre bundles, and so on \ts{6}{1}{1:00:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:00:41}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:02}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: trivial and locally trivial bundle]\label{def:mf-trivial}
Heavily used terminology (not strictly needed, as all definitions are already on the board) \ts{6}{1}{1:01:02}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:23}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A bundle is \textbf{trivial} if it is isomorphic to a product bundle $(M\times F,\mathrm{pr}_1,M)$, with $\mathrm{pr}_1$ the projection to the first factor \ts{6}{1}{1:01:23}\ts{6}{1}{1:01:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:07}.
  \item A bundle is \textbf{locally trivial} if it is locally isomorphic to some product bundle \ts{6}{1}{1:02:27}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:48}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:11}.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
(A product of manifolds immediately gives a bundle, by projecting back to the first factor: the trivial case one started from, which needed generalisation \ts{6}{1}{1:02:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:02:27}.)
Trivial implies locally trivial, but not the other way around \ts{6}{1}{1:03:11}.
\begin{example}[Cylinder and Möbius strip]\label{ex:mf-cyl-moeb}
The cylinder is trivial as a bundle, hence also locally trivial. The Möbius strip is \emph{not} trivial as a bundle, but it is locally trivial \ts{6}{1}{1:03:11}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:32}\ts{6}{1}{1:03:52}.
\end{example}
A locally trivial bundle is not yet a fibre bundle, since the fibre could change along the base (a typical fibre of the locally trivial pieces could differ from one connected piece of the base to another); \ts{6}{1}{1:03:52}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:14} [?: as said, ``it could change the fibre along'']. It everywhere needs only to be locally trivial.

A student asks for an example of a bundle that is \emph{not} locally trivial: it always feels like one could deform it. Such examples are hard to come by; in the course one will always restrict to local triviality. Schuller offers a bundle: over a base manifold attach a complex line at every point up to a certain
point, and from there on a real line. At a point away from the change, the restricted bundle is locally $M\times\mathbb C$ or $M\times\R$; but around the point of the change no open neighbourhood works, since in one half it is $\times\,\mathbb C$ and in the other $\times\,\R$. The projection can be made
continuous [?: ``I think one can''] \ts{6}{1}{1:04:14}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:35}\ts{6}{1}{1:04:57}\ts{6}{1}{1:05:17}\ts{6}{1}{1:05:40}\ts{6}{1}{1:06:01}\ts{6}{1}{1:06:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:06:43}.

From now on only locally trivial bundles are considered \ts{6}{1}{1:06:43}\ts{6}{1}{1:07:03}. Immediate implication: if one restricts to a part where the bundle is a product, every section can be represented there as a function, a map from base space to fibre; but only locally, because globally it is not a
product \ts{6}{1}{1:07:03}\ts{6}{1}{1:07:24}\ts{6}{1}{1:07:47}. So in quantum mechanics with a $\mathbb C$ line bundle over physical space that is locally trivial, on some patches it is perfectly fine to speak of the wave function; on other parts one needs another function representing the same global section (if a global section exists at all: to come)
\ts{6}{1}{1:07:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:12}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:33}.
\begin{exercise}[Local trivialisations]\label{ex:mf-loctriv}
Several problems on the problem sheet make this clear: for the Möbius strip find open sets of $S^1$ over which it is a product, show that it is not trivial, and write a section locally as a map to the fibre \ts{6}{1}{1:08:12}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:33}. The long version is in \cref{exam:ch06}.
\end{exercise}

\section{The pull-back bundle}\label{sec:mf-pullback}

Before the break, one more very important definition \ts{6}{1}{1:08:33}\ts{6}{1}{1:08:54}.
\begin{definition}[New concepts: pull-back bundle]\label{def:mf-pullback}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle and $f\colon M'\to M$ a map from some manifold $M'$ \ts{6}{1}{1:08:54}\ts{6}{1}{1:09:29}\ts{6}{1}{1:09:50}. The \textbf{pull-back bundle} $(E',\pi',M')$ has base space $M'$, total space
\[
  E':=\bigl\{(m',e)\in M'\times E\ \bigm|\ f(m')=\pi(e)\bigr\}\subseteq M'\times E
\]
and projection $\pi'(m',e):=m'$ \ts{6}{1}{1:09:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:10:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:10:38}\ts{6}{1}{1:10:59}\ts{6}{1}{1:11:20}\ts{6}{1}{1:11:41}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:25}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:08}.
\end{definition}
At first sight it looks like a product bundle, since one writes $M'\times E$; but one crosses with the \emph{entire} total space of the original bundle, a huge thing, and then imposes a restriction: only the pairs $(m',e)$ for which projecting $e$ down by $\pi$ gives
the same as mapping $m'$ by $f$ are kept \ts{6}{1}{1:10:59}\ts{6}{1}{1:11:20}\ts{6}{1}{1:11:41}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:25}\ts{6}{1}{1:12:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:08}. (Schuller first wrote the whole product, then hesitated and corrected himself: ``I'm sorry,
of course it is not the entire space'' \ts{6}{1}{1:12:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:08}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:29}.) From the original data one also constructs a morphism $u\colon E'\to E$, $u(m',e):=e$, which together with $f$ is a bundle morphism (not necessarily an isomorphism)
\ts{6}{1}{1:13:29}\ts{6}{1}{1:13:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:14:12}\ts{6}{1}{1:16:01}.
\[
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,node distance=2.4cm]
  \node (E1) {$E'$}; \node[right=of E1] (E) {$E$};
  \node[below=of E1] (M1) {$M'$}; \node[right=of M1] (M) {$M$};
  \draw[->] (E1) -- node[above]{$u$} (E);
  \draw[->] (M1) -- node[below]{$f$} (M);
  \draw[->] (E1) -- node[left]{$\pi'$} (M1);
  \draw[->] (E) -- node[right]{$\pi$} (M);
\end{tikzpicture}
\]

\begin{proposition}[Sections pull back]\label{prop:mf-pullback-section}
Let $(E,\pi,M)$ be a bundle with a section $\sigma$ (so $\pi\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_M$), and $f\colon M'\to M$ a map. Then there is a section $\sigma'$ of the pull-back bundle $(E',\pi',M')$, i.e.\ a map $\sigma'\colon M'\to E'$ with $\pi'\circ\sigma'=\mathrm{id}_{M'}$
\ts{6}{1}{1:14:33}\ts{6}{1}{1:14:54}\ts{6}{1}{1:15:16}\ts{6}{1}{1:15:39}\ts{6}{1}{1:16:01}\ts{6}{1}{1:16:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:17:04}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
It is the homework to find $\sigma'$; from the diagram there is only one possibility given the data $f$, $\sigma$, $\pi$ \ts{6}{1}{1:16:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:16:43}\ts{6}{1}{1:17:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:17:24}. The one possibility is
\[
  \sigma'(m'):=\bigl(m',\ \sigma(f(m'))\bigr),
\]
which lies in $E'$ since $\pi(\sigma(f(m')))=f(m')$, and satisfies $\pi'(\sigma'(m'))=m'$. (Formula not written in class; continuity of $\sigma'$ not discussed.)
\end{proof}
So a map into the base space of a bundle with a section, from the only additional datum $M'\to M$, gives the whole picture; this is used very heavily \ts{6}{1}{1:17:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:17:24}\ts{6}{1}{1:17:44}.
\begin{exercise}[Pulled-back sections]\label{ex:mf-pullback-sec}
Construct $\sigma'$ as above and check that it is a section; (homework in class) \ts{6}{1}{1:16:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:16:43}. A long version, with local trivialisations, is in \cref{exam:ch06}.
\end{exercise}

\section{Manifolds from atlases}\label{sec:mf-atlas}

The next part (3.4, ``I believe'') is in a completely different spirit from the bundles, which are now covered and will be revisited when there is more structure. It is something fully \emph{redundant}: not needed for topological manifolds, but pedagogically invaluable later on
\ts{6}{1}{1:17:44}\ts{6}{1}{1:18:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:18:27}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: chart, component functions, coordinates]\label{def:mf-chart}
Let $(M,\mathcal O)$ be a topological manifold of dimension $D$. A pair $(U,x)$ with $U\in\mathcal O$ and $x\colon U\to x(U)\subseteq\R^D$ a homeomorphism (as in \cref{def:mf-manifold}) is called a \textbf{chart} of the manifold
\ts{6}{1}{1:18:27}\ts{6}{1}{1:18:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:19:10}\ts{6}{1}{1:19:31}\ts{6}{1}{1:19:52}\ts{6}{1}{1:20:14}. The \textbf{component functions} of $x$ are the maps $x^i\colon U\to\R$,
$x^i(p):=\mathrm{pr}_i\bigl(x(p)\bigr)$, the $i$-th component of the image (the projection of $\R^D$, a Cartesian product of $D$ copies of $\R$, to the $i$-th factor). They are called the \textbf{coordinates of the point $p\in U$ with respect to the chart $(U,x)$}
\ts{6}{1}{1:20:14}\ts{6}{1}{1:20:34}\ts{6}{1}{1:20:55}\ts{6}{1}{1:21:16}\ts{6}{1}{1:21:36}\ts{6}{1}{1:21:57}.
\end{definition}
What is new in this definition? Nothing: it is almost only terminology, but extremely useful \ts{6}{1}{1:19:52}\ts{6}{1}{1:20:14}. And very important: one cannot speak of \emph{the coordinates of a point}, only of the coordinates of a point \emph{with respect to a certain chart} \ts{6}{1}{1:21:57}\ts{6}{1}{1:22:18}.
All of one's physics education so far has taken place in charts: a particle in $\R^3$ and its coordinates; the Lagrangian $x^1,\dots,x^D$ of a $D$-dimensional configuration space \ts{6}{1}{1:22:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:22:39}\ts{6}{1}{1:23:00}. The question is what happens
if one looks at the same point with respect to different charts \ts{6}{1}{1:23:00}\ts{6}{1}{1:23:21}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: atlas]\label{def:mf-atlas}
By the definition of a manifold there is a set $\mathcal A$ of charts such that $\bigcup\{U\mid(U,x)\in\mathcal A\}=M$, every point being covered by some chart; such a collection is called an \textbf{atlas}; many charts will overlap
\ts{6}{1}{1:23:21}\ts{6}{1}{1:23:42}\ts{6}{1}{1:24:03}\ts{6}{1}{1:24:24}\ts{6}{1}{1:24:44}.
\end{definition}
(Another fully redundant definition, which in later chapters will be changed slightly.) If the whole world is covered with charts, one can print the charts and stack them into an atlas. On page 300 there is Paris, with coordinates 2 cm to the right, 2 cm up from the lower left corner; turn the page and Paris appears again, now with
different coordinates. The coordinates depend on the chart; Paris is Paris \ts{6}{1}{1:24:44}\ts{6}{1}{1:25:06}\ts{6}{1}{1:25:26}\ts{6}{1}{1:25:46}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $C^0$ compatible charts, transition map]\label{def:mf-compat}
Two charts $(U,x)$ and $(V,y)$ are called \textbf{$C^0$ compatible} if either
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $U\cap V=\emptyset$ (``you are certainly compatible with someone if you never meet that person''), or
  \item $U\cap V\ne\emptyset$ and the \textbf{transition map} (coordinate change map)
        \[
          y\circ x^{-1}\colon x(U\cap V)\to y(U\cap V),
        \]
        a map from a subset of $\R^D$ to a subset of $\R^D$, is continuous
\end{enumerate}
\ts{6}{1}{1:25:46}\ts{6}{1}{1:26:09}\ts{6}{1}{1:26:29}\ts{6}{1}{1:26:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:16}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:37}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:58}\ts{6}{1}{1:28:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:28:40}\ts{6}{1}{1:29:01}.
\end{definition}
The ``$C^0$'' will later be changed into $C^1,C^2,C^k,C^\infty,C^\omega$ and other kinds of $C$; here it is just a name \ts{6}{1}{1:26:09}\ts{6}{1}{1:26:29}. In the picture: the chart $U$ with $x$ to $\R^D$ and the chart $V$ with $y$ to $\R^D$; on the overlap one can apply $x$ landing in
$x(U\cap V)$, or $y$ landing in $y(U\cap V)$, and so $y\circ x^{-1}$ goes from the former to the latter. ``All of a sudden one has constructed a map from $\R^D$ to $\R^D$ from two chart maps'' \ts{6}{1}{1:26:50}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:16}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:37}\ts{6}{1}{1:27:58}\ts{6}{1}{1:28:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:28:40}.
What matters is that one can decide on continuity without knowing anything about the topology of the original manifold in which the intersections are taken: it suffices to know the topology of the charts \ts{6}{1}{1:29:01}\ts{6}{1}{1:29:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:29:43}.

\begin{proposition}[Any two charts of a topological manifold are $C^0$ compatible]\label{prop:mf-c0}
Let $(U,x)$ and $(V,y)$ be charts of a topological manifold. Then $y\circ x^{-1}$ is continuous \ts{6}{1}{1:29:43}\ts{6}{1}{1:30:04}\ts{6}{1}{1:30:26}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$x$ and $y$ are homeomorphisms, so $x^{-1}$ is continuous too, and the composition of two continuous maps is continuous (the pre-image of an open set under $y\circ x^{-1}$ is the pre-image under $x^{-1}$ of the pre-image under $y$; restricted to the overlap
with the induced topologies) \ts{6}{1}{1:29:43}\ts{6}{1}{1:30:04}.
\end{proof}
At first sight it is fully redundant to have the definition, since it applies to any two charts. But later one wants differentiability: the manifold still being a topological space, there is no way of saying that the maps into coordinates should be differentiable, as that needs a differentiable structure. Instead
one will require that two charts be $C^1$ compatible, $C^2$ compatible, and so on: that the transition maps are once, twice, $k$ times or infinitely often continuously differentiable as maps $\R^D\to\R^D$, which can be done as differentiation on $\R^D$ is understood
\ts{6}{1}{1:30:26}\ts{6}{1}{1:30:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:31:07}. So it is only for topological manifolds that the notion is redundant; later it is refined \ts{6}{1}{1:31:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:31:28}.

All of physics so far has taken place in charts; whenever one talked about changing coordinates one talked about this map: one looked at the problem in one chart, say Cartesian coordinates, and then went to other coordinates. The coordinate changes of introductory physics are between charts;
one never considers the real world that lies above, which is the manifold. The choice of chart is largely arbitrary: whether one expresses a particle path in one coordinate system or another does not change the path; it is one curve in the manifold, and the chart change is the transition map $y\circ x^{-1}$ (or its inverse $x\circ y^{-1}$)
\ts{6}{1}{1:31:28}\ts{6}{1}{1:31:48}\ts{6}{1}{1:32:08}\ts{6}{1}{1:32:33}\ts{6}{1}{1:32:59}. Only in general relativity (so far) does one meet coordinate-free notions; the abstract notions lie above the chart level \ts{6}{1}{1:32:59}.

\begin{definition}[New concepts: $C^0$ atlas, maximal atlas]\label{def:mf-c0atlas}
A \textbf{$C^0$ atlas} is an atlas whose charts are pairwise $C^0$ compatible. An atlas $\mathcal A$ is called \textbf{maximal} if every chart $(U,x)$ that is $C^0$ compatible with every chart $(V,y)\in\mathcal A$ is already contained in $\mathcal A$
\ts{6}{1}{1:33:36}\ts{6}{1}{1:34:00}\ts{6}{1}{1:34:44}\ts{6}{1}{1:35:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:35:28}\ts{6}{1}{1:35:48}.
\end{definition}
By \cref{prop:mf-c0}, any atlas of a topological manifold is a $C^0$ atlas; that will change when stronger conditions on compatibility are imposed: not every atlas will satisfy them, and that is one way to give extra structure to manifolds, in particular a differentiable
structure \ts{6}{1}{1:34:00}\ts{6}{1}{1:34:23}\ts{6}{1}{1:34:44}. Not every atlas is maximal:

\begin{example}[Atlases of the real line]\label{ex:mf-atlases}
Let $M=\R$ with the standard topology, clearly locally homeomorphic to $\R^1$: a one-dimensional manifold \ts{6}{1}{1:35:48}\ts{6}{1}{1:36:10}. Then $\mathcal A=\{(\R,\mathrm{id}_\R)\}$ is an atlas: one chart covers all of $\R$, which is what one usually does when
one takes all of physical space to be $\R$ and uses numbers $1,2,3,4,5,\pi,7\tfrac12$ as coordinates \ts{6}{1}{1:36:10}\ts{6}{1}{1:36:32}\ts{6}{1}{1:36:52}. A different atlas: $\mathcal A'$ keeps this chart and has a second one, $\bigl((-\infty,0),\mathrm{id}\bigr)$,
``a two-page atlas'': it covers only half of $\R$, and the two charts are compatible, since any two are \ts{6}{1}{1:36:52}\ts{6}{1}{1:37:12}\ts{6}{1}{1:37:36}\ts{6}{1}{1:37:57}. One can add more and more charts, each compatible with the old ones; once all
compatible charts have been added one has a maximal atlas, since every chart compatible with all that is in it is already in it \ts{6}{1}{1:37:57}\ts{6}{1}{1:38:18}\ts{6}{1}{1:38:38}.
\end{example}
(For maximal atlases the notion becomes more interesting later, when the $C^0$ restriction is intensified \ts{6}{1}{1:38:38}\ts{6}{1}{1:38:59}.)

\section{Two ways of looking at a curve}\label{sec:mf-curve}

What is all this about? Topological manifolds, and objects on them, can be looked at from two points of view \ts{6}{1}{1:38:59}\ts{6}{1}{1:39:26}\ts{6}{1}{1:39:47}. Consider a \textbf{curve} $\gamma\colon\R\to M$, a map from all of $\R$ into the topological manifold. Is it continuous, as it should be
if it is the trajectory of a classical particle in physical space $M$ (it should not jump all of a sudden)? \ts{6}{1}{1:39:47}\ts{6}{1}{1:40:07}\ts{6}{1}{1:40:29}\ts{6}{1}{1:40:49}

\paragraph{Level one.} A no-brainer: $\R$ has the standard topology, $M$ the topology it was given as a topological manifold, and $\gamma$ is continuous if for every open set of the target its pre-image under $\gamma$ is open in the domain: that is the definition of a continuous map,
$\gamma$ being just an instance of a map. One can decide this fully on the topological level, and there is nothing more to say \ts{6}{1}{1:40:49}\ts{6}{1}{1:41:10}\ts{6}{1}{1:41:30}\ts{6}{1}{1:41:51}.

\paragraph{Level two.} This is not how one looked at it so far in physics courses \ts{6}{1}{1:41:51}\ts{6}{1}{1:42:12}. One maps $\R$ into physical space, but then looks only at one part of the world: the image of $\gamma$ inside a chart domain $U$, so the domain is
$\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(U)$, the parameter range where the curve lies in $U$; with $(U,x)$ a chart ($\dim M=D$) one lands in $x(U)\subseteq\R^D$ \ts{6}{1}{1:42:12}\ts{6}{1}{1:42:33}\ts{6}{1}{1:42:54}. One was instructed not to look at $\gamma$
but at the curve $x\circ\gamma$, from a part of $\R$ into $\R^D$, and even at its $i$-th component, the coordinates where the curve is at a given parameter; and to check that it is continuous in each slot (a curve into $\R^D$ is continuous if each component function is)
\ts{6}{1}{1:42:54}\ts{6}{1}{1:43:14}\ts{6}{1}{1:43:36}.
Can one conclude from this that $\gamma$ itself is continuous? On a topological manifold it is simple: $x\circ\gamma$ is a composition of continuous maps, and
\[
  x\circ\gamma\ \text{is continuous}\iff\gamma\ \text{is continuous (on }\operatorname{preim}_\gamma(U)\text{)}
\]
\ts{6}{1}{1:43:36}\ts{6}{1}{1:43:56}\ts{6}{1}{1:44:18}. So if the curve is continuous in its components, then the real curve in the real world, not its chart representation, is continuous \ts{6}{1}{1:44:18}.
\begin{coursenote}[The two directions]
``$\Rightarrow$'' (continuity of $x\circ\gamma$ from that of $\gamma$) is the composition of continuous maps; ``$\Leftarrow$'' uses that $x^{-1}$ is continuous, $\gamma=x^{-1}\circ(x\circ\gamma)$ where the curve lies in $U$. To conclude for the whole curve one needs
every parameter to have an open neighbourhood on which $\gamma$ stays inside the domain of some chart and the coordinate curve is continuous; a single chart need not contain the whole image. Not said in class beyond ``if and only if''
(see the exercise in \cref{exam:ch06}).
\end{coursenote}

\begin{intuition}[The bird in the room]\label{int:mf-bird}
Imagine a bird flying through this room; $M$ is the physical space, or our model of it. The bird does what it does. One can put coordinates into the room, $x,y,z$, and look at the path of the bird in these arbitrarily chosen coordinates, as in elementary physics; or choose a different coordinate system, another
chart $y$ into some part of $\R^D$, and consider the bird flight as $y\circ\gamma$: the same bird, the same trajectory, a different coordinate representation. That is how one puts numbers to the bird's path; one needs to choose coordinate frames. But the real bird flight is encoded in the curve $\gamma$

Again a triviality: $x\circ\gamma$ is continuous iff $\gamma$ is. How does one have access to the real bird's curve? Only through the descriptions in different charts, and the chart transition map $y\circ x^{-1}$, which is continuous if the charts are $C^0$ compatible. If one knows the description is continuous
in one chart, one composes with the chart change and gets that it is continuous in the other chart too. So by looking at transition maps one can forget the inner workings of the real world ``in blue'': work in some chart, change coordinates to another chart, and describe the situation in full. But it would be a shame:
what one wants to describe is the real world, and one now sees why different coordinate systems still describe the same world. That is the philosophy behind it all; here everything is trivial, one could go either way and know everything about all the maps
\ts{6}{1}{1:46:00}\ts{6}{1}{1:46:21}\ts{6}{1}{1:46:41}\ts{6}{1}{1:47:01}\ts{6}{1}{1:47:22}\ts{6}{1}{1:47:43}.

But as soon as one wants to speak not of the continuity of a curve but, say, of its \emph{differentiability}, there is no way to do it via the topological manifold alone, one has to impose that the transition functions preserve the properties one wants: if one imposes that the curve in a chart be differentiable, it better be differentiable in
another coordinate frame, so the transition function must be differentiable as a map $\R^D\to\R^D$, and the atlases must be restricted accordingly. In the topological context it is all redundant, but it will become important later \ts{6}{1}{1:47:43}\ts{6}{1}{1:48:03}\ts{6}{1}{1:48:23}.

\section*{Key concepts}
\begin{keyconcepts}
\textbf{Retrieval questions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the definition of a $D$-dimensional topological manifold. Why must the same $D$ occur at every point?
  \item Why are two touching circles and the light cone not submanifolds? What is the dimension of $M\times N$ and of $T^n$?
  \item Define a bundle, the fibre at $p$, a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$, a section. Why is the Möbius strip a bundle but not a product?
  \item When is a section the same thing as a function? Why is the wave function a section?
  \item Define bundle morphism and isomorphism; trivial and locally trivial bundles; which implies which?
  \item Construct the pull-back bundle of $(E,\pi,M)$ along $f\colon M'\to M$ and the pulled-back section.
  \item Define chart, coordinates, atlas, $C^0$ compatibility, transition map, maximal atlas. Why are any two charts of a topological manifold $C^0$ compatible?
  \item Why can the continuity of a curve be checked in a chart?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Concepts.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Topological manifold, submanifold, product manifold (\cref{def:mf-manifold,def:mf-sub,def:mf-product}).

  \item Sub-bundle, restriction, morphism, isomorphism (\cref{def:mf-subbundle,def:mf-restricted,def:mf-morphism,def:mf-iso}).
  \item Local isomorphism, trivial, locally trivial (\cref{def:mf-local-iso,def:mf-trivial}).
  \item Pull-back bundle and pulled-back section (\cref{def:mf-pullback,prop:mf-pullback-section}).
  \item Chart, atlas, transition map, $C^0$ atlas, maximal atlas (\cref{def:mf-chart,def:mf-atlas,def:mf-compat,prop:mf-c0,def:mf-c0atlas}).
\end{itemize}
